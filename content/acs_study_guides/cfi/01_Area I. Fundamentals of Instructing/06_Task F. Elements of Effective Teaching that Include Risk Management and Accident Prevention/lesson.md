# Task F. Elements of Effective Teaching that Include Risk Management and Accident Prevention

**Area I — Fundamentals of Instructing**

## Objective

To determine the applicant understands teaching practical risk management, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate appropriate skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Many aviation accidents involve poor risk-management decisions. Risk management must therefore be taught from the beginning of training, practiced during actual flights, and reinforced throughout a pilot's training and currency activities.

The flight instructor manages risk on three levels: the risk of the maneuver or phase of flight, the learner's risk-management learning, and the risks created by providing instruction in flight.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested Schedule**

- Ground lesson: approximately 60 minutes
- Scenario and risk-assessment practice: approximately 20 minutes
- Flight application: integrated into every instructional flight

**Equipment**

- Aviation Instructor's Handbook
- Risk Management Handbook
- CFI Airplane ACS
- Risk matrix or locally approved FRAT
- Flight Guide visual aids

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner Actions**

- Identify hazards using PAVE.
- Assess likelihood and severity and select a mitigation.
- Use a FRAT or risk matrix to organize risk information.
- Explain risk decisions during preflight, flight, and postflight discussion.
- Apply risk management and ADM to an evaluator-assigned scenario.

**Instructor Actions**

- Introduce risk management before flight and integrate it into all briefings.
- Model systematic identification, assessment, mitigation, and review.
- Scale learner risk-management responsibility with training phase and experience.
- Monitor the learner, aircraft, environment, and changing conditions continuously.
- Avoid unnecessary distractions and intervene when safety requires it.

## Completion Standards

The applicant can teach and assess risk identification, assessment, mitigation, ADM, and SRM in a realistic scenario. The applicant can maintain instructional safety through awareness of learner actions and condition, aircraft state, traffic, environment, and changing circumstances.

# Teach

## FI.I.F.K1 — Teaching Risk Identification, Assessment, and Mitigation

Risk management identifies operational hazards, assesses the degree of risk, and determines measures to reduce risk to personnel, equipment, and the mission. A hazard is a real or potential condition that can cause injury, illness, death, damage, loss, or degradation.

Assess each identified risk by likelihood and severity. Mitigation reduces likelihood or severity, eliminates a risk, transfers it, or requires a conscious decision to accept remaining risk. Risk management is sequential, balanced, cyclic, and should involve the people exposed to the risk.

## FI.I.F.K2 — Teaching Risk Management Tools

### FI.I.F.K2a — PAVE Checklist

PAVE organizes flight risk identification into Pilot, Aircraft, enVironment, and External pressures. It helps the learner identify hazards methodically before flight and decide whether individual hazards or their combination can be managed safely. The Pilot category includes both the learner and instructor, including qualification, currency, proficiency, and aeromedical factors.

### FI.I.F.K2b — Flight Risk Assessment Tools (FRATs)

A FRAT records hazards and associated risks during planning. A numerical FRAT assigns values and may trigger scrutiny or mitigation by total score; it must be used cautiously because a low score can still hide an unlisted or extreme hazard. A narrative FRAT identifies hazards, associates them with risk, determines likelihood and severity, and analyzes each risk for management.

## FI.I.F.K3 — When and How to Introduce Risk Management

Introduce risk management before the first flight and integrate it into every instructional flight rather than teaching it as a separate subject. Include discussion and activity during preflight and postflight briefings, invite learner participation, and allow the learner to lead as experience increases. Continue risk-management training during recurrent, transition, flight-review, instrument-proficiency, and currency training.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=186 | end=187]]

## FI.I.F.K4 — Risk Management Teaching Techniques by Phase of Instruction

Use a building-block approach. Before solo, lead and guide risk-management discussions and demonstrate a non-numerical FRAT; by first solo, the learner should conduct a basic analysis. After solo, review the learner's analysis and use postflight debriefing to discuss the risk-management aspects of the flight. During cross-country training, require a full analysis for dual and solo cross-country flights and review it as part of preflight preparation.

For transition, instrument, recurrent, and proficiency training, construct scenarios that reflect the pilot's operating profile and potential new risks. Scale the process to flight complexity: an abbreviated PAVE review may suit a local flight, while a complex flight may justify a FRAT.

## FI.I.F.K5 — Managing Risk During Flight Instruction

### FI.I.F.K5a — Common Flight Instruction Risks

Use PAVE to identify instructor and learner qualification or aeromedical risks, aircraft airworthiness, performance and payload risks, crowded or complex airspace, visibility, weather, terrain, night operations, and external pressures such as schedule, work, family, and financial concerns. Flight instruction adds the learner's actions as a hazard because the learner is less proficient and may make errors that require the instructor to take control before the situation deteriorates.

### FI.I.F.K5b — Best Practices

Conduct a risk analysis before providing instruction and include the learner in it. Select safe practice locations, allow sufficient altitude and space, use appropriate training for each maneuver, remain alert to learner and external changes, and be prepared to take over controls. Before flight, verify airworthiness, review discrepancies, and resolve questions with maintenance personnel. Monitor instructor and learner aeromedical condition, aircraft state, weather, traffic, airspace, and learner plans throughout the lesson.

### FI.I.F.K5c — Special Considerations While Teaching Takeoffs and Landings

Teach takeoff information before entering the runway, because the learner's attention during takeoff is devoted to aircraft control and runway clearance. Use concise prompting during approach and landing to avoid missed transmissions, reduced collision avoidance, and loss of situational awareness. Teach learners to use current conditions, aircraft, environment, and expected performance to select aiming and touchdown points rather than applying the maneuver mechanically. Maintain adequate spacing from other aircraft and anticipate learner errors during low-speed, high-workload operations.

## FI.I.F.K6 — Aeronautical Decision-Making, CRM, and SRM

ADM and SRM are taught through risk-management decisions in realistic scenarios. SBT requires the learner to identify hazards, evaluate options, choose and explain a course of action, and reassess as conditions change. Professional pilots may use CRM in a crew environment; pilots operating alone use SRM to manage information, automation, workload, and available resources.

A flight instructor gradually transfers planning and execution responsibility to the learner while continuing to demonstrate maneuvers and protect safety. The learner should use appropriate resources and maintain situational awareness rather than relying on a single tool or decision.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
Ask the learner to verbalize the risk decision before acting: identify the hazard, explain the available options, select a mitigation, and state what would trigger a new decision.
]]

## Instructor Application — Teaching and Managing Risk in Flight

Use a scenario appropriate to the learner's training phase. Before flight, have the learner identify PAVE hazards, assess likelihood and severity, select a TEAM response, and state the remaining risk. During flight, monitor learner actions and condition, aircraft control, traffic, weather, airspace, terrain, and changing circumstances. Prompt the learner to identify hazards and mitigations, but do not add instruction or distraction when it would reduce safe aircraft control, collision avoidance, or situational awareness.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. What are the three basic steps of risk management?

[[answer:
Identify the hazard, assess the risk by likelihood and severity, and mitigate the risk by reducing, eliminating, transferring, or consciously accepting remaining risk.
]]

### 2. What does PAVE represent, and who is included in the Pilot category during dual instruction?

[[answer:
Pilot, Aircraft, enVironment, and External pressures. During dual instruction, Pilot includes both the learner and the instructor, including qualification, proficiency, currency, and aeromedical factors.
]]

### 3. What is a FRAT, and what is the limitation of a numerical FRAT?

[[answer:
A Flight Risk Assessment Tool records hazards and associated risk during planning. A low numerical score can still conceal an unlisted or extreme hazard, so the tool does not replace analysis and mitigation of individual hazards.
]]

### 4. When should risk management be introduced and practiced?

[[answer:
Before the first flight, during every preflight and postflight briefing, throughout actual training flights, and during recurrent, transition, flight-review, proficiency, and currency training.
]]

### 5. How does risk-management teaching progress from pre-solo to cross-country training?

[[answer:
Pre-solo instruction is instructor-led and guided, with a basic analysis by first solo. After solo, the instructor reviews the learner's analysis and debriefs risk management. During cross-country training, the learner completes a full analysis for dual and solo cross-country flights and the instructor reviews and approves it.
]]

### 6. What special risk-management considerations apply during takeoffs and landings?

[[answer:
Brief complex information before takeoff, avoid unnecessary instruction during high workload, maintain collision avoidance and aircraft control, use concise approach cues, anticipate learner errors, maintain spacing, and use current conditions to guide approach and landing decisions.
]]

### 7. How does SBT develop ADM and SRM?

[[answer:
SBT gives the learner realistic situations in which to identify hazards, evaluate options, make and explain decisions, use resources, and reassess when conditions change. The instructor gradually transfers planning and execution responsibility while protecting safety.
]]
