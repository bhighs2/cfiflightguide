## Why This Matters

A flight instructor is not simply presenting information. The instructor is trying to produce a lasting change in what the learner knows, understands, decides, and can safely do.

A learner may be able to repeat an answer without understanding it, perform a maneuver once without truly acquiring the skill, or demonstrate a skill in a familiar training environment but fail to transfer that learning to a new situation.

The instructor therefore needs to understand how learning occurs, how skills are built, why learning sometimes stalls, how memory and practice affect retention, and how to design training that produces safe independent performance rather than short-term test preparation.

# Knowledge

## FI.I.B.K1 — Definitions of Learning

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=52 | end=53]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f-CI1Ufk0NM | start=01:40 | end=01:56 | title=Learning Process — What Is Learning]]

Learning is a change that results from experience. In aviation training, meaningful learning is demonstrated by a change in knowledge, understanding, behavior, judgment, or skill that can be applied when needed.

Learning is more than exposure to information.

A learner has not necessarily learned because the instructor:

- Explained the material
- Demonstrated the maneuver
- Assigned reading
- Received a correct answer once
- Observed one successful repetition

Evidence of learning appears when the learner can recall, explain, apply, perform, adapt, and transfer the material appropriately.

#### Aviation Example

A learner can state that increasing angle of attack eventually produces a stall.

That demonstrates some knowledge.

The learning becomes more useful when the learner can also:

- Recognize increasing angle of attack in flight
- Identify approaching stall cues
- Explain why the stall occurs
- Recover correctly
- Avoid an inadvertent stall in a traffic-pattern scenario

#### Instructor Application

Measure learning through observable performance.

Ask:

- What can the learner explain without prompting?
- What can the learner demonstrate?
- Can the learner apply the concept in a new situation?
- Does the performance remain acceptable when workload changes?

The objective is not simply to complete instruction. The objective is to produce learning.

## FI.I.B.K2 — Learning Theory as It Applies to Ground and Flight Instruction

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=53 | end=58]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f-CI1Ufk0NM | start=01:56 | end=08:16 | title=Learning Process — Theories of Learning]]

Learning theory helps the instructor understand why certain instructional methods produce better results than others.

No single theory explains every aspect of aviation learning. Effective instruction often uses principles from more than one theory depending on the objective and the learner.

### Behaviorism

Behaviorism emphasizes observable behavior and the relationship between a stimulus, a response, and the consequences that follow.

In aviation instruction, behaviorist techniques are useful when the instructor needs to build or reinforce specific observable responses.

Examples include:

- Checklist discipline
- Callouts
- Positive exchange of flight controls
- Standardized flows
- Correct control responses
- Immediate correction of an unsafe action
- Repetition of a procedure until it becomes reliable

#### Reinforcement

Consequences affect whether behavior is likely to be repeated.

Effective reinforcement should be:

- Timely
- Specific
- Connected to the desired behavior
- Appropriate to the learner
- Based on legitimate performance

Instead of:

> Good job.

Use:

> You identified the crosswind early and established the correct wind correction before turning final.

The second response tells the learner exactly what behavior should be retained.

#### Limitations

Training should not become mechanical conditioning without understanding.

A learner who memorizes a checklist response but does not understand why the action matters may fail when the situation changes.

Behavior must eventually be connected to understanding, judgment, and application.

### Cognitive Theory

Cognitive theory focuses on what occurs mentally while the learner receives, organizes, interprets, stores, retrieves, and applies information.

The learner is an active participant rather than simply a receiver of stimuli.

Cognitive learning is especially important when teaching:

- Aerodynamics
- Weather
- Aircraft systems
- Navigation
- Regulations
- Risk management
- Aeronautical decision-making
- Problem solving
- Scenario analysis

#### Building Mental Models

New information is easier to understand when it connects to something the learner already knows.

For example, when teaching a constant-speed propeller, an instructor can begin with the learner's existing understanding of engine power, propeller blade angle, and RPM, then connect those concepts to governor operation.

#### Instructor Application

Ask the learner to:

- Explain relationships
- Compare alternatives
- Predict outcomes
- Solve problems
- Draw a system
- Teach the concept back
- Apply the concept to a scenario

This provides stronger evidence of cognitive learning than simple recall.

#### Risk Management

A learner who is trained only to produce memorized responses may perform correctly in a familiar situation but fail when conditions change.

The instructor should develop both correct behavior and the understanding necessary to adapt that behavior safely.

## FI.I.B.K3 — Perceptions and Insight

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=58 | end=59]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f-CI1Ufk0NM | start=08:16 | end=19:11 | title=Learning Process — Perceptions and Factors Affecting Perception]]

Perception is the learner's interpretation of information received through the senses and prior experience.

Two learners may experience the same instructional event but interpret it differently.

Factors that can affect perception include:

- Physical condition
- Goals and values
- Prior experience
- Expectations
- Self-concept
- Time and opportunity
- Stress or threat
- The instructional environment

#### Aviation Example

During a stall demonstration, one learner may perceive the buffet as useful aerodynamic feedback.

Another learner may interpret the same sensation primarily as evidence that the airplane is becoming dangerously uncontrollable.

The physical event is the same. The perception is different.

#### Instructor Application

Do not assume that the learner interpreted an event exactly as intended.

Ask questions such as:

> What did you notice as we approached the stall?

> What control change caused the nose to move left?

> What did you see outside as the bank increased?

The learner's answer reveals the learner's perception.

### Insight

Insight occurs when the learner organizes perceptions into a meaningful relationship or understanding.

The learner begins to see how individual facts or actions connect.

#### Aviation Example

A learner may separately understand:

- Wind direction
- Groundspeed
- Crab angle
- Bank
- Ground track

Insight develops when the learner recognizes that all of these concepts are related during ground reference maneuvers and later applies the same wind-correction concepts during traffic patterns and cross-country flight.

#### Risk Management

Incorrect perception can create unsafe conclusions.

The instructor should identify misunderstandings early, particularly when a learner interprets normal aircraft cues, risk, weather, performance, or personal capability incorrectly.

## FI.I.B.K4 — Acquiring Knowledge

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=60 | end=62]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f-CI1Ufk0NM | start=19:11 | end=24:15 | title=Learning Process — Building Blocks and Knowledge Progression]]

Knowledge should progress beyond memorization toward understanding and application.

A learner may initially need to remember facts, terminology, procedures, or limitations, but useful aviation knowledge requires the ability to organize and apply those facts.

### From Rote Knowledge to Application

A useful progression is:

1. **Remember** the information.
2. **Understand** what it means.
3. **Apply** it in a familiar situation.
4. **Relate and correlate** it with other knowledge.
5. **Use it independently** in realistic situations.

#### Example — Stall Knowledge

A learner may progress from:

> A stall occurs when the critical angle of attack is exceeded.

to:

> An airplane can stall at any airspeed, attitude, or power setting if the critical angle of attack is exceeded.

and then to:

> In a skidding base-to-final turn, increased load factor and poor coordination can place the airplane closer to a stall while leaving little altitude for recovery.

The last response demonstrates a more useful level of knowledge because the learner is applying and correlating concepts.

### Organizing Knowledge

Knowledge is easier to acquire when instruction:

- Begins with known concepts
- Progresses from simple to complex
- Organizes related information
- Uses meaningful examples
- Requires active learner participation
- Provides opportunities for application
- Revisits important concepts in different contexts

#### Instructor Application

Avoid teaching isolated facts when a relationship can be taught instead.

Whenever possible, connect:

**Fact → Meaning → Application → Operational consequence**

## FI.I.B.K5 — Laws of Learning

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=62 | end=64]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4IxiQeh0FFI | start=22:30 | end=31:55 | title=FLY8MA — Laws of Learning and Application]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f-CI1Ufk0NM | start=24:15 | end=30:04 | title=Learning Process — Laws and Principles of Learning]]

The laws of learning are principles instructors can use to make training more effective.

[[mnemonic: REEPIR | Readiness · Exercise · Effect · Primacy · Intensity · Recency]]

### Readiness

People learn best when they are physically, mentally, and emotionally ready to learn.

Readiness can be reduced by:

- Fatigue
- Illness
- Anxiety
- Lack of preparation
- Lack of purpose
- Excessive workload

#### Instructor Application

Before beginning a demanding lesson, determine whether the learner is ready to benefit from it.

A learner who has not prepared for a cross-country planning lesson may need foundational instruction before meaningful application can occur.

### Exercise

Things most often repeated are generally better remembered and performed.

Practice strengthens learning, but repetition alone is not enough.

**Practice should repeat the correct behavior.**

Repeated errors can also become learned.

#### Instructor Application

Do not allow a learner to perform the same incorrect landing technique repeatedly without meaningful intervention.

### Effect

Learning is strengthened when the experience is satisfying and weakened when it is associated with frustration, humiliation, or repeated failure.

This does not mean every lesson should be easy.

Appropriate challenge can be satisfying when the learner understands the objective and can recognize progress.

### Primacy

What is learned first often creates a strong and lasting impression.

Incorrect first learning can be difficult to replace.

#### Aviation Example

Teach the positive exchange of flight controls correctly from the beginning:

> You have the flight controls.

> I have the flight controls.

> You have the flight controls.

Do not permit an ambiguous control-transfer habit and attempt to repair it much later.

### Intensity

A vivid, realistic, meaningful experience is often learned more strongly than a routine or abstract one.

Scenario-based training, realistic demonstrations, visual aids, and actual application can increase intensity.

Intensity should not be confused with unnecessary fear or unsafe demonstrations.

### Recency

Things learned or practiced recently are generally easier to recall.

#### Instructor Application

Review critical procedures close enough to the time of use that the learner can retrieve them effectively.

Before practicing emergency approaches, review the immediate actions and priorities that will be needed.

## FI.I.B.K6 — Domains of Learning

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=64 | end=69]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f-CI1Ufk0NM | start=30:04 | end=44:51 | title=Learning Process — Domains of Learning]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4IxiQeh0FFI | start=01:03:15 | title=FLY8MA — Cognitive, Affective, and Psychomotor Learning Domains]]

Learning occurs in more than one domain.

Aviation instruction frequently involves all three domains at the same time.

### Cognitive Domain

The cognitive domain involves knowledge, understanding, thinking, problem solving, and judgment.

Examples include:

- Explaining aerodynamic principles
- Interpreting weather
- Calculating performance
- Analyzing risk
- Selecting an alternate
- Explaining a regulation

### Affective Domain

The affective domain involves attitudes, beliefs, values, priorities, and willingness to act.

Examples include:

- Respect for limitations
- Willingness to discontinue an unsafe approach
- Acceptance of checklist discipline
- Attitude toward risk
- Professionalism
- Personal responsibility for safety

A learner may know the correct decision cognitively but still possess an attitude that makes the unsafe choice more likely.

### Psychomotor Domain

The psychomotor domain involves physical skill and coordinated movement.

Examples include:

- Maintaining aircraft control
- Coordinating rudder and aileron
- Establishing pitch and power
- Manipulating avionics
- Performing a landing flare

### Integration

Safe aviation performance normally requires integration of all three.

#### Example — Go-Around

**Cognitive:** Recognize that the approach is unstable.

**Affective:** Be willing to abandon the landing rather than force it.

**Psychomotor:** Apply power, manage pitch, configure the airplane, maintain control, and climb safely.

#### Instructor Application

Do not assess only whether the learner can manipulate the controls.

Ask whether the learner:

- Understands what is happening
- Values the correct safety outcome
- Can physically execute the required action

## FI.I.B.K7 — Characteristics of Learning

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=70 | end=71]]

Learning has several important characteristics.

### Learning Is Purposeful

People learn more effectively when they understand the purpose of the learning.

Connect training to a meaningful aviation objective.

Instead of:

> Today we need to do steep turns because they are on the test.

Explain how the maneuver develops aircraft control, division of attention, energy management, and recognition of performance trends.

### Learning Is the Result of Experience

Learning develops through interaction with information, demonstration, practice, feedback, and application.

The instructor can explain a landing flare, but the learner still needs appropriate experience to build judgment and control.

### Learning Is Multifaceted

A single lesson can involve knowledge, attitudes, physical skills, risk management, communication, and judgment simultaneously.

### Learning Is an Active Process

The learner should actively participate.

Useful activities include:

- Predicting
- Explaining
- Comparing
- Performing
- Evaluating
- Teaching back
- Solving scenarios
- Making decisions

#### Instructor Application

Reduce passive instruction when active participation would produce stronger learning.

## FI.I.B.K8 — Scenario-Based Training (SBT)

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=82 | end=84]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4IxiQeh0FFI | start=44:50 | end=51:50 | title=FLY8MA — Problem-Based Learning and Building Scenarios]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f-CI1Ufk0NM | start=55:55 | end=59:01 | title=Learning Process — Scenario-Based Training]]

Scenario-based training uses realistic situations to develop the learner's ability to integrate knowledge, skill, judgment, and risk management.

A good scenario gives the learner a reason to make decisions rather than simply follow a scripted sequence.

### Characteristics of a Useful Scenario

A scenario should be:

- Relevant to the learner
- Realistic
- Appropriate to the learner's level
- Connected to lesson objectives
- Complex enough to require decisions
- Safe to conduct
- Debriefed afterward

### Example — Cross-Country Scenario

Instead of asking only:

> What is the minimum VFR fuel reserve?

Present:

> You are 35 minutes from destination. The airport is reporting increasing winds and the nearest fuel stop is 20 minutes behind you. Your calculated landing fuel is becoming lower than planned. What information do you need, what risks are developing, and what would you do?

The learner now integrates:

- Fuel planning
- Weather
- Regulations
- Personal minimums
- Alternatives
- External pressure
- Decision-making

### Instructor Role

Do not solve the scenario too early.

Allow the learner to:

1. Recognize the problem.
2. Gather information.
3. Identify hazards.
4. Consider alternatives.
5. Make a decision.
6. Evaluate the outcome.

#### Risk Management

A scenario should teach risk management without creating unnecessary real-world risk.

The instructor remains responsible for safety and should intervene before the exercise becomes unsafe.

## FI.I.B.K9 — Acquiring Skill Knowledge

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=74 | end=77]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4IxiQeh0FFI | start=31:55 | end=35:45 | title=FLY8MA — Levels of Learning and Building Skills]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f-CI1Ufk0NM | start=44:51 | end=48:14 | title=Learning Process — Stages of Skill Acquisition]]

A physical aviation skill is developed progressively.

The learner should understand what the skill is intended to accomplish, practice the correct actions, receive meaningful feedback, and gradually perform with less instructor assistance.

### Stages

#### Cognitive Stage

The learner is trying to understand what to do.

Performance may be:

- Slow
- Deliberate
- Inconsistent
- Highly dependent on instructor explanation

The learner may need to think consciously about every step.

#### Associative Stage

The learner begins connecting the correct cues and responses.

Performance becomes:

- Smoother
- More consistent
- Less dependent on verbal prompting
- More accurate

The learner is increasingly able to recognize and correct errors.

#### Automatic Response Stage

With appropriate practice, portions of a skill can require less conscious attention.

This can free mental capacity for:

- Traffic
- Radio communication
- Navigation
- Risk management
- Higher-level decisions

Automaticity is useful only when the learned response is correct.

Poorly learned habits can also become automatic.

### Knowledge of Results

Learners need meaningful information about the result of their performance.

Feedback should help the learner answer:

- What happened?
- Why did it happen?
- What was done correctly?
- What needs to change?
- What should I do on the next attempt?

#### Example

Instead of:

> You were all over the altitude.

Use:

> During the steep turn, your pitch decreased as the bank passed 30 degrees. That allowed the airplane to descend 120 feet. On the next attempt, anticipate the additional back pressure as the bank increases and cross-check the outside pitch picture with the altimeter.

### How to Develop Skills

Effective skill development generally includes:

1. Clear objective
2. Explanation
3. Demonstration
4. Guided practice
5. Feedback
6. Repetition of correct performance
7. Increasing independence
8. Application under realistic conditions

### Learning Plateaus

A learner may temporarily stop showing visible improvement even though learning continues.

A plateau can occur because of:

- Fatigue
- Overtraining
- Increasing task complexity
- Need for consolidation
- Ineffective practice
- Loss of motivation
- A remaining weakness in a prerequisite skill

#### Instructor Response

Do not automatically interpret a plateau as lack of ability.

Consider:

- Changing the task
- Taking a break
- Reviewing fundamentals
- Varying the practice
- Explaining that plateaus are normal
- Identifying a specific limiting skill
- Returning later with a different instructional approach

## FI.I.B.K10 — Types of Practice

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=77 | end=79]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4IxiQeh0FFI | start=35:45 | end=40:00 | title=FLY8MA — Types of Practice: Deliberate and Blocked]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f-CI1Ufk0NM | start=48:14 | end=55:55 | title=Learning Process — Types of Practice]]

Practice should be purposeful rather than simply repetitive.

### Deliberate Practice

Deliberate practice focuses attention on specific weaknesses or components of performance and includes meaningful feedback.

Example:

A learner consistently allows airspeed to decay on short final.

Instead of flying ten more full traffic patterns with the same error, the instructor identifies the specific pitch-power relationship and deliberately practices the correction.

### Blocked Practice

Blocked practice repeats the same task or maneuver several times before moving to another task.

Example:

- Three steep turns
- Then three power-off stalls
- Then three short-field landings

Blocked practice can help during early skill acquisition because the learner can concentrate on one task.

### Varied or Random Practice

Varied practice changes the sequence, conditions, or context.

Example:

During a later-stage flight, the learner may be asked to perform:

- A diversion
- Followed by a steep turn
- Then an emergency scenario
- Then a landing at an unfamiliar airport

This requires retrieval, recognition, and adaptation rather than simple repetition.

### Whole and Component Practice

Complex skills may be practiced:

- As the complete task
- By isolating a difficult component
- By combining components progressively

#### Instructor Application

Choose the type of practice based on:

- Learner experience
- Complexity
- Safety
- Specific weakness
- Desired level of independence
- Need for transfer to realistic operations

## FI.I.B.K11 — Evaluation Versus Critique

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=79 | end=80]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4IxiQeh0FFI | start=51:50 | end=58:50 | title=FLY8MA — Effective Critiques and Student Assessment]]

Evaluation and critique are related but are not identical.

### Evaluation

Evaluation determines how performance compares with an objective, standard, or desired outcome.

Examples:

- Meets standard
- Does not meet standard
- Within ACS tolerance
- Safe or unsafe
- Proficient or not yet proficient

### Critique

A critique analyzes the performance to help the learner improve.

An effective critique identifies:

- What happened
- What was done well
- What needs improvement
- Why the error occurred
- What corrective action is appropriate

#### Example

**Evaluation:**

> The landing did not meet the short-field standard.

**Critique:**

> The approach became fast after you lowered the nose to correct a low glidepath. That increased float and caused touchdown beyond the selected point. On the next approach, correct the glidepath primarily with power while preserving the target airspeed.

#### Instructor Application

A grade without useful feedback does not tell the learner how to improve.

Feedback without reference to a standard may not tell the learner whether the performance is acceptable.

Use both appropriately.

## FI.I.B.K12 — Distractions, Interruptions, Fixation, and Inattention

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=80 | end=82]]

Workload can interfere with learning and safe aircraft operation.

The instructor should teach learners how to recognize and manage attention rather than simply trying to eliminate every distraction.

### Distractions

Distractions compete for attention.

Examples include:

- Radio calls
- Passengers
- Avionics programming
- Unexpected traffic
- Checklists
- Instructor questions
- Equipment problems

### Interruptions

An interruption breaks the normal sequence of a task.

A common risk is returning to the task at the wrong point or failing to resume it at all.

#### Instructor Technique

After an interruption:

- Re-establish aircraft control
- Identify where the task was interrupted
- Restart or verify the checklist at an appropriate point
- Do not assume the task was completed

### Fixation

Fixation occurs when attention becomes excessively concentrated on one item at the expense of other important information.

Examples include:

- Staring at an avionics screen
- Chasing altitude while ignoring airspeed
- Troubleshooting a minor problem while the airplane drifts toward terrain
- Focusing on making a landing after the approach becomes unstable

### Inattention

Inattention is failure to give necessary attention to a task or cue.

It may result from:

- Fatigue
- Boredom
- Overload
- Distraction
- Poor task management
- Lack of understanding

### Teaching Realistic Distractions

Distractions should be:

- Planned
- Appropriate to learner proficiency
- Introduced at a safe time
- Realistic
- Closely supervised

The goal is to teach workload management, not surprise the learner for entertainment.

#### Risk Management

The instructor should never allow a training distraction to compromise safety.

Aircraft control remains the priority.

## FI.I.B.K13 — Errors

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=84 | end=86]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f-CI1Ufk0NM | start=59:01 | end=01:05:36 | title=Learning Process — Kinds of Errors and Tools to Reduce Errors]]

Errors are a normal part of learning, but unrecognized or repeatedly practiced errors can become unsafe habits.

The instructor should distinguish between:

- A learner who does not know what correct performance looks like
- A learner who understands but lacks physical skill
- A learner who recognizes an error but reacts too late
- A learner who is overloaded or distracted
- A learner who has learned an incorrect habit

### Error Recognition

As proficiency increases, the learner should become increasingly capable of detecting errors without instructor prompting.

Ask:

> What did you notice?

> How did that compare with your target?

> What would you change on the next attempt?

### Correcting Errors

Effective correction should:

1. Protect safety.
2. Identify the actual error.
3. Identify the cause.
4. Provide a specific correction.
5. Give the learner another opportunity to perform.
6. Confirm that the correction produced the desired result.

### Avoid Over-Correction

Do not overwhelm the learner by correcting every small deviation simultaneously.

Prioritize:

1. Safety
2. Aircraft control
3. Major objective
4. Significant performance errors
5. Minor refinements

#### Risk Management

Inadequate correction may allow an unsafe habit to develop.

Excessive correction can overload the learner and reduce the ability to identify and correct errors independently.

## FI.I.B.K14 — Memory

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=86 | end=89]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4IxiQeh0FFI | start=40:00 | end=44:50 | title=FLY8MA — Human Memory and Forgetting]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f-CI1Ufk0NM | start=01:05:36 | end=01:11:16 | title=Learning Process — Memory, Forgetting, and Retention]]

Learning has little practical value if the learner cannot retrieve and use it when needed.

Memory involves receiving, storing, and retrieving information.

### Sensory Memory

Sensory memory briefly receives information from the environment.

Only a portion of available sensory information receives enough attention to move into further processing.

#### Aviation Application

A learner in the traffic pattern may simultaneously receive:

- Visual traffic information
- Radio calls
- Engine sound
- Instrument indications
- Control feel
- Instructor comments

Attention determines which information is processed.

### Short-Term Memory

Short-Term Memory holds a limited amount of information for a limited period.

It is vulnerable to:

- Distraction
- Interruption
- Excessive workload
- Too much information presented at once

#### Instructor Application

Avoid long strings of instructions during high workload.

Instead of giving six corrections at once, prioritize the most important item and allow the learner to act.

### Long-Term Memory

Long-Term Memory stores information for later retrieval.

Information is more likely to be retained when it is:

- Meaningful
- Organized
- Associated with existing knowledge
- Repeated appropriately
- Actively used
- Applied in different contexts

### How Usage Affects Memory

Information that is retrieved and used tends to become easier to retrieve again.

The instructor should create opportunities for the learner to:

- Recall without prompts
- Explain
- Apply
- Perform
- Teach back
- Use knowledge in scenarios

### Forgetting

Forgetting can result when information:

- Is not used
- Was never learned meaningfully
- Is displaced or confused by other learning
- Cannot be retrieved under current conditions

#### Instructor Application

Do not assume a learner retains proficiency indefinitely simply because the task was previously completed.

Review and practice should be based on the importance of the material and the learner's demonstrated retention.

## FI.I.B.K15 — Retention of Learning

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=89 | end=90]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f-CI1Ufk0NM | start=01:11:16 | end=01:16:44 | title=Learning Process — Instructor Tools for Retention]]

Retention is the ability to preserve and retrieve learning over time.

Retention improves when learning is:

- Meaningful
- Organized
- Practiced correctly
- Used repeatedly
- Connected to prior knowledge
- Applied in realistic situations
- Reviewed over time rather than only immediately before an evaluation

### Meaningful Learning

Understanding tends to be retained better than disconnected memorization.

A learner who understands why a procedure works has more ways to reconstruct the correct response if exact wording is forgotten.

### Active Retrieval

Ask the learner to retrieve information rather than always presenting it again.

Instead of:

> Remember, the three things we discussed for an engine failure were...

Ask:

> Engine failure. What are your immediate priorities?

### Spacing and Review

Important skills should be revisited over time.

A learner who performed stalls well three weeks ago should demonstrate retained proficiency rather than receiving credit based solely on past performance.

### Instructor Application

Before introducing advanced material, verify retention of prerequisite knowledge and skills.

## FI.I.B.K16 — Transfer of Learning

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=90 | end=93]]

[[youtube: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=f-CI1Ufk0NM | start=01:16:44 | title=Learning Process — Transfer of Learning]]

Transfer of learning occurs when previous learning affects performance in a new situation.

### Positive Transfer

Positive transfer occurs when previous learning helps with the new task.

Examples:

- Wind correction learned during ground reference maneuvers helps in the traffic pattern.
- Instrument scan skills help during flight in reduced visibility.
- Energy-management concepts from slow flight help during approach and landing.

### Negative Transfer

Negative transfer occurs when previous learning interferes with the new task.

Examples:

- A learner transitioning aircraft applies a control technique appropriate to the previous airplane but not the new airplane.
- A pilot brings an incorrect checklist habit from another operation.
- A learner applies automobile steering instincts to rudder use during taxi.

### Instructor Application

Before teaching a new task:

1. Identify related prior learning.
2. Use helpful similarities.
3. Clearly identify important differences.
4. Watch for inappropriate transfer.
5. Require application in the new context.

### Building Transfer Deliberately

Learning transfers more effectively when the learner practices concepts in multiple realistic situations.

Do not teach risk management only as a classroom topic.

Apply it during:

- Preflight planning
- Weather decisions
- Fuel decisions
- Traffic patterns
- Diversions
- Equipment abnormalities
- Go-around decisions

The goal is independent application beyond the original lesson.

# Risk Management

## FI.I.B.R1 — Inadequate or Incomplete Instruction

Inadequate or incomplete instruction creates risk when the learner is allowed to progress with missing knowledge, misunderstood concepts, incorrect habits, or skills that are not sufficiently developed for the next task.

### How the Risk Develops

Examples include:

- Teaching a procedure without explaining its purpose or limitations
- Omitting an important prerequisite
- Allowing repeated incorrect practice
- Assuming a correct verbal answer proves practical understanding
- Advancing the learner before the required skill is stable
- Failing to revisit knowledge or skill that has not been retained
- Failing to identify negative transfer from previous training

### Instructor Mitigation

The instructor should:

1. Establish a clear objective and standard.
2. Verify prerequisite knowledge and skill.
3. Present complete and accurate instruction.
4. Observe the learner rather than assuming understanding.
5. Correct significant errors before they become habits.
6. Require application, not only recall.
7. Verify retention before building on previous learning.
8. Delay advancement or endorsement when required proficiency has not been demonstrated.

### Aviation Example

A learner can recite the steps of a go-around but consistently allows the nose to pitch excessively high when power is applied.

The instruction is not complete simply because the learner knows the verbal procedure. The instructor must address the psychomotor response, explain the aerodynamic reason for the pitch change, provide guided practice, and verify independent performance before treating the task as learned.

#### Risk Management

The instructor should not confuse completion of instructional content with completion of learning.

The risk is mitigated only when the learner demonstrates the required knowledge, judgment, and skill at the appropriate level of independence.

## FI.I.B.R2 — Lack of Learner Motivation

A learner who lacks motivation may participate physically without engaging sufficiently to develop durable knowledge, judgment, or skill.

Lack of motivation can reduce:

- Preparation
- Attention
- Persistence
- Willingness to practice
- Receptiveness to feedback
- Retention
- Progress toward independent performance

### Possible Causes

Before labeling the learner as unmotivated, consider whether motivation is being affected by:

- Unclear lesson purpose
- Training that does not connect to the learner's goals
- Repeated failure without useful feedback
- Excessive repetition after proficiency
- Tasks that are too difficult or too easy
- Fatigue or illness
- Anxiety
- Poor instructor preparation
- Lack of visible progress
- A learning plateau
- Outside distractions or pressures

### Instructor Mitigation

The instructor should:

- Identify the learner's goals
- Explain why the lesson matters
- Establish clear and achievable intermediate objectives
- Recognize legitimate progress
- Provide specific feedback
- Adjust the method without lowering the standard
- Vary practice when repetition has become unproductive
- Help the learner recognize that plateaus can be normal
- Modify or stop training when physical or emotional condition prevents meaningful learning

#### Aviation Example

A learner becomes disengaged after repeatedly practicing landings without understanding why performance is not improving.

Instead of adding more identical repetitions, the instructor isolates the limiting skill, gives specific feedback, establishes a measurable objective for the next attempts, and reconnects that objective to the learner's goal of safe solo flight.

## FI.I.B.R3 — Recognizing and Correcting Learner Errors

Errors are expected during learning, but the instructor must recognize when an error creates immediate risk, reflects a misunderstanding, or is becoming an established habit.

### Identify the Type and Cause of the Error

An error may result from:

- Missing knowledge
- Incorrect knowledge
- Misperception
- Incomplete skill development
- Poor habit
- Distraction
- Interruption
- Fixation
- Excessive workload
- Fatigue
- Negative transfer
- Inadequate instruction

The correction should address the cause rather than only the visible result.

### Prioritize Corrections

Correct in this order:

1. Safety
2. Aircraft control
3. Major lesson objective
4. Significant performance errors
5. Minor refinement

Do not overload the learner with simultaneous corrections that exceed the learner's ability to process and apply them.

### Correction Process

1. Protect safety.
2. Identify the error.
3. Determine the likely cause.
4. Explain or demonstrate the correction.
5. Give the learner another opportunity.
6. Require learner self-assessment.
7. Verify that correct performance can be repeated.

#### Aviation Example

A learner repeatedly loses altitude in steep turns.

If the cause is an incorrect pitch picture, simply saying "hold altitude" does not correct the underlying problem.

The instructor should identify the pitch change as bank increases, relate it to lift and load factor, demonstrate the correct sight picture if necessary, and then allow the learner to practice and evaluate the result.

# Skills

## FI.I.B.S1 — Apply Educational Theories to Ground and Flight Instruction

The instructor applicant should be able to move beyond defining educational theories and deliberately use them during actual instruction.

### Ground Instruction Application

When teaching an aircraft system:

- Connect new information to prior knowledge
- Organize the material into a useful mental model
- Ask the learner to explain relationships
- Use questions that require prediction and application
- Reinforce correct reasoning with specific feedback

This applies cognitive learning principles.

### Flight Instruction Application

When teaching a checklist, callout, or control response:

- Define the desired behavior
- Demonstrate it correctly
- Require correct learner performance
- Provide timely and specific reinforcement
- Repeat until the response becomes reliable
- Connect the behavior to the underlying reason and operational consequence

This uses behaviorist principles while still developing understanding.

### Integrated Example

During landing training, the instructor can reinforce a correct crosswind-control response while also asking the learner to explain why the control inputs change as the airplane slows.

The learner is developing both correct observable behavior and the cognitive understanding required to adapt that behavior.

## FI.I.B.S2 — Recognize and Correct Conditions That Undermine the Learning Process

The instructor should continuously evaluate whether the conditions for useful learning still exist.

### Conditions That Can Undermine Learning

Examples include:

- Fatigue
- Illness
- Anxiety
- Lack of motivation
- Excessive workload
- Distraction
- Fixation
- Poor preparation
- Inadequate prerequisite knowledge
- Repeated incorrect practice
- An ineffective instructional method
- Learning plateaus
- Negative transfer
- Poor or delayed feedback

### Instructor Response

When performance deteriorates:

1. Protect safety.
2. Determine whether the cause is knowledge, skill, workload, physical condition, motivation, or instruction.
3. Reduce unnecessary workload when appropriate.
4. Clarify the objective.
5. Break the task into manageable components if necessary.
6. Change the instructional method when the current method is ineffective.
7. Provide specific feedback.
8. Resume full-task practice only when it is productive.
9. Stop or reschedule the lesson when meaningful learning or safety can no longer be maintained.

#### Instructor Self-Check

The instructor should ask:

- Is the learner actually ready for this task?
- Is the objective clear?
- Is the learner overloaded?
- Am I talking too much?
- Is the learner practicing the correct behavior?
- Have I identified the real cause of the error?
- Would a break, different explanation, or different type of practice be more effective?

## FI.I.B.S3 — Plan for and Use Techniques, Including Realistic Distractions That Teach Flight Students How to Manage a Workload

Realistic distractions can be useful instructional tools when they are planned, appropriate to proficiency, and introduced without compromising safety.

### Before Introducing Distractions

The learner should first demonstrate enough baseline proficiency to manage the primary task safely.

The instructor should determine:

- The learning objective
- The distraction to be introduced
- Why that distraction is operationally realistic
- The expected learner response
- Safety limits
- When the instructor will intervene

### Examples of Realistic Distractions

Appropriate examples may include:

- A radio call during a routine cockpit task
- A request to identify traffic while managing avionics
- A passenger-style question during a low-workload phase
- A simulated minor equipment issue
- An interruption during a checklist
- A change in routing that requires reprioritization

The distraction should teach workload management rather than simply create surprise.

### Workload-Management Technique

Teach the learner to:

1. Maintain aircraft control.
2. Recognize the interruption or distraction.
3. Prioritize the most important task.
4. Delay or reject a nonessential task when necessary.
5. Return deliberately to the interrupted task.
6. Verify completion rather than assume completion.
7. Reassess the overall situation.

### Example — Interrupted Checklist

During a low-workload portion of training, the instructor introduces a realistic radio or passenger-style interruption while the learner is completing a checklist.

After the interruption, evaluate whether the learner:

- Maintains aircraft control
- Recognizes that the checklist was interrupted
- Returns to a known point
- Verifies completion
- Avoids fixation on the secondary task

#### Risk Management

The instructor remains responsible for safety.

Do not introduce distractions during a phase of flight or at a learner proficiency level where the exercise creates unnecessary risk.
