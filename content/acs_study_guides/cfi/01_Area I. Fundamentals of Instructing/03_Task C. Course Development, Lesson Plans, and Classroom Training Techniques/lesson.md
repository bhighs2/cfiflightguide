# Task C. Course Development, Lesson Plans, and Classroom Training Techniques

**Area I — Fundamentals of Instructing**

## Objective

To determine the applicant understands the teaching process, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate appropriate skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

The teaching process organizes material so a learner can understand it. A well-planned course of training, syllabus, and lesson plan connect objectives, standards, teaching methods, learner practice, and assessment in a logical sequence.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested Schedule**

- Ground lesson: approximately 60 minutes
- Lesson-plan application: approximately 20 minutes

**Equipment**

- Aviation Instructor's Handbook
- CFI Airplane ACS
- Whiteboard or tablet
- Sample syllabus and lesson plan
- Flight Guide visual aids

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner Actions**

- Explain the teaching process and essential teaching skills.
- Relate course, syllabus, blocks of learning, and lesson plans.
- Select a method appropriate to a stated learning objective.
- Prepare an objective, standards, and a lesson-plan outline.

**Instructor Actions**

- Use objectives and standards to organize instruction.
- Select and combine methods appropriate to the task and learner.
- Use instructional aids to support, not replace, instruction.
- Adapt the lesson plan to learner progress while preserving the course objective.

## Completion Standards

The applicant can explain the teaching process, select a teaching method appropriate to the task and learner, and prepare an instructional lesson plan for a ground lesson and maneuver introduction using clear objectives, completion standards, appropriate materials, and logical sequence.

**There is one main idea in this lesson:** good instruction begins by deciding **what the learner needs to accomplish and what acceptable performance looks like**. Once we know the objective and standard, we can organize the material, select the appropriate teaching method, let the learner apply it, and assess the result.

And throughout all of that, we are going to remember the same principle we've used before:

**Adapt the method, not the objective or the standard.**

### 1. The Teaching Process — Preparation Through Assessment

- **Teaching means organizing material so the learner can understand it and use it.**
- The teaching process moves through four steps:

**Preparation → Presentation → Application → Assessment**

#### Preparation

- **Preparation gets the learner ready to learn.**
- We establish:

    - What are we trying to accomplish?
    - What standard are we trying to reach?
    - What does the learner already know?
    - Why should this particular learner care about the lesson?
- So we are establishing the objective, creating motivation, and connecting new learning to prior knowledge.

#### Presentation

- **Presentation introduces the new knowledge or skill.**
- We may:

    - Explain it.
    - Demonstrate it.
    - Illustrate it.
    - Ask questions.
    - Use an instructional aid when it actually helps.
- The presentation method depends on what we're teaching and who we're teaching.

#### Application

- **Application puts the learning into the learner's hands.**
- They have to do something with what we just presented.

    - In a ground lesson, that may mean explaining or applying a concept.
    - In flight, that may mean actually performing the maneuver.

#### Assessment

- **Assessment tells us whether the instruction produced the intended result.**

- We compare the learner's performance with:

    - The objective.
    - The completion standard.

- Then determine:

    - What was satisfactory?
    - What still needs work?
    - What instruction should come next?

- **So teaching is not simply, “I explained it.”**

- It is:

**Prepare the learner → Present the material → Let the learner apply it → Assess the result**

- **And to carry out that process effectively, an instructor needs more than technical knowledge. That brings us to the essential teaching skills.**

### 2. Essential Teaching Skills — More Than Knowing the Subject

- **Effective instruction depends on four broad instructor skills:**

    - **People skills**
    - **Subject-matter expertise**
    - **Management skills**
    - **Assessment skills**

- **People skills matter because teaching is an interaction between people.**

    - We have to listen, communicate, build rapport, and adapt to the individual learner.

- **Subject-matter expertise means knowing the material well enough to explain it accurately and in more than one way.**

    - If one explanation does not work, we need another way to get the concept across.

- **Management skills include planning, organizing, leading, supervising, and using time effectively.**

    - We should know what we are trying to accomplish with the lesson rather than simply filling an hour.

- **Assessment skills tell us where the learner is before, during, and after the instruction.**

- **So the effective instructor combines technical knowledge with the ability to organize, communicate, manage, and evaluate learning.**

- **Those skills are used one lesson at a time, but each lesson eventually belongs to a larger course of training.**

### 3. Course of Training — Know the Big Goal

- **A course of training is a series of studies leading toward a specific goal.**

    - Private Pilot.
    - Instrument Rating.
    - Commercial Pilot.
    - Flight Instructor.

- The course gives us the **big destination**.

- The FAA's planning structure eventually breaks that course into:

    - Blocks of learning.
    - A training syllabus.
    - Individual lesson plans.

- **We don't need to develop all of that yet.** For now, the important point is that an individual lesson should never exist randomly. It should contribute to the larger training goal.

- **And that means before preparing a lesson, we have to answer the most important question: what exactly should the learner be able to do when this lesson is complete?**

### 4. Preparing the Lesson — Objectives and Standards

- **The objective tells us what the learner is expected to accomplish.**

- **The standard tells us what acceptable performance looks like.**

- The learner should understand both before the instruction begins.

- Objectives can involve any of the three learning domains:

    - **Cognitive:** knowledge.
    - **Affective:** attitudes, beliefs, and values.
    - **Psychomotor:** physical skill.

- **If we do not know what successful performance looks like, it is difficult to teach toward it and impossible to assess it consistently.**

#### Performance-Based Objectives

- **Performance-based objectives describe measurable and repeatable desired performance.**
- They contain three things:

**Skill or behavior → Conditions → Criteria**

- **Skill or behavior:** What exactly does the learner do?

- **Conditions:** Under what circumstances do they do it?

- **Criteria:** How well do they have to do it?

- For example, instead of saying: “Understand steep turns.” we want something measurable: “Given the appropriate aircraft and operating area, perform a steep turn while meeting the applicable performance criteria.”

- **If we cannot determine whether the learner succeeded, the objective is probably too vague.**

#### The ACS in Training

- **The ACS gives us performance objectives and standards for certification.**

- But **the ACS is not a complete training syllabus.**

- It identifies the critical tasks and standards used for certification.

- The instructor still has to develop the progression of:

    - Knowledge.
    - Practice.
    - Skill.
    - Judgment.
    - Risk management.
    needed to reach those standards.

- **The ACS tells us where the learner ultimately has to arrive. It does not tell us every instructional step required to get there.**

#### Decision-Based Objectives

- **Performance-based objectives tell us what the learner needs to perform. Decision-based objectives develop judgment and ADM.**

- The learner has to:

    - Gather information.
    - Evaluate the situation.
    - Consider alternatives.
    - Make a safe and timely decision.

- **Aviation example:** Rather than merely requiring the learner to demonstrate a go-around, put them into a situation where the approach becomes unstable and require them to recognize that a go-around is appropriate.

- **Decision-based objectives supplement maneuver training; they do not replace the underlying knowledge or physical skill.**

- **Once we know the objective and the standard, we have a destination. Now we have to organize the material so the learner can logically get there.**

### 5. Organization of Material — Introduction, Development, Conclusion

- **The traditional lesson organization is:**

**Introduction → Development → Conclusion**

#### Introduction

- **The introduction sets up the lesson.**

- It includes:

    - **Attention:** Get the learner focused.
    - **Motivation:** Explain why this matters.
    - **Overview:** Tell them where the lesson is going.

- **Aviation example:** Before crosswind landings:

    “The wind will rarely be perfectly aligned with the runway. Today we're going to learn how to control drift and maintain runway alignment through touchdown.”

#### Development

- **The development is the main teaching portion of the lesson.**

- Organize the information logically.

- Common arrangements include:

    - Past to present.
    - Simple to complex.
    - Known to unknown.
    - Most frequently used to least frequently used.

- **Aviation example:** Before teaching a complex traffic-pattern scenario, start with aircraft control and normal turns, then add configuration, pattern geometry, wind correction, communications, and traffic.

#### Conclusion

- **The conclusion brings the lesson back to the objective.**

- Review the important points.

- Reinforce what the learner should take away.

- **Do not introduce brand-new material in the conclusion.**

- **Now we know what we are teaching, what standard we're working toward, and how the lesson is organized. The next question becomes: what teaching method best fits this learner and this objective?**

### 6. Training Delivery Methods — Match the Method to the Need

- **There is no single best teaching method for every lesson.**

- The method should fit:

    - The objective.
    - The subject.
    - The learner.
    - The desired level of learning.
    - The environment.

- **We change the method to fit the learner. We do not change the standard to fit the learner.**

#### Lecture

- **Lecture is useful for presenting new material, giving an overview, summarizing ideas, or relating theory to practice.**
- But a teaching lecture is not simply talking continuously.
- We still need to:

    - Watch the learner.
    - Ask questions.
    - Look for confusion.
    - Adjust the presentation.
- **If the learner is lost, more words do not necessarily fix the problem.**

#### Discussion

- **Discussion combines instruction with active exchange.**
- It encourages:

    - Participation.
    - Recall.
    - Use of knowledge.
    - Higher-order thinking.
- The instructor keeps the discussion directed toward the objective and summarizes the important learning.

#### Guided Discussion

- **Guided discussion uses questions to draw knowledge and reasoning out of the learner.**

- The learner needs enough background knowledge to participate.

- Good questions are generally open-ended.

  Poor:

    **“Would you go around?”**

  Better:

    **“What are you seeing on this approach?”**

  Or:

    **“Why does that make you concerned?”**

- **How and why questions are especially useful because they expose the learner's reasoning rather than simply producing a yes or no answer.**

#### Cooperative or Group Learning

- **Cooperative learning places learners into small groups working toward a common objective.**

- One of its biggest advantages is that it **continually requires active participation.**

- Successful group learning still needs structure:

    - Clear objectives.
    - Complete instructions.
    - Access to the required information.
    - Enough time.
    - Individual accountability.
    - Opportunity for success.
    - Reflection afterward.

- The instructor becomes more of a coach or facilitator and keeps the group moving toward the objective.

#### Demonstration-Performance

- **Demonstration-performance is particularly suited to mental and physical skills and is based on learning by doing.**

It has five phases:

**Explanation → Demonstration → Learner Performance → Instructor Supervision → Evaluation**

- **Explain** what is going to happen and what correct performance looks like.

- **Demonstrate** the skill correctly.

- **Let the learner perform it.**

- **Supervise and coach** while they practice.

- **Evaluate** the result against what was taught and the applicable standard.

- **This is a natural method for most flight maneuvers.**

#### Drill and Practice

- **Drill and practice strengthens learning through repeated application.**

- It works well when repetition is actually needed to develop reliability.

- But repetition has to remain purposeful.

- **Practicing the same uncorrected error over and over simply makes the error more practiced.**

- **The teaching method determines how we deliver the material, but technology can also help support that delivery.**

### 7. E-Learning — Useful, But It Still Has an Objective

- **E-learning uses an electronic component for instruction.**

    - Interactive courses.
    - Computer-based instruction.
    - Online training.
    - Simulations.

- It can provide:

    - Flexibility.
    - Accessibility.
    - Interactivity.
    - Learner-paced study.

- **But finishing an electronic lesson does not automatically prove learning.**

- The instructor still needs to determine whether the learner actually achieved the objective.

- **The computer is a delivery method—not proof of competence.**

- **And whether instruction is electronic or face-to-face, we may use instructional aids to make the idea clearer.**

### 8. Instructional Aids and Training Technologies — Support the Teaching

- **Instructional aids support, supplement, or reinforce instruction. They are not self-supporting.**

- Examples might include:

    - Sectional charts.
    - Model airplanes.
    - Whiteboards.
    - Diagrams.
    - Cockpit photographs.
    - Videos.
    - Simulators or training devices where appropriate.

- **Good instructional aids should be:**

    - Accurate.
    - Appropriate to the objective.
    - Simple enough to understand.
    - Visible and usable by the learner.

- Use the aid because it helps communicate the idea—not because it looks impressive.

- **Test the aid before the lesson.**

    - Does the video work?
    - Can the diagram be seen?
    - Is the information technically correct?
    - Does it actually support the objective?

- **The aid supports the teaching. It does not become the teaching.**

- **So far we've been talking about ways to teach concepts and skills. Flight instruction adds one particular technique that the ACS specifically requires us to understand: integrated flight instruction.**

### 9. Integrated Flight Instruction — Outside and Instrument References Together

- **Integrated flight instruction has a specific FAA meaning.**

- It means teaching flight maneuvers using **both outside visual references and flight-instrument indications.**

- And for it to be fully effective, **instrument references should be introduced from the first time the maneuver is taught.**

- **The flight controls are operated the same way regardless of whether the learner is looking outside or referencing the instruments.**

- So while teaching something as basic as straight-and-level flight or a turn:

    - Establish the attitude primarily with outside visual references.
    - Also teach the learner to cross-check the instruments and connect instrument indications to what the airplane is doing.

- **The goal is to develop a habit of continuously monitoring both the airplane and its performance.**

- That builds:

    - Better instrument cross-check.
    - Better aircraft control.
    - Better coordination.
    - Better airspeed and altitude control.
    - A foundation for later instrument training.

- **Aviation example:** During a level turn, the learner uses the horizon to establish bank and pitch while also confirming the result with the attitude indicator, altimeter, heading information, and other appropriate instruments.

- **So integrated flight instruction does not mean simply combining everything we've learned into one flight. It specifically means integrating outside visual references with flight-instrument references from the beginning of maneuver training.**

- **Once the learner has the underlying knowledge and skills, we can move from simply following instructions toward actually solving problems. That takes us to problem-based instruction.**

### 10. Problem-Based Instruction — Make the Learner Think

- **Problem-based instruction presents real-world problems that require real-world solutions.**

- The problems should be:

    - Relevant.
    - Open-ended.
    - Connected to the learning objective.
    - Capable of requiring judgment and critical thinking.

- **Aviation example:**

    “You're 45 minutes into a VFR cross-country. The weather ahead is deteriorating faster than forecast, the destination is becoming marginal, and fuel is adequate but not unlimited. What are your options?”

- There may be **more than one acceptable answer.**

- The learner has to:

    - Gather information.
    - Identify alternatives.
    - Make a decision.
    - Explain the reasoning.

- **Then reflect on the decision.**

    - What information drove your choice?
    - What alternatives did you reject?
    - Why?
    - What would cause you to change your decision?

- **The scenario is a learning activity, not a surprise trap.**

- The instructor should know:

    - The objective.
    - The underlying knowledge required.
    - The safe range of possible outcomes.

- **Problem-based instruction shows whether knowledge and skill can actually be used. But these problems still have to fit into a deliberate progression of training. That brings us back to how the entire training program is built.**

### 11. Planning Instructional Activity — Build From One Lesson to the Course

- **The FAA describes the planning structure from the big picture down as:**

**Course of Training → Blocks of Learning → Training Syllabus → Lesson Plans**

- But when I'm building instruction, it is also useful to understand it from the bottom up.

#### Lesson Plan — One Instructional Period

- **A lesson plan is the detailed guide for one instructional period.**

- It tells us:

    - What we're teaching.
    - What the objective is.
    - How the material is organized.
    - What teaching method and aids we will use.
    - What the learner will do.
    - How successful completion will be assessed.

- **Aviation example — steep turns:**

    - **Objective:** Develop understanding and coordinated steep-turn performance.
    - **Preparation:** Review coordinated turns, load factor, clearing procedures, horizontal and vertical lift components, and applicable standards.
    - **Presentation:** Explain and demonstrate entry, sight picture, control relationships, common errors, and rollout.
    - **Application:** Learner performs the maneuver.
    - **Assessment:** Compare the performance with the lesson standard and determine what remains deficient.

- **The lesson plan is a guide—not a script that prevents instructional judgment.**

#### Blocks of Learning — Related Measurable Units

- **Individual lessons and skills contribute to larger blocks of learning.**

- Blocks are measurable units that progressively combine into the total training objective.

- Sequence them logically, generally from simpler learning toward more complex learning.

- **Aviation example:** Before independent traffic-pattern operations, the learner needs related building blocks involving:

    - Basic aircraft control.
    - Climbs and descents.
    - Turns.
    - Configuration changes.
    - Airspeed control.
    - Communications.
    - Landing fundamentals.

#### Training Syllabus — The Road Map

- **The training syllabus organizes those blocks and lessons into a step-by-step progression through the course.**

- It normally identifies:

    - Objectives.
    - Lesson content.
    - Completion standards.
    - Sequence.
    - Review and assessment points.

- **The syllabus should be structured but flexible.**

    - Weather changes.
    - Maintenance happens.
    - Learners progress at different rates.

- **Flexibility does not mean randomness.**

- Preserve the prerequisite relationships and overall progression.

#### Course of Training — The Complete Goal

- **All of those lessons, blocks, and the syllabus ultimately make up the course of training—the full series of studies leading to the final goal.**

So from the learner's immediate perspective, we can think:

**Lesson → Blocks → Syllabus → Course Goal**

while remembering that when planning the overall program, the FAA starts with the **course objective and standards** and works down toward the individual lessons.

- **And that brings us to the risk-management element of this Task: selecting the right teaching method.**

### 12. Selecting the Teaching Method — Adapt the Path, Preserve the Destination

- **Method selection should fit:**

    - The objective.
    - Learner background.
    - Desired level of learning.
    - Subject matter.
    - Training environment.

- Different objectives naturally favor different methods.

    - **Introduce Class B airspace:** lecture or discussion with visual aids.
    - **Develop ADM:** guided discussion or problem-based instruction.
    - **Teach steep turns:** demonstration-performance.
    - **Improve a specific landing deficiency:** drill and practice.
    - **Work through a realistic cross-country decision:** problem-based or scenario-based training.

- **If the learner is not progressing, change the instructional approach before deciding that the learner cannot learn the task.**

    - Explain it differently.
    - Draw it.
    - Demonstrate it.
    - Ask questions.
    - Break the task into pieces.
    - Change the scenario.
    - Give more appropriate practice.

- **What changes is the path to the objective.**

- **What does not change is the objective or the required standard.**

### Close

- **That brings the whole teaching process back to where we started.**

First determine:

**What does the learner need to accomplish?**

Then determine:

**What does acceptable performance look like?**

From there:

**Prepare → Present → Apply → Assess**

And within that process:

- Organize the lesson logically.

- Choose the teaching method that fits the objective and learner.

- Use technology or aids when they actually help.

- Let the learner apply the learning.

- Assess the result.

- Adjust the next instruction based on what you observe.

- **The end state is not a learner who can simply repeat what the instructor said.**

- It is a learner who has progressed through a deliberate training sequence, understands the objective, performs to the standard, and can eventually use the learning independently in actual flight.

- **Adapt the path—not the destination.**
## FI.I.C.K1 — Teaching

### FI.I.C.K1a — Process

Teaching means instructing or training. The teaching process organizes material so the learner can understand it. Its four steps are preparation, presentation, application, and assessment, regardless of the delivery method used.

### FI.I.C.K1b — Essential Skills

Essential teaching skills are people skills, subject-matter expertise, management skills, and assessment skills. Management includes planning, organizing, leading, supervising, and using time effectively. Assessment determines how well the learner is progressing before, during, and after training.

## FI.I.C.K2 — Course of Training

A course of training is a complete series of studies leading to a specific goal. A curriculum includes courses for certificates or ratings. A syllabus summarizes one course and contains multiple lessons, objectives, and completion standards. A lesson plan details how one instructional period is conducted.

## FI.I.C.K3 — Preparation of a Lesson

### FI.I.C.K3a — Training Objectives and Completion Standards

Objectives identify the desired level of learning in the cognitive, affective, and psychomotor domains. Standards describe the desired knowledge, behavior, or skill in specific terms, including conditions and criteria. Clearly defined objectives and standards that the learner understands are essential to teaching.

### FI.I.C.K3b — Performance-Based Objectives

A performance-based objective states a measurable and repeatable desired performance. It has three parts: the description of the skill or behavior, the conditions for the performance, and the criteria used to measure accomplishment.

[[highlight:

### FI.I.C.K3c — Importance of the ACS in Aviation Training Curricula

The ACS supplies specific performance objectives based on standards for issuance of a certificate or rating. It supports content validity by matching the task to actual flying and criterion validity by using acceptable completion standards. ACS tasks are critical job tasks; they do not represent an entire training syllabus.

### FI.I.C.K3d — Decision-Based Objectives

Decision-based objectives develop judgment and ADM through dynamic, meaningful scenarios. They require the learner to gather information and make informed, safe, and timely decisions. They supplement, rather than eliminate, concentrated maneuver training.

## FI.I.C.K4 — Organization of Material

A lesson traditionally has introduction, development, and conclusion. The introduction gains attention, provides motivation, and gives an overview. Development presents main points in a logical pattern such as simple to complex, known to unknown, or most frequently used to least used. The conclusion reviews key elements and relates them to the objective without introducing new ideas.

## FI.I.C.K5 — Training Delivery Methods

The instructor normally combines methods and selects the one that best conveys the information at the appropriate time.

### FI.I.C.K5a — Lecture

A lecture gives a general understanding of a subject, introduces new material, summarizes ideas, and relates theory to practice. A teaching lecture permits some learner participation and requires the instructor to recognize subtle learner feedback.

### FI.I.C.K5b — Discussion

Discussion combines a short lecture with instructor-learner and learner-learner exchange. It promotes active participation, recall, use of information, and higher-order thinking. The instructor keeps the discussion on subject and summarizes learning.

### FI.I.C.K5c — Guided Discussion

Guided discussion draws out learner knowledge using questions. Learners need background knowledge, the instructor maintains control, and open-ended questions guide learners toward the lesson objective.

### FI.I.C.K5d — Cooperative or Group Learning

Group learning organizes small groups to maximize understanding. It requires clear objectives, complete instructions, access to needed information, sufficient time, individual accountability, and instructor facilitation.

### FI.I.C.K5e — Demonstration-Performance

Demonstration-performance teaches mental or physical skills through explanation, demonstration, learner performance, instructor supervision, and evaluation. Learners perform soon after demonstration while the instructor coaches and evaluates performance against how the skill was taught.

## FI.I.C.K6 — Electronic Learning (e-Learning)

E-learning uses an electronic component and may be stand-alone software, an interactive web course, simulation, or computer-assisted learning. ## FI.I.C.K7 — Instructional Aids and Training Technologies

### FI.I.C.K7a — Characteristics of Effective Instructional Aids

Instructional aids support, supplement, or reinforce instruction. Effective aids cover key points, present straightforward factual material, are simple, and are matched to the desired learning outcome.

### FI.I.C.K7b — Reasons for Use

Aids gain and hold attention, support retention, clarify relationships between objects and concepts, address some language barriers, and can communicate information more quickly and accurately.

### FI.I.C.K7c — Guidelines for Use

Establish the objective, research supporting material, organize the lesson, and select only the key ideas that need aid support. Keep words to a minimum, ensure the aid can be seen or heard, test it in the actual environment, and verify technical accuracy, clarity, simplicity, and suitability for the learner.

### FI.I.C.K7d — Types

Aids include marker boards, printed charts, diagrams, graphs, projected material, video, interactive computer systems, computer-assisted learning, models, mock-ups, and cut-aways.

## FI.I.C.K9 — Problem-Based Instruction

Problem-based learning presents real-life problems that require real-world solutions. Effective problems are open-ended, require decisions, connect previous and new knowledge, reflect objectives, and challenge critical thinking. The basic approach is to set up the problem, determine outcomes, solve it, reflect, consider additional solutions, reevaluate, and decide what makes a solution best.

## FI.I.C.K10 — Planning Instructional Activity

### FI.I.C.K10a — Blocks of Learning

Blocks of learning are measurable units that form necessary parts of the total objective. They are not isolated subjects or merely a sequence of instruction periods. Learners master blocks individually and progressively combine related blocks; the sequence should build from simple to complex and account for learner background.

### FI.I.C.K10b — Training Syllabus

A syllabus is a brief but comprehensive course summary that sequences blocks of learning efficiently and identifies objectives and standards for each lesson. It is a practical guide, flexible enough to accommodate learner progress, scheduling, weather, and aircraft availability without ignoring relationships between learning blocks.

### FI.I.C.K10c — Lesson Plans

A lesson plan is a written outline for one instructional period. It identifies what to do, the order, and the teaching procedure. It includes objectives, content supporting the objectives, and completion standards. It guides sequence, helps eliminate unimportant detail, promotes uniform instruction, relates the lesson to the course objective, and should be revised as conditions change.

### Selection of Teaching Method

Method selection must fit the objective, learner background, desired level of learning, material, and training environment. The instructor uses the method that best supports understanding and performance, changes the approach when learner progress shows it is needed, and continues to apply objectives and standards.
