# Task A. Effects of Human Behavior and Communication on the Learning Process

**Area I — Fundamentals of Instructing**

## Objective

To determine the applicant understands human behavior and effective communication, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate appropriate skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Human behavior affects learning. The instructor observes learner needs, motivation, emotional reactions, prior experience, and communication responses to organize productive instruction. Communication is effective only when the learner understands the idea, concept, or action as the instructor intended.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested Schedule**

- Ground lesson: approximately 45 minutes
- Discussion and teach-back: approximately 15 minutes

**Equipment**

- Aviation Instructor's Handbook
- CFI Airplane ACS
- Whiteboard or tablet
- Flight Guide visual aids

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner Actions**

- Explain how human behavior and motivation affect instruction.
- Recognize learner emotional reactions and communication barriers.
- Describe appropriate instructor actions for defense mechanisms and serious abnormal reactions.
- Demonstrate clear communication in a ground or flight lesson.

**Instructor Actions**

- Use observable learner behavior to adjust the presentation.
- Establish objectives, standards, and a respectful learning climate.
- Check understanding through learner feedback and performance.
- Preserve safety and withhold endorsements when competence cannot be established.

## Completion Standards

The applicant can explain the human-behavior and communication elements in this Task, recognize learning conditions that require an instructional adjustment, and use communication appropriate to the learner, subject, and training environment.

**There are two main concepts in this lesson.** First, we need to **understand the learner**—why people behave as they do, what motivates them, what needs or reactions may interfere with learning, and how adults approach learning. Second, we need to **communicate effectively**—making sure the idea we intend to convey is actually received, understood, and usable by the learner.

**The governing principle throughout the lesson is simple: adapt the instruction, not the standard.**

### 1. Human Behavior — Understand the Learner

- **The reason we study human behavior is instructional.** We want to understand why people act the way they do because behavior directly affects learning.
- **Behavior comes from innate human nature, individual experience, environment, and attempts to satisfy needs.**
- **What we observe is the behavior; we do not automatically know its cause.**
    - A quiet learner could be concentrating, confused, anxious, tired, or uninterested.
- **So observe first and interpret cautiously.** Ask questions, compare the behavior with prior performance, and adjust the instruction when appropriate.
- **We are not trying to diagnose the learner.** We are trying to understand enough about what may be affecting learning to respond instructionally.
- **The instructor–learner relationship matters here.** Our teaching style and the learner's preferred way of learning may differ. We bridge that difference by understanding the learner's background, motivation, and experience and by establishing clear objectives and standards.
- **Whatever adjustment we make, we do not change the standard.** We may change the explanation, pace, practice, or scenario—but not what competent performance means.
- **So the question becomes: what causes the learner to put effort toward a goal in the first place? That is motivation.**

### 2. Motivation — Why the Learner Engages

- **Motivation is the reason a person acts toward a goal.**
- One of our jobs as instructors is to understand **why this learner came to aviation** and connect training back to that purpose.
    - Airlines
    - Family travel
    - Recreation
    - Career advancement
    - Personal achievement
- **Positive motivation generally supports learning better than fear-based negative motivation.**
- We maintain positive motivation by:
    - Showing the learner genuine progress.
    - Recognizing achievement.
    - Connecting the lesson to their goal.
    - Giving them the next achievable challenge.
- **Negative motivation can increase anxiety and interfere with learning.**
    - Poor: “If you keep flying like this, you'll never solo.”
    - Better: “We're not at solo consistency yet. Airspeed on final is the specific thing we need to improve.”
- **Motivation may change over time, and learning plateaus are normal.**
- A plateau is a temporary lack of visible progress. **It does not necessarily mean the learner has stopped learning or lost motivation.**
- **But motivation alone is not enough. A learner may desperately want to fly and still be unable to focus because another need is consuming their attention. That takes us to human needs.**

### 3. Human Needs — Is the Learner Ready to Learn?

- **Human needs affect how much attention the learner has available for learning.**
- Maslow gives us a useful progression:
    - **Physiological:** Hunger, thirst, illness, fatigue, discomfort, airsickness.
        - If you are actively airsick, you are probably not learning much.
    - **Security:** The learner needs to feel reasonably safe rather than consumed by fear.
        - A learner terrified of stalls may have very little attention left for understanding the maneuver.
    - **Belonging:** The learner needs to feel accepted and part of the learning environment.
    - **Esteem:** The learner needs dignity, confidence, and a sense that progress is possible.
    - **Cognitive and aesthetic needs:** The desire to know, understand, make sense of things, and appreciate order or structure.
    - **Self-actualization:** The desire to reach one's potential.
- **So before treating poor performance as a knowledge or skill problem, ask whether the learner is actually ready to learn right now.**
- **And when needs, frustration, or anxiety threaten the learner's self-image, the learner may unconsciously protect themselves rather than confront the problem directly. Those are defense mechanisms.**

### 4. Defense Mechanisms — Psychological Self-Protection

- **Defense mechanisms are unconscious responses to anxiety, frustration, or unpleasant emotion.**
- They protect the learner psychologically, but they can also **distort reality and hide the actual learning problem.**
**DR DR F CPR**
- **Denial:** Refusing to accept an unpleasant reality.
    - “My altitude control really wasn't a problem.”
- **Repression:** Unconsciously excluding a distressing experience from awareness.
    - A frightening training event may become difficult for the learner to recall or discuss.
- **Displacement:** Redirecting emotion toward another target.
    - A bad day outside aviation becomes anger toward the instructor or airplane.
- **Rationalization:** Creating a plausible explanation that avoids the real cause.
    - “I couldn't hold altitude because it was turbulent,” when the larger problem was an incomplete scan.
- **Fantasy:** Imagining future accomplishment instead of confronting today's deficiency.
    - Talking about airline flying while avoiding work on basic aircraft control.
- **Compensation:** Emphasizing a strength to offset a weakness.
    - Redirecting every discussion of poor landings toward excellent systems knowledge.
- **Projection:** Attributing your own shortcomings to somebody else.
    - An unprepared learner claims the instructor never explained the assignment.
- **Reaction formation:** Displaying an attitude opposite the underlying feeling.
    - A learner frightened by stalls dismisses them as easy, boring, or pointless.
- **Our job is not to win an argument over which defense mechanism the learner is using.** Bring the discussion back to observable performance, objective standards, and the next correctable action.
- **Serious concerns require professional help, not amateur psychology.**
- **Defense mechanisms are one possible response to stress. Stress can also directly change the learner's ability to perceive, decide, and perform. That takes us to emotional and physical reactions.**

### 5. Emotional and Physical Reactions — When Learning Capacity Changes

- **Anxiety and stress can narrow perception and interfere with rational action.**
    - The learner may freeze, delay action, or act without thinking.
- Before a first stall, explain what will happen, what it will feel like, and how recovery works before exposing the learner to it.
- **Impatience causes learners to undervalue prerequisites.**
    - “Why do I need to keep doing traffic patterns? I want to start cross-countries.”
    - Give each step a clear goal and explain how it supports the final objective.
- **Worry or lack of interest competes for attention.**
    - If the learner is worried about a checkride, home, work, or performance, narrow the lesson to an achievable objective and keep them accurately informed of progress.
- **Physical discomfort, illness, fatigue, dehydration, and airsickness can look like a learning problem.**
    - A normally capable learner who suddenly begins missing calls and configuration changes late in a hot, turbulent flight may need hydration or an end to the lesson—not another ten repetitions.
- **Apathy can actually be caused by inadequate instruction.**
    - Poor preparation.
    - Contradictory instruction.
    - Unnecessary repetition.
    - Material at the wrong level.
    - Talking down to the learner.
- **Sometimes the instructor is the problem, and we need to change the presentation.**
- **Serious abnormal reactions create a safety boundary.**
    - Inappropriate reactions.
    - Marked mood changes.
    - Severe anger.
    - We do not diagnose. When justified, **refrain from instruction, obtain an independent instructor evaluation, and withhold solo or practical-test endorsements.**
- **Once the learner is physically and emotionally ready, we can take advantage of something most aviation learners bring with them: adult experience, goals, and a practical reason for learning.**

### 6. Adult Learners — Make It Relevant

- **Most aviation learners are adults, and adults usually have an identifiable use for what they are learning.**
- Adults tend to be:
    - Self-directed.
    - Experience based.
    - Goal oriented.
    - Relevance oriented.
    - Practical.
    - Problem centered.
    - Conscious of respect.
- **Know why they are here and connect instruction to that goal.**
    - If the learner wants to fly the family to the beach, make weight and balance a realistic family cross-country instead of only an abstract calculation.
- **Use prior experience when it helps.**
- But recognize that experience can also cause **negative transfer** when an old response does not fit the new task.
    - A learner may bring an automobile steering habit into aircraft control and need to learn a different relationship among aileron, rudder, and drift.
- **Give adults appropriate ownership over the learning process without allowing them to negotiate away the standard.**
- **At this point we may understand the learner extremely well and have a perfectly designed lesson—but none of it matters if the idea in our head is not the same idea the learner receives. That takes us to effective communication.**

### 7. Effective Communication — Shared Meaning

- **Communication is successful when the learner understands the intended idea—not simply when the instructor says the words.**
**SOURCE → SYMBOLS → RECEIVER**
- **Source:** The instructor has the idea.
- **Symbols:** We represent the idea through:
    - Words
    - Diagrams
    - Gestures
    - Demonstrations
    - Sounds
    - Tactile cues
- **Receiver:** The learner interprets those symbols through their own experience, knowledge, attitude, and present condition.
- **A symbol is not the idea itself.**
    - A learner may be able to repeat “trim off the control pressure” without actually understanding what control pressure feels like.
- **A nod or “I understand” is weak evidence of communication.**
- Stronger evidence is when the learner can:
    - Explain it.
    - Demonstrate it.
    - Apply it.
- **So when the learner does not understand, the question is not simply, “How do I say it again?” The better question is, “Where did the meaning break down?” That takes us to communication barriers.**

### 8. Communication Barriers — Where Meaning Gets Lost

- **COIL is our memory aid for where communication can break down:**
**C — Confusion between symbol and object**
    - The learner knows the word or phrase without understanding what it represents.
    - **Example:** The learner repeats “pitch for airspeed” without understanding what aircraft response the phrase represents.
    - Connect the words to demonstration, sensation, instruments, and actual aircraft response.
**O — Overuse of abstractions**
    - The language is too broad or vague.
    - “Manage your energy” may mean very little to a new learner.
    - Make it concrete:
        - “You're 10 knots fast.”
        - “You're 100 feet high.”
        - “What can we change to correct that?”
**I — Interference**
    - Something disrupts, distorts, truncates, or changes the message between source and receiver.
    - It may be:
        - **Physiological:** fatigue, discomfort, hearing difficulty, divided attention.
        - **Environmental:** noise, turbulence, vibration, temperature, interrupted intercom.
        - **Psychological:** fear, defensiveness, mistrust, preoccupation.
    - **Example:** During a noisy climb the instructor says “turn crosswind,” but the overloaded learner hears “turn downwind.”
    - Shorten the message, reduce the interference when possible, and verify the result.
**L — Lack of common experience**
    - The instructor and learner use the same words but attach different meanings to them.
    - **Example:** “Fly a normal pattern” may mean something very different to someone trained at a quiet grass strip than it does at a busy Class D airport.
    - Create a common reference through explanation or demonstration.
- **Once we reduce the barriers, we still do not know whether communication succeeded until we hear and observe what the learner actually understood. That means listening, questioning, and feedback.**

### 9. Listening, Questions, and Feedback — Close the Loop

- **Listening means hearing with comprehension—not simply waiting for your turn to speak.**
- Listen for:
    - What the learner says.
    - What they are uncertain about.
    - What assumptions they are making.
    - What relationships they think exist.
- **Good questions expose thinking.**
    - “What are you seeing on this approach?”
    - “What changed first?”
    - “Why did that correction work?”
    - “How would this change with a shorter runway and a tailwind?”
- **Weak questions create the appearance of understanding without proving it.**
    - “Do you understand?”
    - “Right?”
    - “You know what I mean?”
- **Performance is often our strongest feedback.**
    - Have the learner explain.
    - Demonstrate.
    - Apply.
- **Communication is therefore a loop:** send the idea, observe the response, determine what meaning the learner actually received, and adjust the instruction from that evidence.
- **We also develop our own communication ability through practice.** Role-playing, actual instruction, additional training, active listening, and observing learner performance all help the instructor become a better communicator.
- **When we combine that communication loop with what we know about human behavior, the whole Task becomes one practical instructional process.**

### 10. Put It Together

- **Understand the learner.**
    - Why are they here?
    - What motivates them?
    - Are needs, emotions, physical condition, or previous experience affecting learning?
- **Adapt the instruction.**
    - Change the explanation.
    - Change the pace.
    - Demonstrate it.
    - Change the scenario.
    - Give additional or different practice.
- **Do not compromise the standard.**
    - We change **how we get there**, not what competent performance means.
- **Communicate concretely.**
    - Use language and examples the learner understands.
    - Avoid unnecessary abstractions.
    - Look for interference and missing common experience.
- **Verify what the learner actually understood.**
    - Listen.
    - Question.
    - Have them explain.
    - Have them demonstrate.
    - Observe them apply it.
- **And that ties the entire lesson together:** understand why the learner may be behaving as they are, determine what is helping or interfering with learning, adapt the instruction, communicate the idea in a way the learner can meaningfully receive, and verify that understanding through performance.
- **The instructor may teach two learners very differently and still require exactly the same outcome.**
- **What changes is the path to competence—not the definition of competence.**
## FI.I.A.K1 — Elements of Human Behavior

### FI.I.A.K1a — Definitions of Human Behavior

Human behavior is explained as the product of innate nature, individual experience, and environment. It can also be understood as attempts to satisfy needs. Observing behavior helps the instructor understand the learner and organize instruction that supports learning.

]]

### FI.I.A.K1b — Instructor and Learner Relationship

An instructor's teaching style and a learner's preferred way of learning may match or differ. The instructor cannot simply change a preferred style, but can bridge differences by observing the learner, identifying motivation and background, and adapting the lesson scenario or presentation. Clear lesson objectives and completion standards establish reasonable goals and prevent uncertainty about progress.

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=35 | end=35]]

### FI.I.A.K1c — Motivation

Motivation is the reason a person acts toward a goal. It strongly influences effort, progress, and understanding. Positive motivation is generally more effective for learning than fear-based motivation. An instructor identifies why the learner entered training, relates training objectives to that purpose, recognizes incremental success, presents the next challenge, and explains that learning plateaus are normal.

]]

Human needs affect the learner's ability to focus on instruction. The handbook discusses physiological needs, security, belonging, esteem, cognitive and aesthetic needs, and self-actualization. The instructor verifies that the learner is physically and mentally ready, emphasizes safety, and creates a learning environment in which attention can remain on the task.

### FI.I.A.K1e — Defense Mechanisms

- **Purpose:** Psychological defense mechanisms are unconscious processes used to protect a person from anxiety, frustration, or unpleasant emotion.
- **Instructional effect:** A defense mechanism can distort reality, change perception, and hide the actual learning problem from both learner and instructor.
- **Common defense mechanisms:**
    - **Denial:** Refusing to accept an unpleasant reality. A learner insists a repeated altitude deviation “was not really a problem” despite objective evidence.
    - **Repression:** Unconsciously excluding distressing thoughts or experiences from awareness. A learner may be unable to recall details surrounding a particularly frightening training event.
    - **Displacement:** Redirecting emotion from its original source toward a safer target. A learner frustrated by personal problems may become angry with the instructor or aircraft.
    - **Rationalization:** Creating a plausible explanation that avoids the actual cause. A learner blames turbulence for poor altitude control when the larger problem was an incomplete instrument scan.
    - **Fantasy:** Escaping into imagined success rather than addressing present performance. A learner talks confidently about future airline flying while avoiding needed work on basic aircraft control.
    - **Compensation:** Emphasizing strength in one area to offset weakness in another. A learner highlights excellent systems knowledge whenever landing deficiencies are discussed.
    - **Projection:** Attributing one's own unacceptable thoughts or shortcomings to someone else. A learner who arrived unprepared claims the instructor did not explain the assignment.
    - **Reaction formation:** Displaying an attitude opposite to the underlying feeling. A learner who is frightened by stalls acts dismissive and insists the maneuver is boring or unnecessary.
- **Instructor response:** Recognize the observable behavior, discuss the actual performance problem with common sense and objective standards, and restore motivation and self-confidence without arguing over a psychological label.
- **Escalation:** Severe concerns need timely, skillful professional help rather than improvised psychological treatment.

## FI.I.A.K2 — Learner Emotional Reactions

### FI.I.A.K2a — Anxiety and Stress

- **Effect on learning:** Anxiety is worry or unease about a real or imagined threat. It can narrow perception, delay action, cause the learner to freeze, or produce action without rational thought.
- **Handbook response:** Treat fear as a normal reaction rather than ignoring it. Introduce unfamiliar maneuvers carefully by explaining the governing principles, expected sensations, and recovery before exposure.
- **Aviation example:** Before the learner's first stall, review angle of attack and stall characteristics, describe the buffet and control changes, explain the recovery, and then demonstrate progressively. Predictability reduces threat and leaves more attention available for learning.
- **Instructor emphasis:** Reinforce the benefits and satisfaction of safe flight rather than relying on repeated warnings about catastrophic consequences.

### FI.I.A.K2b — Impatience

- **Effect on learning:** The impatient learner focuses on the ultimate objective and undervalues the preliminary knowledge or skill needed to reach it safely.
- **Handbook response:** Give each training step a clearly stated goal, identify what that step contributes to the final objective, and advance the learner when the goal is attained rather than requiring unnecessary repetition.
- **Aviation example:** A presolo learner wants to begin cross-country training before consistently controlling the traffic pattern. Tie each pattern standard—airspeed, spacing, configuration, and stabilized approach—to the workload the learner must manage away from the local airport.

### FI.I.A.K2c — Worry or Lack of Interest

- **Effect on learning:** Outside worry, dissatisfaction, or lack of interest competes for attention and reduces the learner's ability to engage with the lesson.
- **Handbook response:** Do not ignore the influence. Use clear objectives, provide an accurate account of progress and deficiencies after each lesson, and connect the task to the learner's interests and goals.
- **Aviation example:** A learner distracted by an upcoming checkride may rush maneuvers without processing feedback. Narrow the lesson to one attainable objective, identify the specific standard being improved, and show how that progress supports checkride readiness.

### FI.I.A.K2d — Physical Discomfort, Illness, Fatigue, and Dehydration

- **Effect on learning:** Discomfort, illness, fatigue, and dehydration reduce learning rate and can impair judgment, timing, coordination, and receptiveness.
- **Observable cues:** Inattention, distractibility, timing errors, declining accuracy, neglected secondary tasks, and irritability.
- **Handbook response:** Continue only while the learner remains alert, receptive, and reasonably consistent with prior experience. Adjust or discontinue training when physical condition—not lack of knowledge—is driving performance.
- **Aviation example:** After two hours in heat and turbulence, a normally consistent learner begins missing radio calls and configuration changes. A break, hydration, or ending the lesson addresses the likely cause more effectively than repeating the maneuver.
- **Airsickness:** Terminate an instructional flight when incipient airsickness occurs rather than allowing the experience to intensify and create a lasting negative association.

### FI.I.A.K2e — Apathy Due to Inadequate Instruction

- **Instructor-caused problem:** Poor preparation, contradictory or insincere instruction, misplaced emphasis, unnecessary repetition, or talking down to the learner can destroy confidence and attention.
- **Learner reaction:** Apparent apathy may be a response to instruction that feels irrelevant, confusing, repetitive, or disrespectful rather than evidence that the learner lacks motivation.
- **Handbook response:** Use well-planned, accurate instruction matched to the learner's level; clarify the purpose of the task and make progress visible.
- **Aviation example:** A learner disengages after several lessons of unexplained maneuver repetition. Define the performance discrepancy, explain why the next exercise addresses it, and stop repeating the task once the objective is met.

### Serious Abnormal Emotional Behavior

Abnormal reactions under stress can include inappropriate reactions, marked mood changes, and severe anger. These signs are reasons for careful evaluation, not diagnosis. When a serious psychological deficiency may be present, the instructor should refrain from instructing, arrange an independent evaluation by another instructor, and withhold solo or practical-test endorsements when justified.

## FI.I.A.K3 — Teaching the Adult Learner

- **Knowledge—how adults approach learning:**
    - **Use-oriented:** Adults usually seek training because they have a practical use for the knowledge or skill; learning is a means to an end.
    - **Autonomous and self-directed:** They expect some control over pace, planning, and progress.
    - **Experience-based:** They bring prior knowledge, life experience, and decision-making patterns that can support—or interfere with—new aviation learning.
    - **Goal and relevance oriented:** They want to understand why a task matters and apply it promptly.
    - **Practical and problem centered:** They respond well when instruction solves an operational problem rather than only presenting information.
    - **Respect conscious:** Adult learners expect their experience and individuality to be treated respectfully.
- **Skill—instructor application:**
    - **Define objectives and expectations:** Use a syllabus and clear completion standards so the learner can see the route to the goal.
    - **Connect prior experience:** Link useful experience to the new concept while identifying habits that may create negative transfer.
    - **Share appropriate planning:** Invite learner input on pace, scenarios, and areas needing practice while preserving required standards.
    - **Make relevance immediate:** Explain how each task supports the learner's stated aviation goal.
    - **Use scenario-based problems:** Give the learner opportunities to apply knowledge and make decisions rather than only receive explanations.
    - **Aviation example:** An experienced business owner learning cross-country planning can help choose a realistic destination and evaluate options, while the instructor ensures weather, performance, fuel, and risk objectives are met.

## FI.I.A.K4 — Effective Communication

### FI.I.A.K4a — Basic Elements of Communication

- **Source:** The instructor, learner, or other sender selects the idea and must use meaningful, current, accurate information. Feedback allows the source to adapt the presentation.
- **Symbols:** Words, gestures, diagrams, demonstrations, sounds, and tactile cues represent the idea. A symbol has value only when the receiver assigns the intended meaning.
- **Receiver:** The learner interprets the symbols through prior experience, knowledge, attitudes, and current conditions.
- **Successful communication:** The receiver understands the intended idea and responds in a way that shows the message changed knowledge, understanding, or behavior as intended.
- **Aviation example:** The instructor explains coordinated flight, points to the inclinometer, and demonstrates rudder pressure. The learner then explains the relationship and centers the ball during a turn; that response confirms the message was understood.

[[mnemonic:
SOURCE → SYMBOLS → RECEIVER |
The source encodes an idea in meaningful symbols; the receiver interprets them and provides feedback that confirms or corrects understanding.
]]

### FI.I.A.K4b — Barriers to Effective Communication

- **The FAA identifies four barriers:**
    - **Lack of common experience:** Source and receiver assign different meanings because they do not share the background needed to interpret the symbols alike.
        - **Example:** “Fly a standard pattern” may mean something different to a learner trained at a nontowered grass strip than to an instructor expecting a specific paved-airport pattern and radio sequence.
        - **Instructor response:** Identify the learner's background, define the expected behavior, and create a shared reference through demonstration or example.
    - **Confusion between symbol and symbolized object:** The word, diagram, or gesture is mistaken for the actual concept or experience it represents.
        - **Example:** A learner can recite “pitch for airspeed” but applies it mechanically without understanding the aircraft's energy state or maneuver context.
        - **Instructor response:** Connect the symbol to demonstration, sensation, and observed aircraft response.
    - **Overuse of abstractions:** General or technical language has no concrete meaning for the learner.
        - **Example:** Telling a new learner to “manage energy” is too abstract unless it is tied to airspeed, altitude, configuration, drag, and the resulting flightpath.
        - **Instructor response:** Move from a specific, observable example to the broader principle.
    - **External factors:** Physiological, environmental, or psychological conditions interfere with reception or interpretation.
        - **Physiological:** Hearing loss, illness, discomfort, fatigue, injury, or divided attention.
        - **Environmental:** Aircraft noise, vibration, turbulence, temperature, poor lighting, or an interrupted intercom.
        - **Psychological:** Fear, mistrust, defensiveness, preoccupation, or lack of commitment.
        - **Example:** During a noisy climb, the learner hears “turn crosswind” as “turn downwind” while also managing configuration.
        - **Instructor response:** Reduce the interference, shorten and prioritize the message, then confirm understanding through feedback and observed effect.
- **Interference:** After the four named barriers, the handbook separately addresses a message that is disrupted, truncated, distorted, or added to somewhere in the communication sequence.
    - **False assumption:** The instructor and learner may each believe an intact message was sent and received when it was not.
    - **Instructor response:** Observe the effect of the message, obtain feedback, and confirm the intended meaning.

### FI.I.A.K4c — Developing Communication Skills

- **Communication develops through use:** Experience begins with role-playing during instructor training, continues through actual instruction, and improves with feedback and additional training.
- **Subject knowledge supports clarity:** Accurate knowledge lets the instructor select essential information, explain it in more than one way, and use examples without allowing them to obscure the point.
- **Listening is hearing with comprehension:**
    - **Attend fully:** Stop preparing the next response and listen for the learner's words, uncertainty, attitude, and assumptions.
    - **Account for anxiety:** Fear or stress can prevent the learner from receiving, organizing, or recalling the message even when hearing is normal.
    - **Check meaning:** Restate or summarize the learner's point before correcting it to ensure both parties are addressing the same idea.
    - **Use performance as feedback:** Agreement or silence does not prove understanding; explanation, demonstration, and application do.
- **Questions develop and verify understanding:**
    - **Open questions:** “What cues tell you this approach is becoming unstable?” reveals the learner's thought process.
    - **Probing questions:** “What changed first?” or “Why did that correction work?” develops relationships rather than rote answers.
    - **Application questions:** “How would this change with a shorter runway and a tailwind?” checks transfer.
    - **Avoid weak questioning:** Questions that are vague, overly complex, leading, or answerable only with “yes” do not reliably expose understanding.
- **Role-playing develops instructional communication:**
    - **Instructor role:** Practice explaining, questioning, listening, and correcting while another person portrays a learner with a defined background or misconception.
    - **Learner role:** Experiencing the lesson from the receiver's position reveals jargon, missing context, and excessive workload in the presentation.
    - **Observer feedback:** A third participant identifies where the message became unclear and whether feedback actually verified understanding.
    - **Aviation example:** Role-play a learner who repeatedly forces an unstable approach. The instructor must uncover whether the cause is misunderstood criteria, reluctance to go around, or weak maneuver skill before choosing the correction.

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. How does human behavior affect instruction?

[[answer:
Human behavior reflects innate nature, experience, environment, needs, motivation, and emotion. The instructor observes it to adapt instruction and maintain productive learning.
]]

### 2. How should an instructor maintain motivation?

[[answer:
Identify training goals, recognize incremental success, present new challenges, remind the learner of stated goals, and explain that learning plateaus are normal.
]]

### 3. What should an instructor do when a learner shows a defense mechanism?

[[answer:
Recognize the behavior, address the actual learning problem with common sense, and work to restore motivation and self-confidence. Serious concerns need professional help.
]]

### 4. How should an instructor respond to serious abnormal behavior?

[[answer:
Refrain from instructing when justified, arrange an independent instructor evaluation, and withhold solo or practical-test endorsements when a serious deficiency is substantiated.
]]

### 5. What are the three elements of communication?

[[answer:
Source, symbols, and receiver. Communication is effective when the receiver understands the message as intended.
]]

### 6. What are the four communication barriers?

[[answer:
Lack of common experience, confusion between symbol and object, overuse of abstractions, and external factors.
]]

### 7. How does an instructor develop communication skill?

[[answer:
Through role playing, actual instruction, additional training, active listening, feedback, and observing learner performance.
]]
