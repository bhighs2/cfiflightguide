# Task A. Effects of Human Behavior and Communication on the Learning Process

**Area I — Fundamentals of Instructing**

## Objective

To determine the applicant understands human behavior and effective communication, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate appropriate skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Human behavior affects learning. The instructor observes learner needs, motivation, emotional reactions, prior experience, and communication responses to organize productive instruction. Communication is effective only when the learner understands the idea, concept, or action as the instructor intended.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested Schedule**

- Ground lesson: approximately 45 minutes
- Discussion and teach-back: approximately 15 minutes

**Equipment**

- Aviation Instructor's Handbook
- CFI Airplane ACS
- Whiteboard or tablet
- Flight Guide visual aids

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner Actions**

- Explain how human behavior and motivation affect instruction.
- Recognize learner emotional reactions and communication barriers.
- Describe appropriate instructor actions for defense mechanisms and serious abnormal reactions.
- Demonstrate clear communication in a ground or flight lesson.

**Instructor Actions**

- Use observable learner behavior to adjust the presentation.
- Establish objectives, standards, and a respectful learning climate.
- Check understanding through learner feedback and performance.
- Preserve safety and withhold endorsements when competence cannot be established.

## Completion Standards

The applicant can explain the human-behavior and communication elements in this Task, recognize learning conditions that require an instructional adjustment, and use communication appropriate to the learner, subject, and training environment.

# Teach

## FI.I.A.K1 — Elements of Human Behavior

### FI.I.A.K1a — Definitions of Human Behavior

Human behavior is explained as the product of innate nature, individual experience, and environment. It can also be understood as attempts to satisfy needs. Observing behavior helps the instructor understand the learner and organize instruction that supports learning.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
Observe what the learner does and how it affects learning. Adapt the explanation, practice, or pace while preserving the required standard.
]]

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=34 | end=35]]

### FI.I.A.K1b — Instructor and Learner Relationship

An instructor's teaching style and a learner's preferred way of learning may match or differ. The instructor cannot simply change a preferred style, but can bridge differences by observing the learner, identifying motivation and background, and adapting the lesson scenario or presentation. Clear lesson objectives and completion standards establish reasonable goals and prevent uncertainty about progress.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=35 | end=35]]

### FI.I.A.K1c — Motivation

Motivation is the reason a person acts toward a goal. It strongly influences effort, progress, and understanding. Positive motivation is generally more effective for learning than fear-based motivation. An instructor identifies why the learner entered training, relates training objectives to that purpose, recognizes incremental success, presents the next challenge, and explains that learning plateaus are normal.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
Connect each lesson to a learner's stated goal, then make the next achievable challenge clear. This maintains relevance without reducing the standard.
]]

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=35 | end=38]]

### FI.I.A.K1d — Human Needs

Human needs affect the learner's ability to focus on instruction. The handbook discusses physiological needs, security, belonging, esteem, cognitive and aesthetic needs, and self-actualization. The instructor verifies that the learner is physically and mentally ready, emphasizes safety, and creates a learning environment in which attention can remain on the task.

### FI.I.A.K1e — Defense Mechanisms

Psychological defense mechanisms are unconscious processes used to protect a person from anxiety or unpleasant emotion. They can distort reality and change perception. The handbook describes repression, denial, compensation, projection, rationalization, reaction formation, fantasy, and displacement.

An instructor recognizes the behavior, uses common sense to discuss the actual learning problem, and works to restore motivation and self-confidence. Severe concerns need timely, skillful professional help rather than improvised psychological treatment.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
Address the observable performance problem rather than arguing about a defense mechanism. Use objective standards, then identify the next learning action.

## FI.I.A.K2 — Learner Emotional Reactions

### FI.I.A.K2a — Anxiety and Stress

Anxiety is worry or unease about a real or imagined threat and can affect actions and the ability to learn from perceptions. Learners may hesitate, freeze, or act without rational thought. Treat fears as a normal reaction, explain what to expect, and introduce unfamiliar maneuvers carefully with the relevant principles, sensations, and recovery procedures.

### FI.I.A.K2b — Impatience

The impatient learner seeks the ultimate objective without mastering the preliminary training. Correct this by presenting each step with clearly stated goals, identifying the elements mastered in each step, and advancing the learner when the goal is attained rather than requiring unnecessary repetition.

### FI.I.A.K2c — Worry or Lack of Interest

Worry, outside concerns, or dissatisfaction with training reduce learning. The instructor cannot ignore these influences. Prevention includes clear objectives, an accurate account of progress and deficiencies after each lesson, and training that connects learner interests to the task.

### FI.I.A.K2d — Physical Discomfort, Illness, Fatigue, and Dehydration

Discomfort, illness, fatigue, and dehydration reduce the rate of learning and can impair performance and judgment. The instructor observes for inattention, distractibility, timing errors, declining accuracy, neglect of secondary tasks, and irritability. Instruction continues only while the learner is alert, receptive, and performing consistently with experience; an instructional flight is terminated when incipient airsickness occurs.

### FI.I.A.K2e — Apathy Due to Inadequate Instruction

Poor preparation, contradictory or insincere instruction, misplaced emphasis, unnecessary repetition, or talking down to the learner destroy confidence and attention. Use well-planned, accurate instruction matched to the learner's level.

### Serious Abnormal Emotional Behavior

Abnormal reactions under stress can include inappropriate reactions, marked mood changes, and severe anger. These signs are reasons for careful evaluation, not diagnosis. When a serious psychological deficiency may be present, the instructor should refrain from instructing, arrange an independent evaluation by another instructor, and withhold solo or practical-test endorsements when justified.

**Source:**

## FI.I.A.K3 — Teaching the Adult Learner

Adult learners are commonly motivated by a use for the knowledge or skill. They are autonomous, goal and relevance oriented, practical, and bring life experience to learning. They want to solve problems and use new knowledge promptly, while also expecting respect.

Use clearly defined objectives and a training syllabus, connect new ideas to what the learner knows, clarify expectations early, recognize the learner's need for pace and self-direction, create opportunities for mutual planning, and give scenario-based learning opportunities.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=50 | end=51]]

## FI.I.A.K4 — Effective Communication

### FI.I.A.K4a — Basic Elements of Communication

Communication has three interrelated elements: source, symbols, and receiver. The source selects meaningful, current information and needs feedback to adapt the presentation. Symbols may be oral, visual, or tactile and become meaningful when combined into ideas the receiver understands. Communication succeeds when the receiver reacts with understanding and changes behavior according to the source's intent.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Aviation_Instructors_Handbook.pdf | start=95 | end=97]]

### FI.I.A.K4b — Barriers to Effective Communication

The four barriers are lack of common experience, confusion between the symbol and the symbolized object, overuse of abstractions, and external factors. Use terminology the learner understands, link abstract terms to specific examples, and reduce physiological, environmental, and psychological interference where possible. Confirm the message by observing its effect and using feedback.

### FI.I.A.K4c — Developing Communication Skills

Communication skills develop through experience, role-playing, actual instruction, and additional training. Instructors communicate best from accurate subject knowledge, use examples with restraint, and determine whether learner performance shows that instruction was received and retained.

Listening is hearing with comprehension. Instructors learn about a learner's abilities, attitudes, and experience by listening and must recognize that anxiety can interfere with listening. Use role playing, questions, feedback, and learner performance to develop and verify communication in ground and flight instruction.
