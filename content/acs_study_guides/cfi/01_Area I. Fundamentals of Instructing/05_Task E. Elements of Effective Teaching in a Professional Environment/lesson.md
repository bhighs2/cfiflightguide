# Task E. Elements of Effective Teaching in a Professional Environment

**Area I — Fundamentals of Instructing**

## Objective

To determine the applicant understands effects of instructor behavior on effective teaching, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate appropriate skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Aviation instructors are trainers, safety advocates, and role models. Effective instruction depends on the instructor's ability to help learners meet established standards while presenting a knowledgeable, professional, and respectful example.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested Schedule**

- Ground lesson: approximately 45–60 minutes
- Instructor-delivery practice and critique: approximately 15 minutes

**Equipment**

- Aviation Instructor's Handbook
- CFI Airplane ACS
- Whiteboard or tablet
- Flight Guide visual aids

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner Actions**

- Explain instructor and flight-instructor responsibilities.
- Describe methods for maintaining standards and minimizing learner frustration.
- Identify professional behaviors that support effective teaching.
- Deliver a short lesson using a positive, respectful, and organized approach.

**Instructor Actions**

- Teach to established standards and adapt the method to learner needs.
- Keep learners informed of objectives, progress, and deficiencies.
- Demonstrate safety practices, professionalism, and current technical knowledge.
- Evaluate learner performance using observable elements and specific correction.

## Completion Standards

The applicant can deliver ground or flight instruction on an assigned task in a manner consistent with aviation instructor responsibilities, flight-instructor responsibilities, professional characteristics, ethics, learner evaluation, and professional development.

**There is one main idea in this lesson:** an aviation instructor does more than transfer knowledge. The instructor helps the learner progress toward an established standard while also modeling the professional behavior expected in aviation.

Effective teaching therefore requires both **instructional skill** and **professional conduct**.

And we are going to keep the same principle we have used throughout the Fundamentals of Instructing:

**Adapt the instruction for the learner, but do not change the standard.**

### 1. Aviation Instructor Responsibilities — Help the Learner Reach the Standard

- **The basic responsibility of the aviation instructor is to help the learner progress toward the required standard.**
- That means:
    - Transferring knowledge.
    - Developing skill and judgment.
    - Measuring performance against established standards.
    - Reinforcing effective performance.

- **The instructor is also a role model.**
- Learners observe not only what the instructor teaches, but how the instructor behaves.
    - Do we use checklists?
    - Do we manage risk carefully?
    - Do we communicate professionally?
    - Do we follow procedures?
- Those habits become part of the learner's model of aviation behavior.
- **If we routinely cut corners, the learner may imitate that too.**
- That ties directly back to the law of primacy: early habits and examples can leave a lasting impression.

- **The five major aviation instructor responsibilities are:**

**Helping Learners · Providing Adequate Instruction · Training to Established Standards · Emphasizing the Positive · Minimizing Learner Frustration**

- These responsibilities all point toward the same outcome:
  **help the learner progress while preserving the level of competence aviation requires.**

- **So the first question becomes: what does helping the learner actually look like without making the task artificially easy?**

### 2. Helping Learners — Adapt the Path

- **Helping the learner does not mean removing the challenge.**
- Learning can be enjoyable and rewarding while still requiring real effort.
- In fact, satisfaction often comes from successfully meeting a difficult but attainable challenge.

- **Start by stating the objective clearly.**
- The learner should know:
    - What are we trying to accomplish?
    - Why does it matter?
    - What does successful performance look like?

- **Learners differ.**
- They differ in:
    - Background.
    - Interests.
    - Temperament.
    - Confidence.
    - Ability.
    - Ways of thinking and processing information.

- **Adequate instruction means learning enough about the individual to choose an effective teaching approach.**
    - A discouraged learner may need a complex task broken into smaller pieces.
    - A faster learner may need a greater challenge or higher demand.

- **The instructional method changes as necessary, but the required outcome does not.**

- **That leads directly to the next responsibility: if we adapt instruction to the individual, what keeps us from simply lowering expectations? The answer is the established performance standard.**

### 3. Training to Established Standards — Keep the Destination Fixed

- **The instructor trains the learner to established standards for the applicable knowledge, procedures, and maneuvers.**
- The ACS identifies the standards used for certification.

- **But the ACS is a testing document, not a complete teaching document.**
- The instructor still has to build the path that develops:
    - Knowledge.
    - Skill.
    - Judgment.
    - Practice.
    - Risk management.
  needed to reach the standard.

- **The standard defines the destination. The instructor builds the path.**

- Do not confuse **temporary developmental performance** with the final standard.
- A learner may need coaching early in training.
- Instruction continues until the learner can perform independently at the required level.

- **Adapting instruction is not the same as reducing the standard.**
- We may:
    - Change the explanation.
    - Break the task into pieces.
    - Change the pace.
    - Demonstrate again.
    - Give additional practice.
- But we do not redefine competent performance.

- **Training entirely through criticism and deficiency creates another problem: the learner may become fearful, defensive, or discouraged. So while the standard remains fixed, the way we present correction matters.**

### 4. Emphasizing the Positive — Correct Without Discouraging

- **Positive instruction supports effective learning.**
- Emphasizing the positive does **not** mean ignoring deficiencies.
- It means:
    - Recognizing what is working.
    - Being specific about what is deficient.
    - Showing the learner how to improve it.

- A poor critique would be:

**"That pattern was terrible."**

- A better critique would be:

**"Your spacing and configuration were good. Airspeed began increasing on base and carried into final. On the next pattern, let's correct that earlier."**

- The learner now knows:
    - What was done correctly.
    - What went wrong.
    - What to do next.

- **Introduce normal operations before unnecessarily complex abnormal situations.**
- Present safe operating practices positively rather than relying entirely on fear or threat.

- **The learner should leave a lesson knowing both what was accomplished and what comes next.**

- **That positive approach helps with one of the instructor's most common challenges: frustration. A learner who does not understand expectations or cannot see progress can quickly lose motivation.**

### 5. Minimizing Learner Frustration — Make Progress Understandable

- **Frustration increases when the learner does not understand:**
    - The objective.
    - The expectations.
    - Where they stand.
    - Why the training matters.
    - What they need to do next.

- **Connect training back to the learner's purpose.**
- We already learned in Task A that motivation matters.
    - Maybe the learner wants to fly the family.
    - Maybe they want an airline career.
    - Maybe aviation is a personal goal.
- Keep connecting today's task back to why they are here.

- **Keep the learner informed about progress and deficiencies.**
- A learner should never arrive at a stage check believing everything is fine only to discover major deficiencies the instructor never discussed.

- **Give deserved credit.**
- **Criticize constructively.**
- **Be consistent.**
- **Treat the learner as an individual.**
- **Admit instructor errors when they occur.**

- **The goal is not to eliminate all frustration.**
- Some frustration is a natural part of mastering a difficult skill.
- The goal is to minimize **unnecessary frustration** so the learner can continue working productively toward the standard.

- **Up to this point, we have been talking about aviation instructors generally. Flight instruction adds another responsibility: the instructor is now supervising a learner while that learner operates an actual aircraft.**

### 6. Flight Instructor Responsibilities — Build Safe Habits Under Supervision

- **Flight training is a habit-building period.**
- Learners are developing patterns of:
    - Attention.
    - Memory.
    - Judgment.
    - Aircraft control.
    - Risk management.
    - Decision-making.

- **The flight instructor helps mold the learner into a safe pilot—not simply someone who can perform maneuvers.**

- **Supervision and surveillance continue throughout training.**
- One successful performance does not establish proficiency.

- **Solo requires consistent performance.**
- Before endorsing a learner for solo, the instructor observes the operation from:
  **preflight through engine shutdown**
  and determines that the necessary tasks can be performed:
    - Consistently.
    - Safely.
    - Without instructor assistance.

- **The instructor must also remain current and proficient in the aircraft being used for training.**

- The flight instructor teaches and models safe practices including:
    - Risk mitigation.
    - Checklist use.
    - Positive aircraft control.
    - Clear exchange of flight controls.
    - Collision avoidance.
    - ADM.
    - Other applicable safety practices.

- **Supervision does not end because the learner performed something correctly once.**
- Observe consistency.
- Intervene when safety requires it.
- Use the result to determine the next instruction.

- **Because learners imitate what they see, safe supervision depends heavily on the instructor's own knowledge, habits, demeanor, and professionalism. That brings us to flight instructor qualifications and professionalism.**

### 7. Flight Instructor Qualifications and Professionalism — Be the Example You Expect

- **A professional flight instructor must know the aircraft and training environment thoroughly.**
- That includes familiarity with:
    - Aircraft systems.
    - Instruments.
    - Avionics.
    - Operating characteristics.
    - Current training techniques.
    - Certification requirements.
    - Safety procedures.

- **Professionalism is more than technical knowledge. It is visible in conduct.**

- A professional instructor demonstrates:
    - Sincerity.
    - Honesty.
    - Respect for the learner.
    - Proper language.
    - Calm and thoughtful behavior.
    - Discipline.
    - Consistency.
    - Professional appearance and habits.

- Avoid behavior that undermines learning:
    - Contradictory directions.
    - Unreasonable demands.
    - Unfair criticism.
    - Flippant or careless behavior.

- **Professionalism becomes especially visible under pressure.**
- The learner is watching how the instructor responds to:
    - Weather changes.
    - ATC complications.
    - Maintenance concerns.
    - Traffic.
    - Unexpected events.

- **The instructor's behavior becomes part of the lesson even when no formal teaching is occurring.**

- But being professional today is not enough.
- Aviation technology, procedures, regulations, and teaching methods change.
- **Maintaining professionalism therefore requires continued development.**

### 8. Professional Development — The Instructor Keeps Learning Too

- **A professional instructor continues developing both technical knowledge and teaching ability.**
- Aviation changes rapidly, so instructors need to remain alert for ways to improve:
    - Qualifications.
    - Technical knowledge.
    - Teaching effectiveness.
    - Safety knowledge.
    - Service to learners.

- Professional development may include:
    - Current aviation publications.
    - Training courses.
    - Government programs.
    - Seminars.
    - Workshops.
    - Professional organizations.

- **Teaching itself also requires continued development.**
- Experience alone does not guarantee improvement.
- The professional instructor actively looks for better ways to:
    - Explain.
    - Demonstrate.
    - Assess.
    - Communicate.
    - Manage risk.

- **Continued development keeps the instructor technically capable, but knowledge alone does not define professionalism. Ultimately, professionalism is demonstrated through ethical and consistent conduct.**

### 9. Instructor Ethics and Conduct — Practice What You Teach

- **Instructor ethics and conduct are expressed through daily aviation behavior.**
- That includes:
    - Safety.
    - Good judgment.
    - Risk management.
    - Accountability.
    - Responsibility.
    - Courtesy.
    - Prudent operating practices.
    - Compliance with laws and regulations.

- **The instructor's safety message has to match the instructor's personal behavior.**
- Learners commonly imitate instructor habits.
- What the instructor repeatedly demonstrates may become more influential than what the instructor merely says.

- **If I teach checklist discipline but routinely skip checklist items, the learner receives two different lessons—and the behavior they observe may be the one that sticks.**

- The **Aviator's Model Code of Conduct** can provide broader guidance concerning professionalism and airmanship.
- It complements legal requirements and established standards; **it does not replace them.**

- **Professionalism therefore has to be visible and consistent.**
- Prepare thoroughly.
- Communicate honestly.
- Apply standards consistently.
- Demonstrate the safety behavior expected from the learner.

- **That brings the entire Task together. Instructor responsibilities, technical qualifications, teaching method, ethics, and professionalism all become visible during an actual lesson.**

### 10. Applying Professional Behavior During Instruction

- **A professional lesson begins with a clear objective and a clear standard.**
- Then:
    - Match the presentation to the learner's experience and readiness.
    - Maintain a calm, respectful, and positive manner.
    - Observe learner performance.
    - Make specific corrections.
    - Keep the learner informed of progress.
    - Demonstrate the safety and professional behavior you expect the learner to adopt.

- **Aviation example:** A learner is struggling with short-field landings.
    - Restate the objective and standard.
    - Identify the specific weak element.
    - Break the task down if necessary.
    - Demonstrate again if needed.
    - Recognize what the learner is doing correctly.
    - Give specific correction.
    - Increase complexity again as proficiency improves.
    - Continue until the learner meets the required standard.

- **That captures the entire responsibility of the aviation instructor:**
    - Understand the learner.
    - Provide adequate instruction.
    - Maintain the standard.
    - Emphasize productive progress.
    - Minimize unnecessary frustration.
    - Supervise safely.
    - Model the professionalism expected of the learner.

### Close

- **The learner should leave training with more than knowledge and maneuver proficiency.**
- They should also have seen what safe, disciplined, and professional aviation behavior looks like.

- That is why professionalism is part of effective teaching.

- **The instructor is always teaching:**
    - Through the lesson.
    - Through the standard that is enforced.
    - Through the decisions that are made.
    - Through the example the learner sees.

- **Adapt the instruction. Preserve the standard. Model the behavior you expect the learner to carry forward.**



## FI.I.E.K1 — Aviation Instructor Responsibilities

Aviation instructors transfer knowledge, help learners meet and exceed established standards, measure performance against those standards, and provide positive reinforcement. Learners view instructors as role models, so instructors must project a knowledgeable and professional image.

### FI.I.E.K1a — Helping Learners

Learning should be enjoyable and interesting, but standards are not reduced to make a task easy. Satisfaction comes from meeting the challenge of a difficult task. State the objective for each lesson so the learner understands the purpose and can measure progress.

### FI.I.E.K1b — Providing Adequate Instruction

Learners differ in background, interests, temperament, thinking, ability, and confidence. The instructor learns about the individual and adjusts the teaching method as training progresses. For a discouraged learner, break a complex task into discrete elements and increase difficulty as confidence grows. For a fast learner, raise the performance demand without inventing deficiencies or using unfair criticism.

### FI.I.E.K1c — Training to Established Standards of Performance

The instructor trains the applicant to the established standards in applicable ACS tasks, subject areas, procedures, and maneuvers. The ACS is a testing document, not a teaching document; instruction must build the knowledge, skill, and judgment needed to meet the standard.

### FI.I.E.K1d — Emphasizing the Positive

Positive, professional instruction supports learning. Threat, fear, negative self-concept, and excessive information can interfere with perception and learning. Introduce normal operations before complex emergencies, adapt the method when needed, and present safe practices as supporting satisfying, efficient operations.

### FI.I.E.K1e — Minimizing Learner Frustrations

Motivate learners by relating the lesson to their goals. Keep them informed about expectations, progress, examinations, assignments, and deficiencies. Treat learners as individuals, give deserved credit, criticize constructively, remain consistent, and admit instructor errors when they occur.

## FI.I.E.K2 — Flight Instructor Responsibilities

Flight instruction is a habit-building period in which learners develop correct use of attention, memory, and judgment. The instructor helps mold a safe pilot, supervises training, and determines when a learner has demonstrated consistent proficiency for solo.

For solo readiness, the instructor observes the learner from preflight through engine shutdown and verifies consistent performance without instructor assistance in the tasks necessary for the flight. The instructor remains current and proficient in the training aircraft and teaches safe use of risk mitigation, positive aircraft control, control exchange, collision avoidance, checklist use, ADM, and other applicable ACS safety areas.

## FI.I.E.K3 — Flight Instructor Qualifications and Professionalism

A flight instructor is thoroughly familiar with the functions, characteristics, and proper use of the instruments, avionics, and aircraft systems used for training. The instructor remains current on pilot-training techniques, certification requirements, and safety procedures.

Professional instructors maintain high levels of knowledge, training, and currency. They are sincere and honest, accept learners as individuals, maintain a professional appearance and habits, use proper language, and demonstrate a calm, thoughtful, disciplined demeanor. They avoid contradictory directions, unreasonable demands, unfair criticism, and flippant behavior.

## FI.I.E.K4 — Professional Development

Aviation changes rapidly, so professional instructors continually develop their knowledge and teaching skill. They remain alert for ways to improve qualifications, effectiveness, and service to learners. Continuing education includes current technical publications, training courses, government programs, seminars, workshops, and professional organizations.

## FI.I.E.K5 — Instructor Ethics and Conduct

Instructor ethics and conduct are expressed through safety, good judgment, risk management, accountability, responsibility, courtesy, prudent operating practices, and adherence to applicable laws and regulations. An instructor's safety advocacy must be matched by consistent personal example, because learners commonly imitate instructor habits.

The Aviator's Model Code of Conduct offers broad guidance for airmanship, safety, training and proficiency, security, technology, environmental issues, and promotion of general aviation. It complements legal requirements but is not itself a standard.

## Instructor Application — Delivering a Professional Lesson

For the assigned instructional task, state the objective and standard, match the presentation to the learner's experience and readiness, and use a calm, respectful, positive manner. Observe performance, identify specific corrections, and keep the learner informed of progress. Demonstrate the safety and professional conduct expected in aviation operations.
