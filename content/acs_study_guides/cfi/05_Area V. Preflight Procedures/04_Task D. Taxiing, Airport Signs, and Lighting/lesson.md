# Task D. Taxiing, Airport Signs, and Lighting

**Area V — Preflight Procedures**

## Objective

Determine that the applicant understands taxiing an airplane, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate the required skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Every flight begins with taxiing, and an airplane is built to fly, not to drive. Wide wingspans, poor ground visibility, and the tendency to weathervane make taxiing a genuine hazard — and a wrong turn onto a runway is a runway incursion. A flight instructor must taxi safely, read the airport's markings, signs, and lights, and teach the procedure and its risks.

This is a ground and aircraft Task. The ACS skill elements require the applicant to **read back clearances, use an airport diagram, position the controls for the wind, complete the checklist, perform a brake check, maintain positive control, comply with markings and clearances, and position relative to hold lines**.

## Schedule / Equipment

- Ground: 10 minutes in the airplane
- Initial: 5 minutes before flight — introduction and demonstration
- Every flight: 5 minutes before flight — learner performs
- Airplane checklist and the airport diagram

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** explain the basics of taxiing and how to steer the airplane on the ground, then taxi the airplane — perform the before-taxi checklist and brake check, clear the area, position the flight controls for the wind, use an airport diagram to plan the route, read back the taxi clearance (or make CTAF calls), maintain vigilance, hold the centerline at a safe speed, and keep a sterile cockpit while avoiding runway incursions.

**Instructor:** deliver the ground lesson, demonstrate the maneuver before flight, and debrief after each flight.

## Completion Standards

- **Ground:** the learner can explain the basics of taxiing and how to steer the airplane on the ground.
- **Flight:** the learner can taxi the airplane — performing the before-taxi checklist and brake check and visually clearing the area; positioning the flight controls appropriately for the wind; using an airport diagram to plan the taxi route; receiving and correctly reading back ATC taxi clearances (or making CTAF calls); maintaining vigilance in non-movement areas and around other airplanes; maintaining the taxiway centerline, taxiing at an appropriate speed, and using the throttle and brakes properly; and maintaining a sterile cockpit while avoiding runway incursions.

### Purpose

Taxiing looks simple, but it combines aircraft control, radio communication, airport geometry, signs, markings, lighting, and runway-incursion avoidance.

The objective is to taxi the airplane safely while maintaining situational awareness and complying with:

- Airport markings.
- Airport signs.
- Airport lighting.
- ATC clearances and instructions.
- Published airport information.

The pilot should always know:

- Where the airplane is.
- Where the airplane is going.
- What runway or taxiway is ahead.
- What clearance or restriction applies next.

### Safe Taxi Operations

Taxiing is the controlled movement of the airplane on the surface under its own power.

The airplane should be moved:

- At an appropriate speed.
- Along the intended taxi route.
- With positive directional control.
- Without excessive brake use.
- With continuous awareness of other aircraft, vehicles, personnel, and obstacles.

The airplane is designed primarily for flight, not ground maneuvering.

That creates some unique hazards:

- Wide wingspan.
- Limited forward or side visibility.
- Propeller clearance.
- Jet or prop blast.
- Weathervaning.
- Brake overheating.
- Runway-incursion risk.

### Before Moving

Before taxi begins:

- Complete the appropriate checklist.
- Review the airport diagram.
- Identify the planned taxi route.
- Identify any published Hot Spots.
- Review NOTAMs for closures or construction.
- Verify the area around the airplane is clear.
- Make sure the flight controls are positioned for the existing wind.

Do as much setup as possible before the airplane moves.

Once taxi begins:

**the pilot's attention belongs primarily outside.**

### Brake Check

Immediately after the airplane begins moving:

- Reduce power as appropriate.
- Apply the brakes.
- Confirm braking action is normal.

If the brakes do not respond normally:

**stop the airplane before continuing.**

### Steering and Directional Control

In most light airplanes, directional control on the ground is maintained through:

- Rudder-pedal steering.
- Nosewheel or tailwheel steering.
- Differential braking when necessary.

Use smooth inputs.

Do not taxi faster than necessary.

Reduce speed before turns.

Avoid using power against the brakes to control taxi speed.

### Flight Controls for Wind

The flight controls should be positioned so the wind does not lift a wing or tail.

For a quartering headwind:

- Hold aileron into the wind.

For a quartering tailwind:

- Hold the aileron away from the wind.
- Hold the elevator down as appropriate to the airplane.

As wind direction changes during taxi:

**reposition the controls.**

The exact control position depends on:

- Wind direction.
- Aircraft configuration.
- Whether the airplane is tricycle gear or tailwheel.

### Airport Diagram

Use the airport diagram before and during taxi.

The diagram shows:

- Runways.
- Taxiways.
- Aprons.
- Hold-short positions.
- Hot Spots.
- Airport geometry.

At an unfamiliar airport:

- Trace the expected route before moving.
- Keep the diagram available.
- Stop if position becomes uncertain.

### Hot Spots

A Hot Spot is a location on an airport movement area with a history or potential risk of:

- Collision.
- Runway incursion.

Hot Spots should be briefed before taxi.

Know:

- Where they are.
- Why they are hazardous.
- What action is needed to avoid confusion.

### Taxi Clearances

At a towered airport:

- Listen carefully.
- Write down complex instructions if needed.
- Read back runway assignments, hold-short instructions, and runway crossing clearances.
- Do not move until the clearance is understood.

If the clearance does not match what was expected:

**stop and clarify.**

Expectation bias can cause a pilot to hear the clearance they expected rather than the clearance ATC actually issued.

### Non-Towered Airports

At a non-towered airport:

- Monitor and transmit on the CTAF.
- Announce intentions.
- Maintain visual awareness of traffic.
- Verify runways are clear before entering or crossing.

There is no controller providing runway separation.

The pilot is responsible for determining that movement is safe.

### Runway Crossing

At a towered airport:

**do not cross or enter a runway without a specific clearance.**

Before crossing:

- Confirm the runway.
- Read back the clearance.
- Look in both directions.
- Verify the runway is clear.

At a non-towered airport:

- Look both ways.
- Listen for traffic.
- Make the appropriate CTAF call.
- Cross only when safe.

### Runway Incursion Avoidance

A runway incursion can result from:

- Crossing a hold line without clearance.
- Turning onto the wrong runway.
- Misunderstanding a taxi clearance.
- Losing position.
- Distraction.
- Continuing when unsure.

Prevention includes:

- Using the airport diagram.
- Reviewing Hot Spots.
- Reading back clearances.
- Maintaining a sterile cockpit.
- Verifying every runway crossing.
- Stopping when uncertain.

If position is unclear:

**stop the airplane and ask.**

### Runway Markings

Runway markings are generally:

**white.**

Common runway markings include:

- Runway designation.
- Centerline.
- Threshold.
- Aiming point.
- Touchdown zone.
- Side stripes.

### Runway Designators

Runway numbers correspond to the runway's magnetic direction rounded to the nearest 10 degrees, with the final zero omitted.

Example:

- Runway 17 is approximately 170° magnetic.
- Runway 35 is approximately 350° magnetic.

Parallel runways may also use:

- L — Left.
- C — Center.
- R — Right.

### Runway Centerline

The runway centerline is:

- White.
- Dashed.

It provides alignment guidance for:

- Takeoff.
- Landing.

### Threshold Markings

The threshold identifies the beginning of the portion of runway available for landing.

Threshold markings are:

- White.
- Longitudinal stripes.

### Displaced Threshold

A displaced threshold is located beyond the physical beginning of the runway.

The pavement before a displaced threshold may normally be available for:

- Taxi.
- Takeoff.
- Landing rollout from the opposite direction.

It is not available for touchdown from the displaced-threshold direction.

A displaced threshold is identified by:

- White arrows.
- Arrowheads.
- Threshold bar.

### Blast Pad / Stopway

A blast pad or stopway is marked with:

**yellow chevrons.**

It is not intended for normal:

- Taxi.
- Takeoff.
- Landing.

Do not confuse a paved surface with usable runway.

### Closed Runway

A closed runway is identified by:

**yellow X markings.**

Temporary closures may not always have every visual marking installed.

Check:

- NOTAMs.
- ATIS.
- Airport information.

### Taxiway Markings

Taxiway markings are generally:

**yellow.**

### Taxiway Centerline

The taxiway centerline is:

- A single continuous yellow line.

Taxi with the airplane centered on the line when practical.

### Enhanced Taxiway Centerline

An enhanced taxiway centerline warns that a runway holding position is ahead.

It consists of:

- The normal yellow centerline.
- Additional dashed yellow lines on both sides.

Its purpose is to increase awareness before reaching a runway.

### Taxiway Edge Markings

Taxiway edges may be identified by:

**double yellow lines.**

Continuous lines separate the taxiway from pavement not intended for aircraft use.

Dashed double yellow lines may separate the taxiway from adjoining pavement that aircraft may enter, such as an apron.

### Runway Holding Position Marking

The runway holding-position marking consists of:

- Two solid yellow lines.
- Two dashed yellow lines.

When approaching a runway from a taxiway:

**hold on the solid-line side.**

Do not allow any part of the airplane to cross the hold line until authorized at a towered airport.

### ILS Critical Area Holding Position

An ILS critical-area holding position is used when aircraft must remain clear so they do not interfere with ILS signals.

The marking is different from a standard runway hold line.

When instructed to hold short of the ILS critical area:

**stop before the marking.**

### Nonmovement Area Boundary

The nonmovement-area boundary separates:

- ATC-controlled movement area.
- Nonmovement area.

It consists of:

- One solid yellow line.
- One dashed yellow line.

The solid side is on the nonmovement-area side.

### Mandatory Instruction Signs

Mandatory instruction signs have:

- **Red background.**
- **White inscription.**

They identify locations where a pilot must comply with a restriction or clearance.

Examples include:

- Runway holding position.
- ILS critical area.
- Runway approach area.
- No entry.

A red sign requires immediate attention.

### Location Signs

Location signs have:

- **Black background.**
- **Yellow inscription and border.**

They tell the pilot:

**where the airplane is.**

### Direction Signs

Direction signs have:

- **Yellow background.**
- **Black inscription.**
- Arrow.

They identify the direction to an intersecting taxiway.

### Destination Signs

Destination signs have:

- **Yellow background.**
- **Black inscription.**
- Arrow.

They direct the pilot toward locations such as:

- Runways.
- Terminals.
- FBOs.
- Cargo areas.

### Runway Distance Remaining Signs

Runway distance remaining signs have:

- **Black background.**
- **White numeral.**

The number represents:

**thousands of feet of runway remaining.**

### Runway Edge Lights

Runway edge lights are generally:

**white.**

On certain instrument runways, the lights become:

**yellow**

near the departure end as a caution that the runway end is approaching.

### Threshold and Runway-End Lights

Threshold and runway-end light fixtures are directional.

Toward the landing aircraft, the threshold appears:

**green.**

Toward an aircraft on the runway approaching the end, the runway end appears:

**red.**

### Taxiway Edge Lights

Taxiway edge lights are:

**blue.**

### Taxiway Centerline Lights

Taxiway centerline lights are:

**green.**

### Runway Guard Lights

Runway guard lights increase awareness of a runway holding position.

They may appear as:

- Two elevated flashing yellow lights.
- In-pavement flashing yellow lights across the taxiway.

They indicate:

**a runway is immediately ahead.**

### Stop Bar Lights

Stop bars are:

- Red.
- Steady-burning.
- Installed across the taxiway at some runway holding positions.

Do not cross an illuminated red stop bar.

If a clearance conflicts with an illuminated stop bar:

**stop and contact ATC.**

### Clearance Bar Lights

Clearance bar lights are:

- Three steady-burning yellow lights.

They help identify:

- Holding positions.
- Taxiway intersections.

### Runway Centerline Lights

On runways equipped with runway centerline lighting:

- Lights are white initially.
- They alternate red and white near the runway end.
- They become all red for the final portion.

The color change gives the pilot another cue that the runway end is approaching.

### Touchdown Zone Lights

Touchdown-zone lights are:

- White.
- Installed in rows on each side of the runway centerline.

They help define the touchdown area in reduced visibility.

### Taxiway Lead-On and Lead-Off Lights

Lead-on and lead-off lights help guide aircraft between:

- Runway.
- Taxiway.

They may use:

- Green.
- Alternating green and yellow.

The color coding also helps indicate when the aircraft is still within the runway or protected area.

### Approach Light Systems

Approach light systems help transition from instrument references to the visual runway environment.

They are located:

- On or near the extended runway centerline.
- Before the runway threshold.

Their configuration depends on the installed system.

### REIL

**Runway End Identifier Lights** identify the approach end of a runway.

They consist of:

- Two synchronized flashing lights.
- One on each side of the runway threshold.

They are especially useful when the runway is difficult to distinguish from surrounding lights or terrain.

### VASI

A **Visual Approach Slope Indicator** provides visual glidepath information.

A typical two-bar VASI displays:

- White over white — high.
- Red over white — on glidepath.
- Red over red — low.

### PAPI

A **Precision Approach Path Indicator** typically uses four light units.

Typical indications are:

- Four white — high.
- Three white / one red — slightly high.
- Two white / two red — on glidepath.
- One white / three red — slightly low.
- Four red — low.

### Airport Beacon

Common airport beacon combinations include:

- White and green — civilian land airport.
- White and yellow — water airport.
- Green, yellow, and white — heliport.
- Two quick white flashes followed by green — military airport.

A beacon operating during daylight at a controlled airport may indicate reduced weather conditions, but:

**do not use the beacon as the sole source for determining whether conditions are VFR or IFR.**

### Pilot-Controlled Lighting

At some airports, the pilot can control airport lighting by keying the microphone on the published frequency.

Common selections are:

- **3 clicks** — lower intensity.
- **5 clicks** — medium intensity.
- **7 clicks** — highest intensity.

The exact response depends on the installed lighting system.

Verify the published frequency and installed lighting system in the Chart Supplement.

### Wind Indicators

Visual wind indicators may include:

- Windsock.
- Wind cone.
- Wind tee.
- Tetrahedron where installed.

Use them to determine:

- Wind direction.
- Approximate wind strength.
- Expected weathervaning.

### Aircraft Lighting During Taxi

Use aircraft lighting in a way that improves conspicuity without creating a hazard.

Consider:

- Beacon.
- Position lights.
- Taxi light.
- Landing light.
- Strobes.

Avoid shining bright lights or strobes directly at other pilots when it would impair their vision.

Use the aircraft checklist and operating procedures.

### Night Taxi

At night:

- Taxi slower.
- Use the airport diagram.
- Follow taxiway centerline lighting and signs.
- Confirm every runway crossing.
- Avoid fixation on bright lights.
- Stop if position is uncertain.

### Low-Visibility Taxi

In low visibility:

- Reduce taxi speed.
- Keep the airport diagram available.
- Follow centerline markings and lighting closely.
- Confirm every instruction.
- Expect more restrictive routing.
- Stop if unsure.

At airports using low-visibility surface-movement procedures:

follow the published procedure and ATC instructions.

### Common Errors

Common errors include:

- Taxiing too fast.
- Riding the brakes.
- Failing to perform an initial brake check.
- Improper flight-control positioning for wind.
- Losing position on the airport.
- Taxiing without the airport diagram available.
- Missing a Hot Spot.
- Misunderstanding a taxi clearance.
- Failing to read back a hold-short instruction.
- Crossing a hold line without authorization.
- Confusing a runway with a taxiway.
- Misreading location and direction signs.
- Failing to comply with lighting indications.
- Continuing to taxi when unsure of position.
- Allowing distraction during taxi.

## AI.V.D.K1 — Elements of Safe Taxi Operations

Taxiing is the controlled movement of the airplane under its own power on the surface. An airplane is designed to fly, not to drive — wide wingspans, long fuselages, and poor ground visibility create unique challenges. Safe taxi rests on:

- **Situational awareness** — know the ramp, taxiways, and runway environment, and the people, equipment, and aircraft moving on it. Review the complete taxi plan; expectation bias is real.
- **Steering with the rudder pedals** — an airplane does not steer like a car. Apply rudder in the direction of the turn and use power or brake to control speed; release the rudder just short of the desired heading. Some airplanes have no nosewheel steering and steer by differential braking.
- **Throttle and brakes used properly** — more power is needed to start moving than to keep moving; retard the throttle once moving. Taxi **slowly enough to stop on throttle alone** — if you are riding the brakes, you are too fast. **Test the brakes as soon as the airplane begins moving.**
- **Holding the taxiway centerline** — visually place the yellow centerline under the center of the fuselage. Slow before a turn; sharp high-speed turns side-load the landing gear and can cause a swerve or ground loop.
- **Flight-control positioning for wind** — position the controls so the wind cannot get under a wing or the tail: aileron into a quartering headwind, elevator down in a quartering tailwind.
- **Positive control** — control direction and speed without excessive brake use.

## AI.V.D.K2 — Airport Aeronautical References and Information

Use the current references to plan the taxi before moving:

- **Airport diagram** — the map of the runways, taxiways, aprons, hold lines, and **hot spots**. Have it out and in view at all times during taxi.
- **Chart Supplement** — the airport's data, frequencies, lighting, and remarks.
- **NOTAMs** — runway, taxiway, and apron closures and construction that change the picture.

## AI.V.D.K3 — Taxi Instructions and Clearances

At a **towered airport**, receive the taxi clearance from ground control and **read it back correctly**, writing down the route if it is complex. At a **non-towered airport**, self-announce on the **CTAF** and scan for conflicting traffic. A clearance to cross or hold short of a runway is a specific instruction — read it back and comply. If a clearance is unclear or seems wrong, **stop and query it**.

## AI.V.D.K4 — Airport Markings, Signs, and Lights

Teach the learner to read the airport's visual system:

- **Markings** — the yellow taxiway centerline, the **runway holding-position marking** (two solid and two dashed yellow lines), the enhanced taxiway centerline before a runway, and the runway markings themselves.
- **Signs** — **mandatory instruction signs** (red with white letters — runway designations and holding positions: stop unless cleared), **location signs** (yellow on black), **direction and destination signs** (black on yellow), and **runway distance-remaining signs**.
- **Lights** — **blue taxiway edge lights**, **green taxiway centerline lights**, **runway edge lights** (white, with yellow in the last 2,000 feet), threshold and runway-end lights, and the runway status-light systems (REL, THL) where installed.

## AI.V.D.K5 — Visual Indicators for Wind

Read the wind before and during taxi from the **windsock, wind cone, or wind tee**, and from the **flags and the movement of grass, water, and other aircraft**. The wind indicator drives both the control positioning and the expectation of the airplane's tendency to weathervane.

## AI.V.D.K6 — Aircraft Lighting, as Appropriate

Use the aircraft's lights appropriately on the ground: the **anti-collision beacon** on before engine start, the **position lights** at night, and the **taxi/landing light** to see — without blinding other pilots. Limit the landing light if it overheats or overloads the electrical system.

## AI.V.D.K7 — Taxi Procedures

### K7a — Flight deck activities prior to taxi

Plan the route on the airport diagram before moving, identify the **hot spots**, brief the expected clearance, and set up the cockpit so the pilot's attention can stay outside. Complete the before-taxi checklist and the instrument check while taxiing.

### K7b — Radio communications at towered and non-towered airports

At a towered airport, use ground control and read back clearances; at a non-towered airport, self-announce on the CTAF and maintain a visual scan.

### K7c — Entering or crossing runways

**Never enter or cross a runway without a clearance** at a towered airport. Read back the hold-short or crossing instruction, verify the runway, and look both ways. At a non-towered airport, verify the runway is clear before entering.

### K7d — Night taxi operations

At night the markings and signs are harder to see and the references fewer. Taxi slowly, use the taxi/landing light, follow the centerline and the blue edge lights, and confirm position against the airport diagram.

### K7e — Low-visibility taxi operations

In low visibility, slow down further, use the airport diagram continuously, follow the taxiway centerline and signs carefully, and stop if the route is uncertain. Surface movement guidance systems (SMGCS) apply at some airports in very low visibility.

## Risk Management

### AI.V.D.R1 — Activities and Distractions

Taxiing demands full attention; a distraction during taxi leads to a wrong turn or a missed hold line. Keep a **sterile cockpit** during taxi — no nonessential conversation, heads-up, eyes outside.

### AI.V.D.R2 — Confirmation or Expectation Bias in Taxi Instructions

Expecting the usual clearance and not hearing the actual one is a real hazard. Read back the clearance, and if it differs from what you expected, stop and resolve it before moving.

### AI.V.D.R3 — A Taxi Route or Departure Runway Change

A changed route or runway invalidates the plan. Re-plan on the airport diagram, brief the new route, and confirm the new clearance before moving.

### AI.V.D.R4 — Runway Incursion

A runway incursion is any entry onto or presence on a runway without a clearance — a crossed hold line, a wrong turn onto a runway, or continuing past a hold line. The Tenerife disaster showed the catastrophic result. Mitigate by never crossing a hold line without a clearance, reading back hold-short instructions, verifying the runway, and stopping when in doubt.

### Common Errors and Corrections

- **Improper use of brakes:** control speed with the throttle; brakes are for the stop, not against power.
- **Improper control positioning for wind:** turn into a quartering headwind, dive away from a quartering tailwind.
- **Taxiing too fast:** slow enough to stop on throttle alone.
- **Not complying with markings, signals, or clearances:** follow the visual system and read back the clearance.
- **Distraction during taxi / no sterile cockpit:** keep the taxi sterile and eyes outside.
- **Failing to use runway-incursion procedures:** never cross a hold line without a clearance; when in doubt, stop and ask.

[[answer:
Situational awareness of the ramp, taxiways, and runway environment; steering with the rudder pedals (not like a car); proper use of throttle and brakes (taxi slowly enough to stop on throttle alone); holding the taxiway centerline; positioning the flight controls for the wind; and positive control without excessive brake use. Test the brakes as soon as the airplane begins moving.

2. How do you position the flight controls for the wind while taxiing?

[[answer:
Turn into the wind and dive away from it. In a quartering headwind, hold the aileron into the wind (upwind aileron up). In a quartering tailwind, hold the elevator down (and the upwind aileron down). The goal is to keep the wind from getting under a wing or the tail and to reduce weathervaning.
]]

3. What references do you use to plan a taxi, and why the airport diagram?

[[answer:
The airport diagram (out and in view during taxi — the runways, taxiways, hold lines, and hot spots), the Chart Supplement (airport data and frequencies), and NOTAMs (closures and construction). The airport diagram lets you plan the route and maintain situational awareness before and during the taxi.
]]
