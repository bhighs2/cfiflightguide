# Task C. Engine Starting

**Area V — Preflight Procedures**

## Objective

Determine that the applicant understands engine starting, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate the required skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Starting an airplane engine is easy, but it is more complicated than starting a car. A spinning propeller is lethal, a hard-starting carbureted engine has its own temperament, and an engine fire on the start is a genuine emergency with a counterintuitive response. A flight instructor must be able to start the engine safely under various conditions, manage the hazards, and teach the procedure and its emergencies.

This is a ground and aircraft Task. The ACS skill elements require the applicant to **position the airplane safely** and **complete the appropriate checklist**.

## Schedule / Equipment

- Ground: 5 minutes in the airplane
- Initial: 5 minutes before flight — introduction and demonstration
- Every flight: 5 minutes before flight — learner performs
- Airplane checklist

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** explain the purpose of the engine-start procedure and how to execute it, then perform it — ensure the area and propeller are clear, prime as needed, follow the start checklist, manipulate the engine controls for a successful start, verify the engine indications after start, and respond to an engine fire during start.

**Instructor:** deliver the ground lesson, demonstrate the procedure in the airplane, and debrief after each flight.

## Completion Standards

- **Ground:** the learner can explain the purpose of the procedure and how to execute it properly.
- **Flight:** the learner can explain and perform the procedure — ensuring the area and propeller are clear, priming as needed, following the engine-start checklist, manipulating the engine controls, verifying the indications after start, and responding correctly to an engine fire during start.

### Purpose

Engine starting is a normal procedure with several serious hazards:

- The propeller can injure or kill someone.
- The airplane can move unexpectedly.
- Incorrect priming can flood the engine or cause a fire.
- Starter limits can be exceeded.
- Abnormal oil pressure or other indications can reveal a problem immediately after start.

The objective is to start the engine safely, using the approved checklist and the procedure appropriate to the conditions.

### Before Starting

Before engaging the starter:

- Position the airplane so propeller blast will not endanger people, aircraft, vehicles, or buildings.
- Verify the area around the propeller is clear.
- Verify the area behind the airplane is clear.
- Remove chocks, tow bar, and tiedowns as appropriate.
- Set the brakes as directed by the POH.
- Turn on the anti-collision beacon as appropriate.
- Use position lights at night as appropriate.
- Open the window or door as needed and call **“CLEAR PROP.”**
- Pause long enough for anyone nearby to respond or move away.

Do not assume the area is clear simply because no one was visible a few seconds earlier.

### Propeller Safety

Treat every propeller as though the ignition system is live.

A stationary propeller can move if:

- A magneto switch is faulty.
- The ignition is unintentionally left on.
- The engine fires unexpectedly.

Never allow:

- Passengers.
- Baggage.
- Ground personnel.

to move through the propeller arc.

### Positioning the Airplane

The ACS requires the airplane to be positioned with consideration for:

- Structures.
- Other aircraft.
- Wind.
- Nearby persons.
- Property.

Before starting, consider:

- Propeller blast.
- Loose debris.
- Fuel spills.
- Nearby vehicles.
- Open hangars.
- People walking on the ramp.

Wind direction may also matter because it affects:

- Propeller blast.
- Engine cooling.
- Smoke or fumes.

### Use the Correct Checklist

Use the engine-start checklist for the actual airplane.

Starting procedures vary based on:

- Carbureted versus fuel-injected engine.
- Cold versus hot engine.
- Flooded engine.
- External-power start.
- Installed equipment.

Do not substitute a remembered procedure from another airplane.

### Starting Under Normal Conditions

Set the engine controls according to the POH.

Typical items may include:

- Fuel selector.
- Mixture.
- Throttle.
- Primer.
- Carburetor heat.
- Boost pump.
- Propeller control.
- Master switch.
- Beacon.

The exact sequence is aircraft-specific.

### Keep Control of the Throttle

Maintain immediate access to the throttle during the start.

When the engine fires:

- Set the RPM to the value specified by the POH.
- Prevent an excessive initial RPM.
- Keep the airplane from moving.

Do not let the engine start at a high power setting.

### Preventing Aircraft Movement

During and immediately after start:

- Hold the brakes.
- Monitor outside.
- Verify the airplane is not creeping forward.
- Avoid excessive heads-down time.

A successful engine start does not help if the airplane rolls into:

- Another aircraft.
- A vehicle.
- A person.
- A building.

### Cold-Weather Starting

Cold weather can affect:

- Oil viscosity.
- Battery output.
- Starter performance.
- Fuel vaporization.
- Spark-plug performance.

Use the cold-start procedure in the POH.

Preheat may be appropriate or required depending on:

- Temperature.
- Aircraft guidance.
- Oil grade.

Cold-start risks include:

- Excessive starter use.
- Overpriming.
- Poor oil circulation.
- Engine damage from operating before adequate lubrication is established.

### Hot-Weather / Hot-Engine Starting

A hot engine may require a different procedure from a cold engine.

Fuel-injected engines may be particularly susceptible to:

- Fuel vaporization.
- Vapor in the fuel lines.
- Difficult hot starts.

Use the POH hot-start procedure.

Do not compensate for a difficult hot start by randomly adding more fuel.

That may turn a difficult start into a flooded start.

### Flooded / Overprimed Engine

Too much fuel can flood the engine.

Possible indications include:

- Strong fuel smell.
- Fuel draining from the induction system.
- Repeated unsuccessful firing.

Use the flooded-start procedure in the POH.

Do not continue adding prime.

Overpriming can also increase the risk of an engine fire during start.

### Starter Limitations

The starter is not designed for unlimited continuous operation.

Use the starter duty cycle specified in the POH or aircraft documentation.

Excessive cranking can cause:

- Starter overheating.
- Electrical damage.
- Battery depletion.
- Starter failure.

If the engine does not start:

- Stop cranking.
- Allow the required cooling period.
- Reassess the starting condition and procedure.

Do not keep grinding the starter because the engine “almost started.”

### Engine Limitations During Start

The start is not complete when the engine begins running.

Immediately monitor:

- Oil pressure.
- RPM.
- Electrical-system indications.
- Other required engine instruments.

If oil pressure does not rise as required by the POH:

**shut the engine down.**

Do not continue operating an engine without confirmed lubrication.

### After-Start Engine Indications

After start:

- Set the specified RPM.
- Confirm oil pressure.
- Confirm electrical charging.
- Check annunciators.
- Listen for abnormal noises.
- Feel for abnormal vibration.
- Watch for smoke or unusual smell.

An abnormal indication is a reason to stop and investigate.

### Engine Fire During Start

An engine fire during start requires the **aircraft-specific POH/AFM emergency procedure**.

For many light piston airplanes, the procedure attempts to draw the fire into the engine by continuing to crank.

A typical sequence may involve:

- Mixture idle cutoff.
- Throttle open.
- Continued cranking.

But:

**use the actual POH procedure for the airplane.**

If the fire does not extinguish:

- Shut down.
- Turn off fuel and electrical sources as directed.
- Evacuate.
- Use a fire extinguisher if appropriate and safe.
- Obtain assistance.

Do not teach one generic fire-on-start sequence as universal.

### External Power Starting

An external power unit may be used when approved by the airplane manufacturer.

Use:

- Correct voltage.
- Correct connector.
- Correct polarity.
- Correct connection sequence.
- Correct disconnection procedure.

Follow the POH/AFM supplement.

External power introduces risks including:

- Electrical-system damage.
- Arcing.
- Incorrect connection.
- Ground personnel near the propeller.

Coordinate clearly with the ground crew.

### External Power Is Not a Shortcut Around Maintenance

A discharged battery may have an identifiable cause.

Before using external power, consider whether the battery condition indicates:

- Electrical-system fault.
- Charging-system problem.
- Maintenance issue.

Starting the engine does not fix the underlying problem.

### Hand Propping

Hand propping is hazardous and should only be performed:

- When appropriate for the aircraft.
- By people trained in the procedure.
- In accordance with the POH/AFM.

The risks include:

- Unexpected engine start.
- Loss of balance.
- Contact with the propeller.
- Airplane movement after start.

### Hand-Propping Precautions

If hand propping is appropriate and authorized:

- Use trained personnel.
- Establish clear verbal commands.
- Ensure the brakes are set.
- Use chocks or other securing methods as appropriate.
- Use a stable, debris-free surface.
- Verify ignition status before moving the propeller.
- Never place an untrained person at the controls.

For an airplane equipped with a normal electric starter:

an inoperative starter is normally a maintenance issue, not a reason to improvise a hand-prop start.

### Risk Management — Propeller Safety

The propeller hazard exists:

- Before start.
- During start.
- After start.

Mitigate it by:

- Clearing the area.
- Calling “clear prop.”
- Using the beacon appropriately.
- Treating the prop as live.
- Preventing people from approaching the propeller arc.

### Risk Management — External Power

External power adds:

- Electrical hazards.
- Ground-personnel coordination.
- Propeller exposure.

Mitigation:

- Use approved equipment.
- Follow the exact procedure.
- Coordinate before start.
- Keep everyone clear of the propeller.

### Risk Management — Starting Limitations

Starting limitations include:

- Starter duty cycle.
- Priming limits.
- Oil-pressure requirements.
- RPM limits.
- Electrical limitations.

Mitigation is simple:

**know the limits before starting and stop when an indication is abnormal.**

### Common Errors

Common errors include:

- Failing to use the proper checklist.
- Starting without clearing the propeller.
- Failing to check the area behind the airplane.
- Inadequate brake application.
- Excessive throttle during start.
- Incorrect priming.
- Overpriming.
- Using the wrong hot- or cold-start procedure.
- Exceeding starter duty-cycle limitations.
- Ignoring low or absent oil pressure.
- Failing to monitor outside after start.
- Improper use of external power.
- Improvised hand propping.
- Failing to follow the POH for an engine fire during start.

Teach engine starting as a hazard-management task, not a button-push: the propeller is lethal, the start has a defined checklist, the engine has limitations, and a fire on the start has a specific, counterintuitive response. The Overview maps the ACS knowledge, risk, and skill elements; the sections below teach the material once, in the order an instructor would perform a start.

## AI.V.C.K1 — Starting Under Various Conditions

**Use the approved engine-start checklist from the AFM/POH — read and do, every time.** Engines vary, and the specific procedure governs. Atmospheric conditions change the priming and technique:

- **Hot vs. cold start** — a hot fuel-injected engine and a cold carbureted engine start differently; the conditions change the priming and the throttle/mixture settings.
- **Overpriming** — too much prime floods the engine; the remedy is the flooded-start procedure (typically throttle open, mixture cutoff, crank to clear, then a normal start — per the POH).
- **Season and temperature** — use the proper **grade of oil for the season** and **preheat when temperatures approach freezing**.

## AI.V.C.K2 — Starting the Engine by External Power

When the battery is dead, an **external power unit** can start the engine. This requires the proper equipment, connections, and procedure — follow the AFM/POH. An inoperative starter is a maintenance issue, not a reason to improvise a start.

## AI.V.C.K3 — Engine Limitations During Starting

The engine and starter have limits the pilot must honor.

- **After start:** set the engine rpm to the AFM/POH-prescribed value — generally about **1,000 rpm** — to let the oil pressure rise and to minimize wear. **Monitor the oil pressure**: it should rise to at least the lower limit within about **30 seconds**; if it does not reach and hold the POH range, **shut down immediately** to prevent internal engine damage. Watch for abnormal sounds, vibrations, smells, or smoke, and shut down and investigate any concern.
- **Starter limitations:** the starter is not a continuous-duty motor. **Crank no more than about 30 seconds, then allow a 30-second to 1-minute cool-down** (longer if the POH specifies). The smell of burning insulation means the cranking limit was exceeded. A **continuously engaged starter** shows as a high continuous ammeter draw (or a starter-engaged warning light) — shut down immediately.

### Engine fire during start

An engine fire on the start has a counterintuitive response: **mixture cutoff, throttle full, and continue cranking** — the cranking can draw the fire back through the engine and extinguish it. If the fire is not extinguished, **shut down, shut off the fuel and electrical, and evacuate**. Know and follow the specific aircraft POH emergency procedure.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
The natural instinct on an engine fire is to stop cranking — which is exactly wrong on many airplanes. Teach the POH procedure cold: mixture cutoff, throttle full, keep cranking. Rehearse it before it is ever needed.
]]

## Hand Propping

**Hand propping is extremely dangerous and difficult** and should be performed only by persons trained and competent in the procedure, per the AFM/POH. A spinning propeller can be lethal, and a magneto switch can be faulty in the "off" position — **always assume the ignition is on when touching a propeller**. Hand propping requires **two trained people**, a stable debris-free surface, agreed voice commands, and the correct blade position and stance. A person unfamiliar with the controls must never occupy the pilot's seat. **Do not attempt hand propping if you are not trained** — most modern trainers with electric starters should be started normally, and an inoperative starter is a maintenance issue, not a reason to hand prop.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
Hand propping is a last resort for aircraft built without starters, performed by two trained people. If the airplane has an electric starter and it fails, that is a maintenance call — not a hand-propping invitation.
]]

## Risk Management

### AI.V.C.R1 — Propeller Safety

The spinning propeller is invisible and lethal. Position the airplane for the safety of nearby persons and property, do a final walk-around (tow bar, chocks, tiedowns removed; area clear, including **behind** the airplane), turn the **anti-collision beacon ON** (position lights at night), **set the brakes**, and call **"CLEAR"** out the window and **wait for a response** before cranking. Treat every propeller as if the ignition is live.

### AI.V.C.R2 — Use of External Power Unit

A dead battery and external power bring connection and procedure risks. Mitigate with the correct equipment and the AFM/POH procedure — never improvise.

### AI.V.C.R3 — Limitations During Starting

The starter duty cycle, the oil-pressure window, and the priming limits. Mitigate by honoring the crank/cool-down limits, watching the oil pressure, and shutting down on abnormal indications.

### Common Errors and Corrections

- **Not using the appropriate checklist:** follow the AFM/POH start checklist, read and do, every time.
- **Not using the safety precautions:** the beacon, the area check, the brakes, and the "CLEAR" call are not optional.
- **Improper adjustment of the engine controls during start:** know the priming and control settings for the conditions and the POH.
- **Not assuring the propeller is clear:** the area around and behind the airplane must be checked before cranking.
- **Stopping the cranking on a fire:** follow the POH — on many airplanes, continue cranking.
