# Task C. Engine Starting

**Area V — Preflight Procedures**

## Objective

Determine that the applicant understands engine starting, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate the required skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Starting an airplane engine is easy, but it is more complicated than starting a car. A spinning propeller is lethal, a hard-starting carbureted engine has its own temperament, and an engine fire on the start is a genuine emergency with a counterintuitive response. A flight instructor must be able to start the engine safely under various conditions, manage the hazards, and teach the procedure and its emergencies.

This is a ground and aircraft Task. The ACS skill elements require the applicant to **position the airplane safely** and **complete the appropriate checklist**.

## Schedule / Equipment

- Ground: 5 minutes in the airplane
- Initial: 5 minutes before flight — introduction and demonstration
- Every flight: 5 minutes before flight — learner performs
- Airplane checklist

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** explain the purpose of the engine-start procedure and how to execute it, then perform it — ensure the area and propeller are clear, prime as needed, follow the start checklist, manipulate the engine controls for a successful start, verify the engine indications after start, and respond to an engine fire during start.

**Instructor:** deliver the ground lesson, demonstrate the procedure in the airplane, and debrief after each flight.

## Completion Standards

- **Ground:** the learner can explain the purpose of the procedure and how to execute it properly.
- **Flight:** the learner can explain and perform the procedure — ensuring the area and propeller are clear, priming as needed, following the engine-start checklist, manipulating the engine controls, verifying the indications after start, and responding correctly to an engine fire during start.

# Teach

Teach engine starting as a hazard-management task, not a button-push: the propeller is lethal, the start has a defined checklist, the engine has limitations, and a fire on the start has a specific, counterintuitive response. The Overview maps the ACS knowledge, risk, and skill elements; the sections below teach the material once, in the order an instructor would perform a start.

## AI.V.C.K1 — Starting Under Various Conditions

**Use the approved engine-start checklist from the AFM/POH — read and do, every time.** Engines vary, and the specific procedure governs. Atmospheric conditions change the priming and technique:

- **Hot vs. cold start** — a hot fuel-injected engine and a cold carbureted engine start differently; the conditions change the priming and the throttle/mixture settings.
- **Overpriming** — too much prime floods the engine; the remedy is the flooded-start procedure (typically throttle open, mixture cutoff, crank to clear, then a normal start — per the POH).
- **Season and temperature** — use the proper **grade of oil for the season** and **preheat when temperatures approach freezing**.

## AI.V.C.K2 — Starting the Engine by External Power

When the battery is dead, an **external power unit** can start the engine. This requires the proper equipment, connections, and procedure — follow the AFM/POH. An inoperative starter is a maintenance issue, not a reason to improvise a start.

## AI.V.C.K3 — Engine Limitations During Starting

The engine and starter have limits the pilot must honor.

- **After start:** set the engine rpm to the AFM/POH-prescribed value — generally about **1,000 rpm** — to let the oil pressure rise and to minimize wear. **Monitor the oil pressure**: it should rise to at least the lower limit within about **30 seconds**; if it does not reach and hold the POH range, **shut down immediately** to prevent internal engine damage. Watch for abnormal sounds, vibrations, smells, or smoke, and shut down and investigate any concern.
- **Starter limitations:** the starter is not a continuous-duty motor. **Crank no more than about 30 seconds, then allow a 30-second to 1-minute cool-down** (longer if the POH specifies). The smell of burning insulation means the cranking limit was exceeded. A **continuously engaged starter** shows as a high continuous ammeter draw (or a starter-engaged warning light) — shut down immediately.

### Engine fire during start

An engine fire on the start has a counterintuitive response: **mixture cutoff, throttle full, and continue cranking** — the cranking can draw the fire back through the engine and extinguish it. If the fire is not extinguished, **shut down, shut off the fuel and electrical, and evacuate**. Know and follow the specific aircraft POH emergency procedure.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
The natural instinct on an engine fire is to stop cranking — which is exactly wrong on many airplanes. Teach the POH procedure cold: mixture cutoff, throttle full, keep cranking. Rehearse it before it is ever needed.
]]

## Hand Propping

**Hand propping is extremely dangerous and difficult** and should be performed only by persons trained and competent in the procedure, per the AFM/POH. A spinning propeller can be lethal, and a magneto switch can be faulty in the "off" position — **always assume the ignition is on when touching a propeller**. Hand propping requires **two trained people**, a stable debris-free surface, agreed voice commands, and the correct blade position and stance. A person unfamiliar with the controls must never occupy the pilot's seat. **Do not attempt hand propping if you are not trained** — most modern trainers with electric starters should be started normally, and an inoperative starter is a maintenance issue, not a reason to hand prop.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
Hand propping is a last resort for aircraft built without starters, performed by two trained people. If the airplane has an electric starter and it fails, that is a maintenance call — not a hand-propping invitation.
]]

## Risk Management

### AI.V.C.R1 — Propeller Safety

The spinning propeller is invisible and lethal. Position the airplane for the safety of nearby persons and property, do a final walk-around (tow bar, chocks, tiedowns removed; area clear, including **behind** the airplane), turn the **anti-collision beacon ON** (position lights at night), **set the brakes**, and call **"CLEAR"** out the window and **wait for a response** before cranking. Treat every propeller as if the ignition is live.

### AI.V.C.R2 — Use of External Power Unit

A dead battery and external power bring connection and procedure risks. Mitigate with the correct equipment and the AFM/POH procedure — never improvise.

### AI.V.C.R3 — Limitations During Starting

The starter duty cycle, the oil-pressure window, and the priming limits. Mitigate by honoring the crank/cool-down limits, watching the oil pressure, and shutting down on abnormal indications.

### Common Errors and Corrections

- **Not using the appropriate checklist:** follow the AFM/POH start checklist, read and do, every time.
- **Not using the safety precautions:** the beacon, the area check, the brakes, and the "CLEAR" call are not optional.
- **Improper adjustment of the engine controls during start:** know the priming and control settings for the conditions and the POH.
- **Not assuring the propeller is clear:** the area around and behind the airplane must be checked before cranking.
- **Stopping the cranking on a fire:** follow the POH — on many airplanes, continue cranking.

## Skills — Teaching the Task

The ACS skill elements require the applicant to **position the airplane** and **complete the checklist**:

- **AI.V.C.S1 — Position the airplane:** the learner positions the aircraft with regard to structures, other aircraft, wind, and the safety of nearby persons and property, and confirms the area and propeller are clear.
- **AI.V.C.S2 — Complete the checklist:** the learner performs the engine-start checklist, manipulates the engine controls for the conditions, verifies the indications after start, and explains the response to an engine fire and to a difficult start.
