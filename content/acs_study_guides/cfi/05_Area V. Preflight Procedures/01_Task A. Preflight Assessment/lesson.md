# Task A. Preflight Assessment

**Area V — Preflight Procedures**

## Objective

Determine that the applicant understands preflight assessment, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate the required skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

A safe flight begins before the engine starts. Mechanics forget to attach items, wind damages airplanes sitting on the ramp, and fuel and fluids leak — the preflight inspection is how the pilot catches them. A flight instructor must be able to perform a thorough preflight assessment, teach the learner what is normal and abnormal for the specific airplane, and model the self-assessment and judgment that decide whether the flight goes.

This is a ground and aircraft Task. The ACS skill elements require the applicant to **inspect the airplane with a checklist, verify it is in condition for safe flight, perform a self-assessment, and continue to assess the environment**.

## Schedule / Equipment

- Ground: 10 minutes at the airplane
- Initial: 15 minutes before flight — introduction and demonstration
- Every flight: 10 minutes before flight — learner performs
- PAVE checklist, the airplane checklist, and the airplane maintenance records
- The fuel-sump and inspection figures used in this lesson

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** explain the purpose of the preflight assessment and how to execute it, then perform it — follow the PAVE checklist, verify the airworthiness paperwork, follow the preflight inspection checklist, inspect the aircraft's systems, note any defects, and apply sound judgment on airworthiness.

**Instructor:** deliver the ground lesson, demonstrate the procedure at the airplane, and debrief after each flight.

## Completion Standards

- **Ground:** the learner can explain the purpose of the preflight assessment and how to execute it properly.
- **Before every flight:** the learner can perform the preflight assessment — follow the PAVE checklist; determine the airworthiness paperwork (inspections, ADs) is correct and current; follow the preflight-inspection checklist; inspect the flight controls, fuselage, engine, fuel, wheels/brakes, lights, pitot/static system, and antennas; and note any defects, using sound judgment to determine airworthiness.

### Purpose

Preflight assessment answers one question:

**Can this flight be conducted safely with this pilot, this airplane, and these conditions?**

Use **PAVE** to tell the story:

- **P — Pilot**
- **A — Aircraft**
- **V — enVironment**
- **E — External pressures**

Then consider **aviation security** before departure.

---

### P — Pilot

Start with the pilot.

Use **IMSAFE**:

- **I — Illness:** Am I sick or recovering?
- **M — Medication:** Am I taking anything that could impair me?
- **S — Stress:** Is stress affecting concentration or judgment?
- **A — Alcohol:** Am I fully free of its effects and within the regulation?
- **F — Fatigue:** Did I get enough sleep, and am I alert?
- **E — Emotion:** Am I angry, anxious, distracted, or emotionally preoccupied?

Then ask:

- Am I current?
- Am I proficient for this airplane and these conditions?
- Am I within my personal minimums?

Being legal does not automatically mean being fit for this flight.

---

### A — Aircraft

Now determine whether the airplane is:

1. Appropriate for the mission.
2. Legally airworthy.
3. Properly equipped.
4. Physically in condition for safe flight.

### Is the Airplane Appropriate?

Consider:

- Occupants and baggage.
- Weight and balance.
- Fuel capacity.
- Runway length.
- Density altitude.
- Terrain.
- Weather.
- Required navigation and communication equipment.

An airplane may be airworthy and still be inappropriate for the mission.

### Airworthiness — §91.7

The PIC is responsible for determining that the aircraft is in condition for safe flight.

Airworthiness requires that the airplane:

- Conform to its approved type design or properly altered configuration.
- Be in condition for safe operation.

### Required Inspections — AV1ATE

Use **AV1ATE** to organize recurring inspections and checks:

- **A — Annual:** within the preceding 12 calendar months. **§91.409**
- **V — VOR:** checked within the preceding 30 days when used for IFR navigation. **§91.171**
- **1 — 100-hour:** when required by **§91.409(b)**
- **A — Altimeter/static:** within 24 calendar months for the applicable IFR operation. **§91.411**
- **T — Transponder:** within 24 calendar months when required. **§91.413**
- **E — ELT:** inspection and battery requirements. **§91.207**

The mnemonic helps organize the review; the regulation determines whether each item applies.

### Required Equipment — §91.205

For **day VFR**, use **A TOMATO FLAMES**:

- **A — Airspeed indicator**
- **T — Tachometer** for each engine
- **O — Oil pressure gauge** for each engine using a pressure system
- **M — Manifold pressure gauge** for each altitude engine
- **A — Altimeter**
- **T — Temperature gauge** for each liquid-cooled engine
- **O — Oil temperature gauge** for each air-cooled engine
- **F — Fuel quantity gauge** for each tank
- **L — Landing gear position indicator**, if retractable
- **A — Anti-collision light system**, when required
- **M — Magnetic direction indicator**
- **E — ELT**, when required
- **S — Safety belts and shoulder harnesses**, as applicable

Then ask whether additional equipment is required by:

- Night VFR.
- IFR.
- POH/AFM.
- KOEL or equipment list.
- Type design.
- AD.
- Other operating rules.

### Inoperative Equipment — §91.213

If something is inoperative, determine whether the flight may legally and safely continue.

Check whether the item is required by:

- §91.205.
- Type design.
- POH/AFM or KOEL.
- AD.
- Other operating rule.

If it is required, resolve the discrepancy before flight.

### Documents in the Airplane

Verify the documents actually required for the operation:

- **Airworthiness certificate** — displayed at the cabin or cockpit entrance so it is legible to passengers or crew. **§91.203**
- **Registration certificate** — current and aboard. **§91.203**
- **Operating limitations** — approved AFM/POH, markings, and placards applicable to the airplane. **§91.9**
- **Current weight-and-balance information** — applicable to the aircraft configuration.
- **FCC radio station license** — when required for international operations.

Maintenance records are normally **not carried in the airplane**; review them or an appropriate status summary before flight when determining inspection and airworthiness status.

---

### Physical Preflight Inspection

Now answer a different question:

**The paperwork is good—but is the airplane physically safe to fly today?**

Use the **aircraft-specific POH/AFM checklist**.

For each item, teach:

- What am I checking?
- Why does it matter?
- What defect am I looking for?

### Start While Approaching the Airplane

Look at the whole airplane before touching it.

Look for:

- Flat or low tire.
- Uneven landing gear.
- Fluid beneath the airplane.
- Structural distortion.
- Open panels or doors.
- Wind or ground-handling damage.
- Signs of tampering.

### Cabin

Verify:

- Airworthiness certificate.
- Registration.
- POH/AFM and required placards.
- Current weight-and-balance information.
- Control lock removed.
- Ignition and electrical switches positioned as required by the checklist.
- Fuel selector positioned as required.
- Circuit breakers or fuses appear normal.
- Seats and seat rails secure.
- Safety belts and shoulder harnesses serviceable.
- Doors and windows secure and serviceable.
- Required emergency or survival equipment aboard, when applicable.

### Ground Equipment

Remove or account for:

- Tie-downs.
- Chocks.
- Control locks.
- Pitot cover.
- Engine or inlet plugs.
- Tow bar.
- Protective covers.

### Flight Controls

Inspect:

- Ailerons.
- Elevator or stabilator.
- Rudder.
- Trim tabs.
- Flaps.
- Hinges.
- Control rods, cables, and attachment hardware.

Look for:

- Free and correct movement.
- Binding.
- Excessive play.
- Missing hardware.
- Cracks.
- Bent or damaged surfaces.

### Structure

Look for:

- Dents.
- Cracks.
- Wrinkles.
- Buckling.
- Loose or missing fasteners.
- Separation or delamination.
- Unusual staining.

If a defect is not understood, stop and get qualified help.

### Propeller and Engine

Inspect the propeller for:

- Nicks.
- Cracks.
- Dents.
- Erosion.
- Spinner security.
- Oil leakage, if applicable.

Inspect accessible engine items for:

- Oil or fuel leaks.
- Damaged hoses.
- Loose components.
- Belt condition.
- Air-filter condition.
- Exhaust damage.
- Foreign material.
- Cowling security.

### Oil

Verify:

- Quantity per the POH.
- Correct grade/type.
- Cap or dipstick secure.
- No unexplained leakage.

### Fuel

Verify **quantity, grade, and quality**.

#### Quantity

Use the approved method for the airplane:

- Visual check.
- Calibrated dipstick.
- Known fuel load.
- Gauges as supporting information.

#### Grade

Confirm the fuel is approved for the airplane.

Do not rely only on:

- Truck location.
- Line-service assumption.
- Color.

#### Quality

Drain the required sumps and strainer in the POH sequence.

Look for:

- Water.
- Sediment.
- Debris.
- Wrong fuel.
- Unusual appearance.

If contamination persists:

**do not fly until the cause is resolved.**

Also verify:

- Fuel caps secure.
- Fuel vents clear.

### Landing Gear and Brakes

Inspect:

- Tire inflation.
- Tread.
- Cuts or bulges.
- Exposed cord.
- Struts.
- Brake condition.
- Hydraulic leaks.
- Wheel hardware.

### Pitot-Static System

Verify:

- Pitot cover removed.
- Pitot opening clear.
- Static ports unobstructed.
- Drains clear when applicable.

### Lights and Antennas

Inspect applicable:

- Position lights.
- Anti-collision lights.
- Landing light.
- Taxi light.
- Antennas.

Determine whether any inoperative item affects legal or safe operation.

### Ice, Frost, and Snow

Do not depart with contamination that makes the airplane unsafe.

Ice, frost, or snow can:

- Reduce lift.
- Increase drag.
- Increase stall speed.
- Degrade control.

Remove contamination in accordance with the aircraft guidance.

### Baggage and Cargo

Verify:

- Actual loading matches the weight-and-balance plan.
- Cargo is in the intended compartment.
- Everything is secured.
- Nothing can interfere with the flight controls.

### Oxygen System

If installed and applicable:

verify:

- Quantity or pressure.
- Masks or cannulas.
- Hoses and connections.
- General condition.

---

### V — enVironment

Now ask:

**Can this pilot and airplane safely operate in today's environment?**

Assess:

- Weather.
- Airports.
- Airspace.
- Terrain.
- Obstacles.
- Route.
- Runway conditions.
- NOTAMs.

### Weather

Consider:

- Ceiling.
- Visibility.
- Surface wind and gusts.
- Winds aloft.
- Convective activity.
- Turbulence.
- Icing risk.
- Temperature and density altitude.
- Weather trend.

### Terrain, Route, and Obstacles

Review:

- Terrain elevation.
- Towers and obstructions.
- Emergency landing options.
- Airspace.
- Suitable airports.
- Fuel stops.
- Weather along the route.

The shortest route is not always the safest route.

### Continue the Assessment

The ACS requires the environment to be assessed **continuously**.

After departure, continue monitoring:

- Weather.
- Wind.
- Fuel.
- Airport status.
- Runway availability.
- Airspace.
- Aircraft performance.

If the environment changes, change the plan.

---

### E — External Pressures

Identify pressures before they influence the decision:

- Schedule.
- Passenger expectations.
- Work obligations.
- Reservations.
- Cost.
- Desire to get home.

Ask:

**Would I make the same decision if nobody were waiting on me?**

If not, external pressure is affecting the decision.

---

### Aviation Security

Before flight, look for:

- Evidence of tampering.
- Unauthorized access.
- Missing or disturbed equipment.
- Unexplained damage.
- Suspicious activity around the airplane.

Investigate anything unexplained before departure.

---

### Distractions During the Preflight

If interrupted during the walkaround:

- Return to a known point in the checklist.
- Repeat enough of the sequence to know nothing was missed.

Do not guess where you left off.

---

### Go / No-Go Decision

Finish by bringing PAVE back together.

**Pilot**
- Am I fit and proficient?

**Aircraft**
- Is it appropriate?
- Are inspections current?
- Is required equipment installed and operable?
- Are inoperative items properly resolved?
- Did it pass the physical inspection?

**Environment**
- Are weather, airports, airspace, terrain, route, and obstacles acceptable?

**External pressures**
- Am I being pushed toward a decision I would not otherwise make?

**Security**
- Is there any unexplained concern?

If an important question remains unresolved:

**do not depart until it is resolved.**

---

### Common Errors

- Skipping pilot self-assessment.
- Treating PAVE as a recitation instead of a decision tool.
- Forgetting which AV1ATE items apply.
- Treating A TOMATO FLAMES as the entire airworthiness determination.
- Failing to resolve inoperative equipment under §91.213.
- Using the wrong checklist.
- Rushing or interrupting the preflight sequence.
- Failing to recognize abnormal conditions.
- Trusting fuel gauges without verification.
- Failing to verify fuel grade or contamination.
- Missing control locks, pitot covers, tiedowns, or chocks.
- Accepting ice or frost.
- Failing to secure baggage and cargo.
- Ignoring an unexplained discrepancy.
- Allowing external pressure to influence the decision.
- Treating assessment as complete after takeoff.

## AI.V.A.K1 — Pilot Self-Assessment

The first item on the preflight is the **pilot**. Before the airplane, the pilot honestly answers **IMSAFE** — illness, medication, stress, alcohol, fatigue, and emotion — and considers whether they are fit and proficient for this flight. A pilot who is impaired, fatigued, or pressured is a hazard no airworthy airplane can fix. The self-assessment is part of the **PAVE** checklist and is the pilot's own gate on the flight.

## AI.V.A.K2 — Determining the Airplane Is Appropriate and Airworthy

"Airworthy" means the aircraft **conforms to its type design and is in condition for safe operation** (14 CFR § 3.5). The owner/operator is responsible for maintenance, but **§ 91.7 makes the pilot in command responsible for determining the aircraft is in condition for safe flight**. Before the walkaround, the pilot inspects the **airworthiness status** in the maintenance records or a summary, confirming:

- **Annual inspection** within the preceding 12 calendar months (§ 91.409(a)).
- **100-hour inspection** if operated for hire (§ 91.409(b)).
- **Transponder** within the preceding 24 calendar months (§ 91.413).
- **Static system/encoder** within 24 calendar months for IFR (§ 91.411); **30-day VOR check** for IFR VOR navigation (§ 91.171).
- **ELT** inspection within 12 months and the battery status (§ 91.207).
- **Life-limited parts, AD compliance, and FAA Form 337** major repairs (§ 91.417), and the status of any **inoperative equipment** (§ 91.213).

Then confirm the **documents on board** — **ARROW**: airworthiness certificate, registration, radio station license (international/over 12,500 lb), operating limitations (POH/AFM and placards), and current weight-and-balance data.

## AI.V.A.K3 — Airplane Preflight Inspection

The visual preflight mitigates flight hazards. **Use the AFM/POH checklist** — or a third-party checklist based on it — in the manufacturer's sequence, every time. Begin approaching the airplane: note the **general appearance** — landing-gear alignment, distortions of the wings/fuselage/tail, skin damage, and any staining, drips, or puddles of fuel or oil.

Teach the learner to inspect each system and to know **which items to inspect** (K3a), **why** each item matters (K3b), **how to detect a defect** (K3c), and the **associated regulations** (K3d). The major areas:

- **Cabin and documents** — door and latches, seats latched in the rails, belts and harnesses, clean undamaged windows, control locks removed, switches set.
- **Fuselage and flight controls** — ailerons, elevator, rudder, trim tabs, and flaps; hinges, control rods/cables, and **proper free movement**; the stall indicator; cracks, missing screws, and bent or crumpled areas; **no ice or frost permitted**; removal of chocks and tiedowns; the baggage area secured. (Flight-control systems are covered in PHAK Chapter 6.)
- **Engine and propeller** — oil level (checked each preflight and rechecked at refueling), belts, visible leaks, the air filter, the exhaust system, and **propeller dings** (a ding you can catch a fingernail on is a defect).
- **Fuel** — verify **quantity** (visually or with the dipstick), **quality and grade** (color), and check the **sumps and the vent**. Water appears as clear or muddy droplets in the bottom of the sample; sediment appears as dark specks. Drain each sump and the strainer per the POH's order, before flight and after refueling; keep tanks full to limit condensation. If contamination is found, keep sampling until clean — and ground the airplane for maintenance if it persists.

- **Wheels, tires, and brakes** — proper inflation, tread and normal wear, no cuts/bulges/cords, shock struts clean and properly inflated, and no leaking hydraulic fluid. Any leaking fluid is unacceptable.

## AI.V.A.K4 — Environmental Factors

The preflight assessment extends beyond the airplane to the **environment** — the **weather**, the **terrain**, the **route selection**, and the **obstructions** for this flight. This assessment continues: it does not end when the walkaround does. Assess the environment for safe flight before departure and keep assessing it en route — a deteriorating ceiling, a rising wind, or a closing runway is a reason to change the plan.

## Risk Management

Run the whole risk picture with **PAVE**:

- **AI.V.A.R1 — Pilot:** the IMSAFE self-assessment; fitness and proficiency for this flight.
- **AI.V.A.R2 — Aircraft:** airworthy and appropriate — documents, inspections, ADs, and the physical inspection.
- **AI.V.A.R3 — Environment:** weather, terrain, airspace, and obstructions.
- **AI.V.A.R4 — External pressures:** the schedule, the passengers, the get-there pressure — recognized and set aside.
- **AI.V.A.R5 — Aviation security:** secure the aircraft and be alert to tampering or unauthorized access.

**Accept no unnecessary risk.** The go/no-go decision belongs to the pilot and favors safety over the schedule.

### Common Errors and Corrections

- **Not running a PAVE checklist before flight:** make PAVE the first preflight item, every flight.
- **Not using — or misusing — the checklist:** follow the POH checklist in sequence, every time; a checklist read from memory misses items.
- **Letting a distraction interrupt the inspection:** if interrupted, restart that step.
- **Failing to recognize a discrepancy:** know what normal looks like for the specific airplane; when in doubt, ground it.
- **Not verifying the fuel and oil:** confirm the grade, quantity, and cap security yourself.
- **Not securing baggage and loose items:** secure the baggage area, cargo, and equipment.

## Skills — Teaching the Task

The ACS skill elements require the applicant to **inspect, verify, self-assess, and assess the environment**:

- **AI.V.A.S1 — Inspect with a checklist:** the learner performs a full preflight inspection at the airplane using the POH checklist.
- **AI.V.A.S2 — Verify condition for safe flight:** the learner confirms the aircraft conforms to its type design and is airworthy.
- **AI.V.A.S3 — Perform self-assessment:** the learner runs IMSAFE and PAVE aloud.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

1. What makes an aircraft "airworthy," and who determines it before flight?

[[answer:
Airworthy means the aircraft conforms to its type design and is in condition for safe operation (§ 3.5). The owner/operator maintains it, but § 91.7 makes the pilot in command responsible for determining the aircraft is in condition for safe flight. The pilot verifies the airworthiness status and performs the inspection.
]]

2. What records and inspections does the pilot verify before flight?

[[answer:
Annual inspection (12 calendar months, § 91.409(a)); 100-hour if for hire (§ 91.409(b)); transponder (24 calendar months, § 91.413); static system/encoder for IFR (§ 91.411) and 30-day VOR check for IFR VOR (§ 91.171); ELT (12 months and battery, § 91.207); life-limited parts, AD compliance, and Form 337 major repairs (§ 91.417); and the status of any inoperative equipment (§ 91.213).
]]

3. What documents must be on board?

[[answer:
ARROW — Airworthiness certificate, Registration, Radio station license (international/over 12,500 lb), Operating limitations (POH/AFM and placards), and current Weight and balance data.
]]

4. What are the major areas of the preflight inspection, and what are you looking for?

[[answer:
Cabin/documents; fuselage and flight controls (free movement, no damage, no ice or frost); engine and propeller (oil level, leaks, propeller dings); fuel (quantity, grade by color, sumps and vent — water and sediment); wheels/tires/brakes (inflation, wear, no leaks); lights; pitot/static (cover off, port clear); antennas; and oxygen if applicable. The point is to know what normal looks like so the abnormal stands out.
]]

5. How do you check the fuel, and what do contaminants look like?

[[answer:
Verify quantity directly (visually or by dipstick — not just the gauges), confirm the grade by color, and drain each sump and the strainer in the POH's order, before flight and after refueling. Water appears as clear or muddy droplets in the bottom of the sample; sediment as dark specks. If contamination appears, keep sampling until clean, and ground the airplane for maintenance if it persists. Keep tanks full to limit condensation.
]]

6. What are the elements of the PAVE checklist?

[[answer:
Pilot (fitness — IMSAFE), Aircraft (airworthy and appropriate), Environment (weather, terrain, airspace, obstacles), and External pressures (schedule, passengers). Run PAVE before every flight, and accept no unnecessary risk.
]]

7. Why is a checklist not enough by itself?

[[answer:
A checklist is a tool, not a substitute for judgment. The preflight is looking for what is wrong, not reading items. The pilot must know what is normal for the specific airplane, restart a step if a distraction interrupts it, and ground the airplane when a discrepancy cannot be resolved.
]]

8. How does the environmental assessment work, and when does it end?

[[answer:
Assess the weather, terrain, route selection, and obstructions before departure — and continue the assessment en route. It does not end with the walkaround; a deteriorating ceiling, rising wind, or closing runway is a reason to change the plan.
]]

## Completion Standard

The learner explains the purpose of the preflight assessment and performs it: runs PAVE and the IMSAFE self-assessment, verifies the airworthiness paperwork and documents, follows the POH preflight checklist, inspects the aircraft's systems and detects defects with sound judgment, and continues to assess the environment — determining, for a given scenario, that the flight can be conducted safely or must be delayed or canceled.
