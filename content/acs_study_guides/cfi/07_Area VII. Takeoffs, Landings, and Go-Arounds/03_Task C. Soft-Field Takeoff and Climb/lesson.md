# Task C. Soft-Field Takeoff and Climb

**Area VII - Takeoffs, Landings, and Go-Arounds**

## Objective

To determine the applicant understands soft-field takeoff and climb, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate appropriate skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

A soft- or rough-field takeoff reduces the time the airplane bears weight on wheels exposed to mud, sand, snow, tall grass, ruts, and surface irregularities. The goal is not to pull the airplane off and climb immediately. It is to transfer weight to the wings, use ground effect to accelerate, and climb only after a safe speed is established.

The instructor applicant must teach the difference between a **soft-field** procedure and a **short-field** procedure, protect the nosewheel during taxi and takeoff, maintain directional control, and reject the takeoff before an unexpectedly soft surface becomes an airborne performance emergency.

## Schedule / Equipment

- **Ground instruction:** 25-30 minutes.
- **Flight instruction:** 0.6-1.0 hour, only on a suitable approved soft/rough surface or with an appropriate simulation.
- Current weather, NOTAMs, airport/field information, POH/AFM, performance data, checklist, and kneeboard.
- Whiteboard/model airplane and applicable Flight Guide visual references.

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** Inspect the selected surface, calculate/brief performance and departure path, identify V<sub>X</sub>/V<sub>Y</sub>, configure per the POH/AFM, maintain continuous motion and nosewheel protection, then explain ground-effect acceleration and the obstacle/no-obstacle climb transition.

**Instructor:** Follow the Binns progression of soft-surface assessment, nosewheel protection, ground effect, performance, V-speeds, left-turning tendencies, runway safety, and abnormal operations. Demonstrate the maneuver while explaining the outside references, control pressures, and abort decision.

## Completion Standards

The learner explains the purpose, procedure, performance effects, V<sub>X</sub>/V<sub>Y</sub>, configuration, ground effect, weight transfer, left-turning tendencies, and common errors; manages the stated risks; and demonstrates the applicable ACS skills, including selected V<sub>X</sub> or V<sub>Y</sub> within **+/-5 knots** to a safe maneuvering altitude.

### Objective

**Teach the learner to safely plan and perform a soft-field takeoff and climb by protecting the landing gear, transferring weight from the wheels to the wings as early as practical, lifting off at the lowest practical airspeed, accelerating in ground effect, and then climbing at V<sub>X</sub> or V<sub>Y</sub> as appropriate while maintaining directional control and wind correction.**

### Purpose

A soft-field takeoff solves a different problem than a normal or short-field takeoff.

On a soft or rough surface, the airplane may be fighting:

- grass,
- mud,
- sand,
- snow,
- ruts,
- standing water,
- or uneven terrain.

Those conditions increase rolling resistance and can damage or bog down the landing gear.

So the goal is:

**get the weight off the wheels and onto the wings as early as practical — but do not climb away until the airplane has enough airspeed.**

That gives us the instructional story:

**inspect and plan → keep moving → protect the nosewheel → transfer weight to the wings → lift off early → stay in ground effect → accelerate → climb at V<sub>X</sub> or V<sub>Y</sub> → configure and continue the departure.**

---

### Start With the Surface — Is This Takeoff Even a Good Idea?

Before the technique matters, decide whether the surface is suitable.

Under **14 CFR §91.103**, the PIC must become familiar with the available information necessary for the flight. For a soft-field departure, that means looking beyond runway length and considering the actual condition of the surface.

Inspect or evaluate:

- **Firmness**
- **Grass length**
- **Mud / snow / sand**
- **Standing water**
- **Ruts / potholes**
- **Rocks / debris**
- **Slope**
- **Usable length**
- **Obstacles**
- **Wind**
- **Density altitude**
- **Aircraft weight**

A soft field can dramatically increase takeoff distance, and the published paved-runway number may not represent what the airplane will actually do on grass, mud, or another unprepared surface.

The planning question is:

**“Can I accelerate, lift off, remain in ground effect, and still have enough room and performance to climb safely?”**

If the answer is uncertain:

**do not use technique to compensate for an unsafe surface.**

---

### Soft Field Is Not Short Field

Make this distinction early.

A **short-field takeoff** is about **minimum distance and obstacle clearance**.

A **soft-field takeoff** is about **reducing wheel loading and rolling resistance**, then using ground effect to accelerate.

The techniques can overlap, especially when a soft field also has an obstacle, but the purpose is different.

For soft field:

**get light on the wheels → fly off early → accelerate in ground effect → then climb.**

---

### C172S Airspeeds Used in This Lesson

Use these POH values unless the aircraft-specific POH in use says otherwise:

- **Normal climb out:** 75–85 KIAS
- **V<sub>Y</sub> — Best rate:** 74 KIAS sea level → 72 KIAS at 10,000 ft
- **V<sub>X</sub> — Best angle:** 62 KIAS sea level → 67 KIAS at 10,000 ft
- **Short-field takeoff, flaps 10°, at 50 ft:** 56 KIAS
- **Normal approach, flaps up:** 65–75 KIAS
- **Normal approach, flaps 30°:** 60–70 KIAS
- **Short-field approach, flaps 30°:** 61 KIAS
- **Balked landing, max power, flaps 20°:** 60 KIAS
- **Maximum demonstrated crosswind:** 15 knots

---

### C172S Configuration

For the **C172S**, the POH calls for:

- **10° flaps** for soft or rough field takeoffs.
- Lift off **as soon as practical** in a **slightly tail-low attitude**.
- If no obstacle is ahead, **level off immediately** and accelerate to a higher climb speed.
- If 10° flaps are used, leave them down until:
    - obstacles are clear, and
    - at least **60 KIAS** is reached before retracting.
- With an **aft CG**, the POH recommends adjusting elevator trim somewhat **nose-down** to give more comfortable control forces during the initial climb.

Flaps greater than **10° are not approved for takeoff**.

The POH controls the airplane-specific technique.

---

### Keep the Airplane Moving

The soft-field technique begins before the takeoff roll.

Stopping on a soft surface may let the wheels sink or bog down.

So, when conditions permit:

**maintain continuous motion.**

That means:

- Use only the braking you actually need.
- Keep turns shallow.
- Keep the nosewheel light.
- Plan the lineup so you do not stop unnecessarily on the takeoff surface.

This directly supports the ACS requirement to taxi into takeoff position and align on centerline **without stopping** while maintaining the required control inputs.

---

### Protect the Nosewheel

In a tricycle-gear airplane, the nosewheel is usually the most vulnerable gear component on a soft surface.

Use **back-elevator pressure** to reduce nosewheel loading while taxiing and during the beginning of the takeoff roll.

The goal is not to drag the tail.

The goal is to:

**increase angle of attack enough to transfer weight from the wheels to the wings as lift develops.**

That weight transfer is a core ACS knowledge item.

As speed increases and the elevator becomes more effective, adjust back pressure so the nosewheel stays light without creating an excessive pitch attitude.

---

### Wind Controls Still Apply

Soft-field technique does not replace crosswind technique.

Before the roll:

**position the flight controls for the existing wind.**

With a crosswind:

- Start with **aileron into the wind**.
- Use **rudder** to maintain centerline.
- Reduce aileron only as needed as control effectiveness increases.

Do not let the desire to get airborne early cause:

- side-skipping,
- loss of centerline,
- or the upwind wing to lift.

---

### Align Without Stopping — Then Add Power Smoothly

Once the runway or takeoff path is clear:

- make the appropriate radio call,
- verify the correct runway,
- clear final and the departure path,
- and align on centerline **without stopping** when safe and practical.

Advance the throttle **smoothly to takeoff power**.

As power comes in, verify:

- **Takeoff power**
- **Engine indications normal**
- **Airspeed alive**
- **Acceleration reasonable**
- **Directional control maintained**

If the engine is rough, acceleration is abnormal, or directional control is deteriorating:

**reject the takeoff while stopping is still the safer option.**

---

### Anticipate Left-Turning Tendencies

Soft-field takeoff combines:

- high power,
- low airspeed,
- relatively high angle of attack.

That makes left-turning tendencies more noticeable.

Expect:

- torque,
- spiraling slipstream,
- P-factor,
- gyroscopic effects where applicable.

The correction is not a memorized amount of right rudder.

It is:

**use the rudder required to keep the airplane aligned and coordinated.**

---

### Transfer Weight From the Wheels to the Wings

As the airplane accelerates, maintain a pitch attitude that gets the nosewheel light and progressively transfers weight from the landing gear to the wings.

This is the heart of the maneuver.

The airplane should feel lighter on the surface as lift builds.

Do not:

- hold so little back pressure that the nosewheel stays heavily loaded,
- or hold so much that the airplane develops excessive drag or an extreme nose-high attitude.

The ACS skill is to establish the pitch attitude that transfers the weight **as rapidly as practical** from wheels to wings.

---

### Let the Airplane Lift Off Early — Then Stay in Ground Effect

The airplane may become airborne **before normal rotation speed**.

That is expected.

The C172S POH says to lift off as soon as practical in a **slightly tail-low attitude**.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Takeoff_Soft_Field.png" alt="Soft-field takeoff showing early nosewheel lift, liftoff, ground-effect acceleration, and climb" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>Soft-field takeoff — lift off early, remain in ground effect while accelerating, and only then begin the climb at the selected V<sub>X</sub> or V<sub>Y</sub>.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is the key sequence:

**lift off early → stay low in ground effect → accelerate to V<sub>X</sub> or V<sub>Y</sub> → then pitch for the climb and climb away.**

Do **not** lift off and immediately begin climbing.

The airplane may be airborne only because ground effect has reduced induced drag. Until it reaches the selected climb speed, it may not have enough performance to climb safely away from the surface.

---

### Ground Effect — Accelerate First, Climb Second

Once the main wheels leave the surface:

**briskly but smoothly reduce backpressure enough to hold the airplane in ground effect — about 10 feet AGL in this training technique — and accelerate.**

The sequence is deliberate:

1. **Main wheels lift off.**
2. **Lower the nose enough to stay in ground effect.**
3. **Hold the airplane low while airspeed builds.**
4. **Wait until the selected V<sub>X</sub> or V<sub>Y</sub> is reached.**
5. **Only then establish the climb attitude and climb out of ground effect.**

Do not push abruptly.

Do not climb several feet and then force the airplane back toward the surface.

The goal is a smooth, level-ish acceleration phase close to the runway.

Ground effect reduces induced drag close to the surface, allowing the airplane to accelerate more efficiently than it could while still rolling through a high-drag surface.

If the airplane climbs out of ground effect **before reaching the selected climb speed**:

- induced drag increases,
- climb performance may disappear,
- the airplane may **mush or settle**,
- or it may stall.

The teaching phrase should be:

**“Fly off early, stay low, accelerate, then climb.”**

---

### V<sub>X</sub> or V<sub>Y</sub>?

The next question is:

**“Which speed am I accelerating to before I climb out of ground effect?”**

If an obstacle controls the departure:

**stay in ground effect until V<sub>X</sub>, then pitch for V<sub>X</sub> and climb.**

For the C172S, V<sub>X</sub> is **62 KIAS at sea level**, increasing to **67 KIAS at 10,000 feet**.

If obstacles are clear:

**stay in ground effect until V<sub>Y</sub>, then pitch for V<sub>Y</sub> and climb.**

For the C172S, V<sub>Y</sub> is **74 KIAS at sea level**, decreasing to **72 KIAS at 10,000 feet**.

Remember:

- **V<sub>X</sub>** = greatest altitude gain per horizontal distance
- **V<sub>Y</sub>** = greatest altitude gain per unit of time

Maintain the selected speed within **±5 knots**.

Once the obstacle is clear:

**transition from V<sub>X</sub> to V<sub>Y</sub> or the manufacturer-recommended climb speed.**

---

### Leaving Ground Effect

As the airplane climbs out of ground effect, induced drag increases.

That means the airplane may require:

- a slightly different pitch attitude,
- continued full power,
- and deliberate airspeed control.

One common error is failing to anticipate the pitch/drag change as ground effect disappears.

Teach the learner to:

**set the climb attitude, verify the airspeed, and trim — do not chase the airspeed indicator.**

---

### Configure After Positive Climb / POH Point

Do not rush flap retraction.

For the C172S with 10° flaps:

- leave the flaps down until obstacles are clear,
- and at least **60 KIAS** before retracting.

The ACS requires configuration after a positive rate or in accordance with manufacturer guidance.

So the order is:

**aircraft control → climb established → obstacle clear / POH condition met → configure.**

---

### Continue to a Safe Maneuvering Altitude

After the airplane is established in the climb:

- maintain **V<sub>X</sub> or V<sub>Y</sub> ±5 knots** as appropriate,
- maintain coordination,
- correct for wind drift,
- continue the outside scan,
- and follow the intended departure/noise-abatement path.

Do not let the unusual soft-field sequence cause checklist or navigation items to be missed.

---

### Abnormal Operations — Reject Early

A soft field reduces the margin for indecision.

Reject for:

- **Abnormal engine indications**
- **Sluggish or inadequate acceleration**
- **Loss of directional control**
- **Unexpectedly soft/rough surface**
- **Runway or traffic conflict**
- **Any condition that makes continued takeoff unsafe**

The best time to reject is while the airplane still has room to stop.

---

### Engine Failure After Liftoff

If the engine fails after liftoff:

**aircraft control first.**

Immediately:

- lower the nose enough to protect airspeed,
- maintain coordination,
- select the best available landing area,
- and use the POH emergency procedure as time permits.

Do not try to preserve altitude by holding the nose up.

Do not attempt a turnback unless prior training, altitude, wind, runway geometry, and actual conditions support it.

---

### Collision and Runway-Incursion Risk

The soft-field technique creates extra workload, but runway safety does not change.

Before entering the runway:

- verify the **correct runway**,
- clear final,
- clear the runway,
- clear the departure path,
- and make the appropriate radio call.

At a towered airport, comply with the ATC clearance under **§91.123**.

At a nontowered airport, a CTAF call improves awareness but does not guarantee the runway is clear.

Continue scanning during ground-effect acceleration and initial climb.

---

### Crosswind, Tailwind, Windshear, Wake, and Surface

These risks compound the soft-field problem.

A **tailwind** increases ground roll and may make the departure unacceptable.

A **crosswind** increases directional-control demands while the airplane is light on the wheels.

**Windshear** can change airspeed and climb performance immediately after liftoff.

**Wake turbulence** may drift across the runway or departure path.

And surface condition may change along the runway.

If the combined risk is too high:

**use another runway, wait, reduce weight, or do not depart.**

---

### Low-Altitude Risk

The airplane may be airborne at very low airspeed.

That makes low-altitude pitch and coordination especially important.

Avoid:

- abrupt elevator inputs,
- excessive pitch,
- uncoordinated climb,
- early turns,
- or climbing out of ground effect before the airplane has enough energy.

A soft-field takeoff should look **smooth**, not dramatic.

---

### Common Errors

#### Planning / Setup

- **Failure to review POH/AFM and performance charts**
- **Failure to adequately clear the area**
- Poor surface assessment
- Failure to brief obstacle/no-obstacle climb plan

#### Takeoff Roll

- **Insufficient back-elevator pressure**
- **Failure to check engine instruments after applying power**
- **Poor directional control**
- Neutral or inadequate crosswind correction
- Excessive pitch causing drag or tail-strike risk

#### Liftoff / Ground Effect

- **Climbing too high after liftoff**
- **Abrupt or excessive elevator input while leveling**
- **Mushing or settling back onto the surface**
- **Climbing out of ground effect before reaching sufficient climb speed**

#### Initial Climb

- **Failure to anticipate the change as the airplane leaves ground effect**
- Wrong V<sub>X</sub>/V<sub>Y</sub> selection
- Poor coordination
- Premature configuration change
- Failure to maintain selected airspeed within tolerance

---

### Instructor Demonstration

Teach it in the exact order the airplane experiences it:

1. **Inspect and brief the surface** — condition, wind, performance, obstacle, abort plan.
2. **Configure per POH** — C172S: **10° flaps** for soft/rough field.
3. Maintain **continuous motion** and keep the **nosewheel light**.
4. Verify runway, traffic, wind, and departure path.
5. Align on centerline **without stopping** when safe.
6. Apply **smooth takeoff power**.
7. Call: **power normal — gauges normal — airspeed alive**.
8. Maintain centerline with rudder and wind correction with aileron.
9. Use enough back pressure to transfer weight to the wings and let the nosewheel lift.
10. Let the airplane **fly off at the lowest practical speed**.
11. With the wheels clear, **lower the nose enough to hold about 10 feet AGL in ground effect**.
12. **Remain in ground effect and accelerate** to:
    - **V<sub>X</sub>** if an obstacle controls, or
    - **V<sub>Y</sub>** if clear.
13. **Only after reaching that speed**, establish the climb attitude and climb out of ground effect, maintaining selected speed **±5 knots**.
14. Maintain coordination, directional control, and wind-drift correction.
15. Configure per POH after positive climb / obstacle-clearance criteria are met.
16. Continue to a safe maneuvering altitude.
17. Debrief the **first cue of any error** and the correction.

## AI.VII.C.K1 - Purpose of and Procedures for Soft-Field Takeoff and Climb

Soft- and rough-field procedures reduce drag and landing-gear stress from **mud, sand, snow, tall grass, ruts, and surface irregularities**. Inspect the surface whenever possible. A field that appears usable from a distance may conceal soft areas, potholes, debris, standing water, or inadequate length.

Complete planning and the checklist before entering the takeoff area. Maintain **continuous motion** while lining up because stopping in mud, snow, or deep grass can bog the airplane down. With the manufacturer-recommended configuration, apply takeoff power smoothly as the airplane aligns with the runway; use enough back-elevator to establish a positive angle of attack and relieve nosewheel weight.

Allow the airplane to lift off at the lowest possible airspeed, then **gently lower the nose with the wheels clear** and remain in ground effect while accelerating. Climb at V<sub>X</sub> when obstacle clearance is required; otherwise accelerate to and climb at V<sub>Y</sub>. Configure only after a positive rate and the manufacturer guidance permit.

## AI.VII.C.K2 - Effects of Atmospheric Conditions, Including Wind, on Takeoff and Climb Performance

Soft ground already reduces acceleration; high density altitude, high temperature, high weight, tailwind, upslope, wet grass, mud, snow, or a rough surface further reduce the margin. Use the POH/AFM performance data and recognize when the available data do not represent actual surface conditions. A calculated paved-runway takeoff distance is not automatically adequate for an unprepared surface.

Crosswind requires aileron into the wind and rudder to keep aligned; tailwind increases ground roll and may make the soft-field performance unacceptable. Gusts and windshear can reduce control or change climb performance near liftoff. Evaluate wake turbulence from larger aircraft and the drift/sink of its vortices before accepting the departure sequence.

## AI.VII.C.K3 - Best Angle of Climb Speed (V<sub>X</sub>) and Best Rate of Climb Speed (V<sub>Y</sub>)

**V<sub>X</sub>** is best angle of climb: the greatest altitude gain per horizontal distance. Use it after ground-effect acceleration when an obstacle controls the departure. **V<sub>Y</sub>** is best rate of climb: the greatest altitude gain per unit of time. Use it when obstacles are clear unless the POH/AFM specifies otherwise.

The actual values, configuration, and technique belong to the **specific POH/AFM**. A five-knot deviation can materially reduce climb performance in some airplanes, so establish the required pitch attitude with outside references and verify the indicated speed.

## AI.VII.C.K4 - Appropriate Airplane Configuration

Use the **POH/AFM** for flap setting, trim, fuel, mixture, propeller, cowl flaps, and other configuration items. Flaps may be recommended to increase lift and transfer weight to the wings earlier, but do not use flaps by habit where the manufacturer does not call for them.

Keep the airplane configured for the soft-field takeoff through ground-effect acceleration. After a positive rate of climb is established, retract gear/flaps according to the POH/AFM. If an obstacle requires V<sub>X</sub>, maintain the required configuration until clear unless manufacturer guidance states otherwise.

## AI.VII.C.K5 - Ground Effect

**Ground effect** occurs close to the surface, normally within approximately one wingspan, where reduced upwash, downwash, and wingtip vortices reduce induced drag. It allows an airplane to become airborne at a speed below a safe climb speed.

This is useful only when understood. After liftoff, lower the pitch enough to remain just above the surface and accelerate. Climbing away too early increases induced drag and required thrust; the airplane can **mush or settle** back toward the surface even at full power. Anticipate a pitch-up tendency and adjust smoothly as the airplane leaves ground effect.

## AI.VII.C.K6 - Importance of Weight Transfer From Wheels to Wings

The soft-field objective is to transfer support from the landing gear to the wings as rapidly as practical, reducing wheel drag, sinking, and structural stress. Back-elevator creates a positive angle of attack; as lift develops, the wings progressively unload the wheels.

For tricycle gear, keep the nosewheel light while taxiing and during the roll. Use only the brake action required for control because braking can add drag or sink the wheels into a soft surface. The pilot must still preserve directional control and avoid an excessive nose-high attitude that prevents acceleration.

## AI.VII.C.K7 - Left Turning Tendencies

At high power and low airspeed, left-turning tendencies are pronounced. **Torque reaction** rolls/loads the airplane opposite propeller rotation; on the ground, unequal main-wheel loading can contribute to left yaw. **Spiraling slipstream** strikes the vertical tail and produces yaw. **P-factor** shifts thrust asymmetrically at high angle of attack and yaws the nose left. **Gyroscopic precession** is especially noticeable in tailwheel airplanes when the tail is raised.

These forces vary with the airplane and condition. Use the rudder and other controls required to keep the airplane aligned, coordinated, and away from surface hazards. Do not apply a memorized amount of right rudder without responding to the actual airplane.

## AI.VII.C.K8 - Common Errors Related to This Task

### Planning, Scan, Power, and Directional Control

**Errors:** Failure to review AFM/POH and performance charts; failure to adequately clear the area; failure to cross-check engine instruments after power is applied; or poor directional control.

**Correction:** Inspect and brief the surface/obstacle/departure plan, complete the checklist before the runway, clear final and the departure path, apply smooth takeoff power, confirm engine indications, and use outside references with coordinated control.

### Weight Transfer and Ground-Effect Errors

**Errors:** Insufficient back-elevator resulting in inadequate angle of attack; climbing too high after liftoff instead of leveling low enough to remain in ground effect; abrupt/excessive elevator while leveling; or allowing the airplane to mush or settle into an inadvertent touchdown.

**Correction:** Establish a positive but controlled nose-high attitude to unload the wheels. After early liftoff, gently lower the nose with wheels clear, maintain the low ground-effect attitude, and allow acceleration with smooth controls.

### Premature Climb-Out

**Errors:** Attempting to climb out of ground effect before reaching sufficient climb speed, or failing to anticipate increased pitch attitude as ground effect decreases.

**Correction:** Remain in ground effect to at least the selected V<sub>X</sub> or V<sub>Y</sub>, then establish the climb attitude smoothly, maintain selected speed, and configure only per the POH/AFM.

## Risk Management

### AI.VII.C.R1 - Runway Selection, Performance, Available Distance, and Wind

Select a field only after considering pilot capability, aircraft limitations, wind, usable distance, slope, surface firmness, obstacles, density altitude, weight, and calculated performance. Do not attempt a soft-field takeoff on a surface that may damage the airplane or offers inadequate margin to accelerate, reject, and clear obstacles.

### AI.VII.C.R2 - Crosswind, Windshear, Tailwind, Wake Turbulence, and Runway Surface/Condition

Crosswind, tailwind, gusts, windshear, and wake turbulence compound the soft-surface hazard. Wet grass, mud, deep snow, sand, ruts, standing water, debris, and contaminated surfaces may greatly increase rolling resistance or impair directional control. Delay, use another runway/field, reduce weight, or cancel when the combined risk is unacceptable.

### AI.VII.C.R3 - Rejected Takeoff and Engine Failure After Liftoff

Brief a decisive abort point and the cues requiring rejection: inadequate acceleration, abnormal engine indication, loss of directional control, runway conflict, or a surface condition that prevents expected performance. Reject while a stop remains the safer plan.

After engine failure immediately after liftoff, **maintain aircraft control first**: lower the nose to protect airspeed, coordinate, and land toward a plausible area, normally ahead. Do not turn back without prior training, planning, adequate altitude, and suitable conditions.

### AI.VII.C.R4 - Collision Hazards

Clear final, the runway, and the departure path. At an uncontrolled field, aircraft may be NORDO; a CTAF call is not clearance or separation. Continue the scan through ground-effect acceleration and initial climb, where the nose-high attitude and surface demands compete for attention.

### AI.VII.C.R5 - Low Altitude Maneuvering, Stall, Spin, or CFIT

Do not pull the airplane out of ground effect before it has the energy to climb. A premature pitch-up can cause a mush, stall, or collision with surface/obstacles. Use the planned V<sub>X</sub>/V<sub>Y</sub> profile, maintain coordination, and avoid unplanned low-altitude turns.

### AI.VII.C.R6 - Distractions, Task Prioritization, Loss of Situational Awareness, or Disorientation

Complete noncritical tasks before entering the soft surface. Once moving, prioritize **aviate, navigate, communicate**. Do not troubleshoot an EFB, radio, or checklist item at the expense of field condition, centerline, acceleration, or obstacles; reject if the takeoff no longer has a safe margin.

## Skills

### AI.VII.C.S1-S6 - Checklist, Radio, Runway, Wind, Controls, and Continuous-Motion Positioning

Complete the checklist and required radio call; verify the correct runway, wind, and field condition; position controls for the wind; clear the area; and align on centerline **without stopping** while maintaining the required flight-control inputs and advancing power smoothly.

### AI.VII.C.S7-S9 - Power Check, Weight Transfer, Liftoff, and Ground-Effect Acceleration

Confirm takeoff power and expected engine/flight-instrument indications. Establish and maintain the pitch attitude that transfers weight from wheels to wings, lift off at the lowest possible airspeed, and remain in ground effect while accelerating to the selected V<sub>X</sub> or V<sub>Y</sub>.

### AI.VII.C.S10-S13 - Climb Attitude, Configuration, Airspeed, Directional Control, and Wind Drift

Establish the pitch attitude for the selected climb speed and maintain it within +/-5 knots. Configure after a positive rate or as the manufacturer directs. Maintain V<sub>X</sub> or V<sub>Y</sub>, as applicable, to a safe maneuvering altitude while maintaining centerline/extended-centerline alignment, coordination, and wind-drift correction.

### AI.VII.C.S14-S15 - Noise Abatement and Error Correction

Comply with published noise-abatement procedures unless safety, ATC, or an emergency requires otherwise. After each attempt, identify the error, the visual/aircraft cue that revealed it, and the correction to demonstrate on the next takeoff.

# Fly

## Setup

- Inspect the surface when possible: **firmness, moisture, grass length, ruts, rocks, debris, standing water, usable length, slope, obstacles, and turnaround/abort options**.
- Review the POH/AFM soft/rough-field procedure, flap setting, V<sub>X</sub>, V<sub>Y</sub>, performance data, and limitations for the actual airplane.
- Brief runway, wind, crosswind/tailwind, takeoff distance, surface condition, abort point, engine-failure plan, obstacle plan, and intended departure/noise-abatement path.
- Complete the checklist before entering. Clear final, runway, and departure path; obtain clearance/status and make the appropriate radio call.

## Instructor Demonstration

1. Maintain taxi momentum and control inputs appropriate for wind; avoid unnecessary braking and protect the nosewheel with back-elevator as appropriate.
2. Align with centerline **without stopping** when safe and practical. Apply takeoff power smoothly and promptly while holding enough back-elevator to establish a positive angle of attack and transfer weight to the wings.
3. Confirm engine indications, look far ahead, use rudder for directional control, and use aileron into a crosswind. Let the airplane fly itself off at the lowest possible speed.
4. With wheels clear, gently lower the nose to remain in ground effect and accelerate. Do not climb high, mush, or settle back onto the surface.
5. At V<sub>X</sub>, climb to clear obstacles when required; otherwise establish V<sub>Y</sub>. Maintain coordination, directional control, and wind-drift correction.
6. After positive rate and the manufacturer-recommended point, retract configuration as appropriate. Continue to a safe maneuvering altitude and comply with the departure/noise-abatement procedure.

## Learner Practice

Require the learner to state the surface condition, takeoff decision, V<sub>X</sub>/V<sub>Y</sub> plan, and abort point before every attempt. Begin on a suitable surface with conditions within learner capability; add crosswind only when control quality and surface margin support it.

Use safe verbal or high-altitude simulations for engine failure. Do not create a surprise at low altitude. Debrief whether the learner used correct control pressure, stayed in ground effect long enough, and transitioned to the correct climb speed without fixation.

## Instructor Demonstration Technique

Use a progressive verbal demonstration:

- **Taxi/lineup:** “Keep moving, nose light, wind controls, centerline, clear.”
- **Roll:** “Smooth power, gauges, positive angle of attack, rudder, look far ahead.”
- **Liftoff:** “Wheels clear, lower gently, stay in ground effect, accelerate.”
- **Climb:** “V<sub>X</sub> for obstacle or V<sub>Y</sub> when clear; trim, track, configure per POH.”

## Analyze and Correct Common Errors

### Insufficient Back-Elevator / Nosewheel Loading

**Recognize:** The nosewheel remains heavily loaded, the airplane bogs down, or the runway surface creates increasing drag.

**Correct:** Use the planned positive angle of attack to transfer weight to the wings while preserving directional control. Do not use brakes or an excessive pitch attitude to solve the problem.

### Climbing Out of Ground Effect Too Early

**Recognize:** The airplane climbs then loses energy, requires excessive back pressure, mushes, or settles toward the surface.

**Correct:** Gently lower the nose with wheels clear, remain in ground effect, accelerate to the selected safe climb speed, then establish the climb.

### Directional-Control Loss or Surface Excursion

**Recognize:** Centerline drift, increasing rudder inputs, side load, a rising upwind wing, or inability to maintain the planned path.

**Correct:** Use measured rudder and wind-correction inputs while looking ahead. Reject the takeoff while safe runway remains if control or acceleration is inadequate.

### Premature Configuration Change

**Recognize:** Flaps/gear are retracted before positive rate, obstacle clearance, or manufacturer guidance permits, causing a sink or loss of performance.

**Correct:** Fly the airplane first. Maintain the climb attitude and selected V-speed, then configure at the POH/AFM-recommended point.

## Completion Standards

The applicant demonstrates while teaching a soft-field takeoff and climb: suitable surface/performance selection, checklist/runway safety, wind-control inputs, continuous-motion lineup, smooth power/engine check, rapid weight transfer, low-speed liftoff, controlled ground-effect acceleration, V<sub>X</sub>/V<sub>Y</sub> +/-5 knots, proper configuration, drift correction, and common-error correction.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. What is the purpose of a soft-field takeoff?

[[answer:
It reduces the drag and landing-gear stress of a soft or rough surface by transferring weight from the wheels to the wings as soon as practical, lifting off at low speed, and using ground effect to accelerate before climbing.
]]

### 2. How is a soft-field procedure different from a short-field procedure?

[[answer:
Soft-field technique protects the landing gear and reduces surface drag through early weight transfer and ground-effect acceleration. A short-field takeoff uses the POH/AFM maximum-performance procedure to minimize ground roll and clear obstacles. The techniques are not interchangeable.
]]

### 3. Why should the airplane remain in ground effect after liftoff?

[[answer:
Ground effect reduces induced drag and lets the airplane accelerate after an early liftoff. Climbing away below a safe climb speed can cause the airplane to mush or settle back to the surface and may prevent obstacle clearance.
]]

### 4. When do you use V<sub>X</sub> and V<sub>Y</sub>?

[[answer:
Use V<sub>X</sub> after acceleration when obstacle clearance requires the greatest altitude per distance. Use V<sub>Y</sub> when obstacles are clear and the goal is the greatest altitude per time. Use the aircraft-specific POH/AFM values.
]]

### 5. How do you protect a tricycle-gear airplane on a soft surface?

[[answer:
Maintain continuous motion where practical, use enough back-elevator to keep the nosewheel light and transfer weight to the wings, minimize braking, and avoid surface hazards while retaining directional control.
]]

### 6. What errors occur when a pilot climbs out of ground effect too early?

[[answer:
The airplane can develop excessive drag, lose energy, mush, settle back to the surface, or be unable to clear obstacles. The correction is to maintain a low ground-effect attitude, accelerate to the selected safe climb speed, then establish the climb smoothly.
]]

### 7. What conditions require a rejected takeoff?

[[answer:
Inadequate acceleration, abnormal engine indications, loss of directional control, runway/traffic conflict, or unexpectedly soft/rough surface conditions require a rejection while stopping remains the safer choice.
]]

### 8. How do wind and left-turning tendencies affect this maneuver?

[[answer:
Wind requires appropriate aileron/rudder inputs to maintain centerline and departure track. At high power and low speed, torque reaction, gyroscopic precession, spiraling slipstream, and P-factor can demand significant rudder correction. Respond to the actual aircraft, not a rote control input.
]]
