# Task D. Soft-Field Approach and Landing

**Area VII - Takeoffs, Landings, and Go-Arounds**

## Objective

To determine the applicant understands soft-field approach and landing, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate appropriate skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

A soft-field landing is designed to protect the airplane and retain control on grass, mud, sand, snow, and other surfaces that can add drag, damage landing gear, or trap a nosewheel. The goal is a **stabilized, on-speed approach**, a minimum-sink touchdown on the main wheels, and continued back-elevator pressure until it is safe to taxi clear and shut down.

For the instructor applicant, the key teaching distinction is that soft-field does **not** mean an uncontrolled float or an attempt to land slowly with insufficient energy. It requires accurate performance planning, an intentional energy state, judicious power through touchdown, and a prepared go-around if the selected portion of the field cannot be reached safely.

## Schedule / Equipment

- **Ground instruction:** 25-30 minutes.
- **Flight instruction:** 0.6-1.0 hour, only on a suitable approved soft/rough surface or with an appropriate simulation.
- Current weather, NOTAMs, airport/field information, POH/AFM, performance data, checklist, and kneeboard.
- Whiteboard/model airplane and applicable Flight Guide visual references.

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** Assess surface and landing performance, brief the approach/go-around/taxi plan, establish a stabilized soft-field approach using the POH/AFM, use small power as needed through the flare, protect the nosewheel after touchdown, and maintain taxi momentum/control on the soft surface.

**Instructor:** Use the Binns teaching flow of surface assessment, nosewheel protection, trim and energy management, stabilized approach, ground effect, float prevention, performance, safety, and the step-by-step landing. Demonstrate the maneuver while explaining power, pitch, crosswind correction, and timely go-around decisions.

## Completion Standards

The learner explains soft-field purpose/procedure, stabilization and energy management, atmospheric effects, wind correction, and common errors; manages the stated risks; and demonstrates the applicable ACS skills: a minimum-sink, no-side-drift touchdown at the proper pitch attitude, continued manufacturer-recommended elevator use through rollout, and safe soft-surface taxi.

### Objective

**Teach the learner to plan and fly a soft-field approach and landing using a stabilized, on-speed approach; manage energy and wind; touch down on the main wheels at minimum sink with no side drift; protect the nosewheel; and maintain control and momentum throughout the soft-surface rollout and taxi.**

### Purpose

A soft-field landing is about **transferring the airplane's weight from the wings to the wheels as gently as possible**.

On grass, mud, sand, snow, or rough terrain, the surface can add drag, damage the landing gear, or let the nosewheel dig in.

So the landing story is:

**assess the field → stabilize the approach → manage energy → enter ground effect → cushion the flare → touch down softly on the mains → power off → hold the nosewheel off → keep the airplane moving and under control.**

The key idea is:

**keep the wings carrying the airplane as long as practical.**

---

### Start With the Field — Is It Suitable?

Before worrying about the flare, decide whether the surface is suitable for landing.

Under **14 CFR §91.103**, the PIC must become familiar with the available information necessary for the flight. For a soft-field landing, that means considering more than runway length.

Evaluate:

- **Surface firmness**
- **Grass length**
- **Mud / sand / snow**
- **Standing water**
- **Ruts / potholes / rocks / debris**
- **Slope**
- **Usable landing distance**
- **Obstacles**
- **Wind and gusts**
- **Crosswind / tailwind**
- **Density altitude**
- **Aircraft performance**
- **Taxi-out path after touchdown**

The planning question is:

**“Can I make a stabilized approach, touch down softly in a suitable area, keep the nosewheel protected, and keep moving without becoming stuck?”**

A soft field that is also short requires both problems to be solved.

**Soft-field technique protects the airplane and manages the surface. Short-field technique manages limited distance and obstacles.**

In practical operations, the two often occur together, but do not blend the procedures casually. Use the POH/AFM procedure that fits the actual runway, obstacle, and surface conditions.

---

### Select the Touchdown Area Before Final

Choose a touchdown area that is:

- firm enough to support the airplane,
- free of obvious hazards,
- aligned with the landing path,
- and followed by enough usable surface to slow and taxi safely.

The ACS requires selecting and aiming for a suitable touchdown point considering **wind, surface, and obstructions**.

Aim **before** the desired touchdown area because the roundout and flare carry the airplane forward.

If the surface or touchdown area becomes questionable:

**go around.**

---

### Configure Early and Stabilize

A soft-field landing starts like a good normal landing:

**configured early, trimmed, on speed, aligned, and stable.**

For the C172S:

- **Normal approach, flaps UP: 65–75 KIAS**
- **Normal approach, flaps 30°: 60–70 KIAS**
- Flaps:
    - **0°–10° below 110 KIAS**
    - **10°–30° below 85 KIAS**

For a typical soft-field approach in the C172S, use the POH-recommended landing configuration and fly the appropriate published approach speed.

The ACS requires the published approach speed, or if none exists no more than 1.3 V<sub>SO</sub>, within **±5 knots**, with gust factor applied as appropriate.

A stabilized approach means:

- **Correct runway alignment**
- **Correct glidepath**
- **Correct airspeed**
- **Landing configuration established**
- **Trim set**
- **Manageable descent rate**
- **Only small corrections required**

The teaching relationship is:

**pitch for airspeed → power for glidepath → trim off pressure.**

Do not try to stretch a low approach with elevator alone.

If the approach is unstable:

**go around.**

---

### Manage Energy — Soft Does Not Mean Slow and Unstable

The soft-field touchdown should be gentle, but the approach must still have enough energy to remain controllable.

Too much speed creates:

- excess float,
- longer exposure in ground effect,
- and less certainty about the touchdown area.

Too little speed or excessive sink creates:

- hard touchdown risk,
- stall risk,
- and poor control authority.

So the goal is:

**arrive in ground effect on speed, with a low and controlled sink rate.**

We will save the detailed **power-curve / reverse-command discussion** for the short-field lesson. For this task, the practical point is simply:

**if low, add power; if high, reduce power; preserve the target airspeed with pitch and trim.**

---

### Wind Correction Begins Before Final

Wind correction starts in the pattern.

On base:

- maintain the desired ground track,
- anticipate a tailwind or crosswind,
- and do not allow the airplane to overshoot final.

If you overshoot:

**do not skid the airplane back toward the runway.**

Maintain coordination and go around if necessary.

On final, use a **crab** as needed to maintain the extended centerline.

Before touchdown, transition to the correction needed for:

- **No side drift**
- **Longitudinal axis aligned with the runway**

That means:

**aileron into the wind + opposite rudder as required for alignment.**

Continue increasing crosswind correction as the airplane slows and the controls become less effective.

---

### Enter Ground Effect — Now Change the Goal

As the airplane enters ground effect, the goal changes from descending toward the runway to **holding the airplane just off the surface and dissipating the remaining energy gently**.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Landing_Soft_Field.png" alt="Soft-field landing showing transition in ground effect, soft touchdown, and protected nosewheel" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>Soft-field landing — transition into ground effect, reduce sink, touch down softly on the main wheels, then keep the nosewheel protected.</figcaption>
</figure>

Begin the roundout normally.

Then:

**hold the airplane just above the surface in ground effect and let the airspeed decay.**

The objective is not to float indefinitely.

The objective is to:

**reduce the sink rate and let the main wheels touch as softly as practical.**

---

### Add a Little Power in the Flare

As the airplane settles in the flare:

**add or retain a small amount of power as needed to cushion the touchdown.**

This is not a substitute for a stabilized approach.

It is a small energy adjustment that helps reduce the final sink rate and allows the wings to support the airplane slightly longer.

Think:

**small power → soften the sink → hold the landing attitude.**

Too much power creates excessive float.

Too little power combined with excessive sink creates a hard touchdown.

If the airplane balloons, floats excessively, or the touchdown area is no longer assured:

**go around.**

---

### Touchdown — Mains First, Minimum Sink

The touchdown should occur:

- **Main wheels first**
- **Nose-high landing attitude**
- **Minimum sink rate**
- **No side drift**
- **Longitudinal axis aligned with the runway**

As the mains touch:

**POWER OFF.**

Then:

**hold full aft elevator.**

This accomplishes the central soft-field objective:

**keep aerodynamic lift supporting as much of the airplane's weight as practical instead of immediately transferring all of it to the wheels.**

Do not push the airplane onto the runway.

Do not relax the elevator simply because the mains have touched.

---

### Keep the Nosewheel Off

After main-wheel touchdown:

**hold the nosewheel off the surface as long as elevator effectiveness permits.**

The nosewheel is especially vulnerable on a soft or rough surface.

As the airplane slows:

- continue increasing back-elevator as needed,
- maintain directional control with rudder,
- maintain crosswind correction with aileron,
- and allow the nosewheel to descend **under control**.

Do not let it fall.

One of the major common errors is allowing the nosewheel to drop abruptly as lift disappears.

The teaching cue is:

**mains down → power off → full aft → hold the nosewheel off → lower it gently when elevator effectiveness is lost.**

---

### Keep Flying During the Rollout

The landing is not over when the wheels touch.

Maintain:

- **Full aft elevator as appropriate**
- **Crosswind correction**
- **Directional control**
- **Outside scan**
- **Sufficient momentum to prevent sinking or bogging down**

Avoid unnecessary braking.

On a genuinely soft surface, braking can transfer more weight to the nosewheel and increase the chance of digging in.

The surface itself may provide substantial deceleration.

If power is needed to keep the airplane moving:

**use enough power to maintain safe momentum without accelerating excessively.**

The ACS specifically requires exiting the soft area at a speed that precludes sinking into the surface.

---

### Taxi on the Soft Surface

Continue the same soft-field discipline after landing:

- **Elevator aft**
- **Wind controls appropriate**
- **Keep moving when practical**
- **Minimal braking**
- **Avoid sharp turns**
- **Use enough power to avoid bogging down**

Do not become head-down with the checklist while still managing the soft surface.

Aircraft control comes first.

---

### Go Around Early

A go-around is always available until the airplane is safely committed to the landing.

Go around for:

- Unstable airspeed or glidepath
- Excessive sink
- Excessive float
- Poor runway alignment
- Unsafe crosswind drift
- Surface uncertainty
- Traffic or runway conflict
- Wake turbulence
- Windshear
- Ballooning or bounce
- Touchdown area no longer assured
- Any condition that makes the landing unsafe

The ACS requires a **timely go-around** when the approach cannot be completed within tolerance or becomes unsafe.

Do not force a soft-field landing simply because you are already in the flare.

---

### LAHSO

Soft-field conditions deserve extra caution with **Land and Hold Short Operations**.

Before accepting LAHSO, consider:

- available landing distance,
- field/surface condition,
- braking effectiveness,
- wind,
- airplane performance,
- and pilot capability.

The pilot may decline:

**“Unable LAHSO.”**

Once accepted, **§91.123** requires compliance with the ATC clearance unless an exception applies.

A soft or uncertain surface may make LAHSO inappropriate even when the published distance looks adequate.

---

### Collision and Obstruction Risk

Continue scanning for:

- traffic on final,
- aircraft on the runway,
- vehicles,
- people,
- animals,
- obstructions,
- and aircraft that may not be transmitting.

A radio call or tower clearance does not replace the outside scan.

At a towered airport, verify the **correct runway** and comply with ATC instructions.

At a nontowered airport, use appropriate CTAF procedures and visually confirm the landing area is clear.

---

### Low-Altitude Risk

The base-to-final and flare portions occur close to the ground with little room for recovery.

Avoid:

- skidding turns,
- excessive bank,
- abrupt pitch inputs,
- trying to stretch the glide,
- or forcing the airplane onto the surface.

If the geometry or energy is wrong:

**go around.**

---

### Common Errors

#### Final Approach

- **Excessive descent rate**
- **Excessive airspeed**
- **Unstable approach**

Correction:

**stabilize early, trim, control airspeed with pitch, and use power to control the glidepath and sink.**

#### Roundout and Flare

- **Roundout too high**
- **Poor power management**
- **Hard touchdown**

Correction:

**enter ground effect smoothly, hold the landing attitude, and use only enough power to soften the sink.**

#### Touchdown and Weight Transfer

- **Transferring weight from wings to wheels too quickly**
- **Relaxing back-elevator after touchdown**
- **Allowing the nosewheel to fall onto the surface**

Correction:

**mains first → power off → full aft elevator → keep the nosewheel off → lower it gently only as elevator effectiveness is lost.**

#### Rollout

- Poor directional control
- Neutral crosswind controls
- Excessive braking
- Slowing so much that the airplane becomes stuck

Correction:

**keep flying the airplane, keep the controls positioned, and preserve enough momentum to leave the soft area safely.**

---

### Instructor Demonstration

Teach the maneuver in the order it occurs:

1. **Assess the surface, runway, wind, performance, obstacles, touchdown area, and taxi-out plan.**
2. Complete the checklist and verify the correct runway.
3. Scan the runway and adjoining area for traffic and obstructions.
4. Configure the C172S for landing and establish **60–70 KIAS with flaps 30°** when using that normal landing configuration.
5. Trim and establish a stabilized approach.
6. Use pitch for airspeed and power for glidepath.
7. Maintain the correct ground track and transition from crab to the required crosswind sideslip before touchdown.
8. Aim before the desired touchdown area.
9. Enter ground effect and begin the roundout.
10. **Hold the airplane just off the surface and let speed decay.**
11. **Add or retain a small amount of power as needed to cushion the flare.**
12. Touch down softly on the **main wheels** with minimum sink and no side drift.
13. **At touchdown — POWER OFF.**
14. **Hold FULL AFT elevator.**
15. Keep the nosewheel off until elevator effectiveness is lost.
16. Lower the nosewheel gently.
17. Maintain directional control and increasing crosswind correction as the airplane slows.
18. Avoid unnecessary braking.
19. Maintain enough momentum to prevent sinking or bogging down.
20. Continue soft-field taxi technique until clear of the soft surface.

## AI.VII.D.K1 - Purpose of and Procedures for Soft-Field Approach and Landing

Soft-field landing technique is used on **grass, mud, sand, snow, and rough or unprepared surfaces** to reduce sink, minimize landing-gear stress, and prevent the nosewheel from digging in. Inspect the field when possible; a low approach may reveal ruts, soft spots, standing water, debris, or obstacles not apparent from a distance.

Plan a touchdown point in the usable landing area with an adequate margin for obstacles and surface condition. Fly a normal, stabilized pattern/approach using the POH/AFM configuration and speed. In ground effect, round out and flare to a **minimum-sink, nose-high attitude**. A small power addition may be used as necessary to cushion the touchdown, but it must not mask an unstable approach or create excess float.

Touch down softly on the main wheels with **no side drift**, then keep back-elevator pressure as required to hold the nosewheel off as long as elevator effectiveness permits. Avoid unnecessary braking. Maintain control position and enough taxi speed to exit the soft area without sinking, then continue back-elevator pressure as appropriate through taxi and shutdown.

## AI.VII.D.K2 - A Stabilized Approach, Including Energy Management Concepts

A soft-field landing begins with the same disciplined stabilization required for any approach: a controlled glidepath, correct alignment, published airspeed, appropriate landing configuration and trim, an acceptable descent rate, and power that supports the path. Be fully configured and stabilized before the flare; a soft surface does not justify an unstable approach.

At approach speed, the airplane may be on the **back side of the power curve** or region of reversed command. At high angle of attack and low airspeed, induced drag rises. If the airplane is low, increasing pitch alone can slow it, increase drag, and worsen the sink. Use **power for glidepath/altitude trend** and **pitch/trim for airspeed**. If path or airspeed requires large, late, or continuous correction, go around.

For light airplanes, a controlled descent rate is generally in the **500-1,000 FPM** range, adjusted for the aircraft, wind, obstacles, and procedure. Excessive descent rate close to the surface makes a minimum-sink touchdown unlikely. Maintain a hand on the throttle and use small, timely changes; do not remove it before the airplane is safely rolling under control.

## AI.VII.D.K3 - Effects of Atmospheric Conditions, Including Wind, on Approach and Landing Performance

Evaluate wind direction and velocity, gusts, density altitude, runway/field length, slope, obstacles, surface firmness, moisture, grass height, ruts, standing water, and expected braking before committing to land. Tailwind increases groundspeed and landing distance; density altitude increases true airspeed for a given indicated airspeed and reduces margin.

Windshear and gusts can create abrupt airspeed, path, or sink changes. Use the POH/AFM and applicable operational guidance for gust additions, but do not carry excess speed so far that it produces excessive float. Wake turbulence from larger/heavier aircraft can sink and drift into the approach path; increase spacing and remain above the preceding aircraft's path when feasible.

Soft fields add rolling resistance. Wet grass, mud, snow, sand, or a rough surface can improve stopping but can also impede taxi, damage gear, or cause loss of directional control. A field may be unsuitable even when the calculated landing distance appears adequate.

## AI.VII.D.K4 - Wind Correction Techniques on Approach and Landing

Use a **crab** on final to hold the intended ground track. Before touchdown, transition to the wind correction required to align the airplane's longitudinal axis with the landing path and eliminate side drift: aileron into the wind and rudder for alignment. Continue and increase crosswind correction as the airplane slows.

Do not confuse this intentional sideslip near touchdown with a skidding base-to-final turn. Maintain coordination during pattern turns; if wind causes an overshoot or the aircraft cannot remain stable, go around. On the soft surface, protect the upwind wing and retain aileron into the wind as required while using rudder for directional control.

## AI.VII.D.K5 - Common Errors Related to This Task

### Excessive Descent Rate or Airspeed on Final

**Errors:** Excessive descent rate or excessive airspeed on final approach.

**Correction:** Establish the POH/AFM configuration, airspeed, trim, and power early. Use small, timely power changes to manage path and sink; do not carry excess speed that produces float or dive toward the field to correct a high path.

### Unstable Approach

**Error:** Continuing an unstable approach.

**Correction:** Stabilize early with a predictable path, airspeed, alignment, configuration, trim, descent rate, and power setting. If the approach will not promptly stabilize, go around.

### Roundout and Power Errors

**Errors:** Roundout too high; poor power management during roundout/touchdown; or hard touchdown.

**Correction:** Shift vision down the landing area, enter ground effect smoothly, and use only the power needed to reduce sink. Hold the correct nose-high attitude; a hard landing or ballooning sequence requires a go-around when a safe outcome is in doubt.

### Improper Weight Transfer and Nosewheel Control

**Errors:** Inadequate control of airplane weight transfer from wings to wheels after touchdown, or allowing the nosewheel to fall to the runway instead of controlling its descent.

**Correction:** Touch down on the mains with minimum sink, maintain back-elevator to keep aerodynamic lift supporting the airplane as long as practical, and lower the nosewheel only as elevator effectiveness decreases. Continue the manufacturer-recommended elevator/control position while taxiing on the soft surface.

## Risk Management

### AI.VII.D.R1 - Runway Selection, Performance, Available Distance, and Wind

Choose a field/runway based on pilot capability, aircraft limitations, wind, usable length/width, slope, obstacles, density altitude, surface condition, and calculated landing performance. Plan a conservative touchdown point in the usable area. A surface that is too soft, wet, rutted, short, or obstructed may be unsuitable regardless of a nominal landing-distance calculation.

### AI.VII.D.R2 - Crosswind, Windshear, Tailwind, Wake Turbulence, and Surface Condition

Crosswind can create side-load/directional-control risk; tailwind and high density altitude increase ground distance. Windshear, gusts, and wake can disrupt the low-speed approach. Mud, long grass, water, ice, ruts, rocks, and debris can change both touchdown and rollout. Add an operational margin or choose another runway, wait, divert, or go around.

### AI.VII.D.R3 - Rejected Landing/Go-Around and LAHSO

Brief the go-around before downwind/final. Initiate it for unstable energy, excessive sink or float, unsafe surface/touchdown area, loss of alignment, traffic/runway conflict, or any doubt that the landing can be completed safely. On go-around, apply power, pitch for the climb, configure per POH/AFM, compensate for left-turning tendencies, and communicate once the airplane is under control.

Accept **LAHSO** only when the pilot can safely comply after considering the actual surface, landing distance, wind, hold-short point, aircraft performance, and personal capability. Decline when unable. Landing clearance does not eliminate the runway scan or the right to go around.

### AI.VII.D.R4 - Collision Hazards

Scan the runway/landing area and adjoining area for traffic, vehicles, people, animals, obstructions, and NORDO aircraft. Continue scanning during flare, rollout, and soft-surface taxi. A radio call or landing clearance does not replace the visual scan.

### AI.VII.D.R5 - Low Altitude Maneuvering, Stall, Spin, or CFIT

Do not stretch a low approach by increasing pitch without power. At low speed/high angle of attack, induced drag rises and the airplane can mush, stall, or impact obstacles/terrain. Maintain coordination, use appropriate power/pitch, and go around rather than make a steep, skidding, or unplanned low-altitude correction.

### AI.VII.D.R6 - Distractions, Task Prioritization, Loss of Situational Awareness, or Disorientation

Complete the checklist and field/taxi plan early. During the final/flare, prioritize flying the airplane, landing path, speed, sink, alignment, and traffic. Do not become head-down with radio, checklist, or exit planning; communicate after aircraft control is secure.

## Skills

### AI.VII.D.S1-S5 - Checklist, Radio, Runway, Scan, and Touchdown Point

Complete the appropriate checklist, make required calls, verify the correct/assigned runway, scan runway/field and adjoining areas, and select/aim for a suitable touchdown point considering wind, surface, obstacles, and usable distance. Plan the exit/taxi route before touchdown.

### AI.VII.D.S6-S8 - Stabilized Approach, Airspeed, Trim, Directional Control, and Wind Correction

Establish the POH/AFM configuration, trim, and approach speed. Maintain published airspeed, or in its absence no more than 1.3 V<sub>SO</sub>, within **+/-5 knots** with gust factor applied. Use pitch for airspeed, power for path, crab for ground track, then the crosswind correction needed for no-drift/aligned touchdown.

### AI.VII.D.S9-S11 - Roundout, Minimum-Sink Touchdown, Rollout, and Soft-Surface Exit

Make smooth inputs in roundout and flare, use power as needed to control sink, and touch down at a proper pitch attitude with minimum sink, no side drift, and longitudinal-axis alignment. Keep the nosewheel off until elevator effectiveness decreases; maintain elevator/control position per the manufacturer and exit the soft area with enough speed to preclude sinking.

### AI.VII.D.S12-S14 - Go-Around, Soft-Surface Taxi, and Error Correction

Execute a timely go-around when needed. On taxi, keep appropriate control position, maintain sufficient momentum without excessive speed, and protect the nosewheel. After each attempt, identify the common error, its cue, the correction, and whether another landing or a go-around was the proper response.

# Fly

## Setup

- Inspect the landing surface when possible: **firmness, moisture, grass length, ruts, rocks, debris, standing water, usable length, slope, obstacles, wind, and exit/taxi route**.
- Review POH/AFM soft-field landing, approach speed, flap limits, landing performance, go-around, and control-position guidance.
- Brief landing area, touchdown point, wind correction, stabilization gate, small-power use, go-around, LAHSO if offered, nosewheel protection, braking, and taxi momentum.
- Complete the landing checklist before the workload peaks. Verify runway/field, landing clearance/status, traffic, and obstacle picture.

## Instructor Demonstration

1. Establish a stabilized downwind/base/final with the POH/AFM approach speed, full flaps if the aircraft procedure calls for them, proper trim, and a hand on the throttle.
2. On final, hold the planned airspeed. In the Flight Guide C172S/local training flow, **full flaps and 65 KIAS** are used when appropriate to the POH/AFM and conditions; additional power may be needed compared with a familiar normal approach to hold the stable path.
3. Use crab to maintain final ground track; transition to the required crosswind correction before touchdown. Maintain pitch for airspeed and power for glidepath/sink.
4. Enter ground effect, round out, and flare smoothly. Use a **very small power addition** only as necessary to soften the touchdown; do not add power to rescue an unstable approach.
5. Touch the main wheels down with minimum sink and no side drift. Keep **back-elevator pressure throughout** touchdown and rollout; do not let the nosewheel fall.
6. Use minimal braking, if any. Maintain back-elevator/control position and sufficient safe momentum while taxiing through the soft area until clear and shut down.
7. Go around immediately if the approach is unstable, the touchdown area is unsafe, float becomes excessive, or runway/field conflict develops.

The full-flap, 65-knot, and small-power cues are a **Flight Guide C172S/local training flow**. They do not replace the POH/AFM, wind/traffic judgment, airport procedure, or a required go-around.

## Learner Practice

Have the learner brief each attempt: surface condition, touchdown area, approach speed/configuration, power plan, crosswind correction, go-around decision, and taxi route. Start on a suitable surface in manageable wind, then add realistic wind/traffic or a simulated unexpected soft patch only as learner control permits.

Require the learner to say what the throttle is doing on final: path/sink management, not an afterthought. During rollout, require continued back-elevator and deliberate braking restraint. Debrief whether the airplane remained stable, whether the touchdown was minimum sink/no drift, and whether the learner protected the nosewheel through taxi.

## Instructor Demonstration Technique

Use progressive verbal cues:

- **Final:** “Full flaps per POH, 65 KIAS in this C172S flow, trim, hand on throttle, stable.”
- **Ground effect:** “Eyes down the field, small power only if needed, arrest sink, keep flying.”
- **Touchdown/rollout:** “Mains softly, back elevator, align, no side drift, minimal braking.”
- **Taxi:** “Keep the nose light, wind controls, enough momentum, do not sink.”

## Analyze and Correct Common Errors

### Excessive Sink or Airspeed

**Recognize:** The descent rate is too high, airspeed is excessive, the approach floats, or the field picture requires continuous large correction.

**Correct:** Reestablish the published speed, configuration, trim, and a manageable path with small power adjustments. Go around if stabilization is not prompt or the touchdown area is no longer assured.

### Low, Slow, and Behind the Power Curve

**Recognize:** The learner pulls to reach the field, airspeed decays, angle of attack/drag rise, and the airplane continues sinking.

**Correct:** Add appropriate power to arrest the path/sink while setting pitch/trim for safe airspeed. If recovery is not immediate and stable, go around; never mush toward the surface.

### Hard Landing, Bounce, or Dropped Nosewheel

**Recognize:** Excessive sink, abrupt flare, bounce, or the nosewheel contacts early/heavily.

**Correct:** Use smooth flare/power management to reach minimum sink. Keep back-elevator after main-wheel touchdown. Go around if bounce or control loss makes a safe outcome uncertain.

### Stopping or Sinking in the Soft Area

**Recognize:** Braking is excessive, taxi speed decays too much, or the wheels begin to bog down.

**Correct:** Maintain the manufacturer-recommended elevator/control position, use minimal braking, and maintain safe taxi momentum. Stop only where the surface and restart plan permit.

## Completion Standards

The applicant demonstrates while teaching a soft-field approach and landing: surface/performance selection, checklist/radio/runway scan, stabilized configuration/airspeed/trim/path, wind correction, minimum-sink no-drift touchdown, continued back-elevator and soft-surface control, timely go-around, and analysis/correction of common errors.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. What is the purpose of a soft-field landing?

[[answer:
It minimizes sink and landing-gear stress on a soft or rough surface, protects the nosewheel, and preserves directional control and taxi ability where surface drag can be high.
]]

### 2. How does a soft-field landing differ from an uncontrolled float?

[[answer:
Soft-field technique uses a stabilized, on-speed approach and a planned touchdown area. The pilot may use small power to reduce sink and touches down gently, but must go around if float becomes excessive or the available landing area/margin is no longer adequate.
]]

### 3. Explain the back side of the power curve on final approach.

[[answer:
At low airspeed and high angle of attack, induced drag increases. Pulling back to stretch a low approach can slow the airplane and worsen the sink. Use power for path/sink and pitch/trim for airspeed; go around if the approach cannot be stabilized.
]]

### 4. Why might power be used in the soft-field flare?

[[answer:
A very small power addition may reduce sink and cushion touchdown when consistent with the aircraft procedure and a stable approach. It must not be used to compensate for excess sink, low airspeed, poor alignment, or an otherwise unstable approach.
]]

### 5. How is crosswind correction used for a soft-field landing?

[[answer:
Crab on final to maintain ground track, then use aileron into the wind and rudder for longitudinal alignment before touchdown. Maintain/increase correction as the airplane slows, eliminate side drift, and continue wind-control inputs through rollout and taxi.
]]

### 6. What does the ACS require after touchdown on a tricycle-gear airplane?

[[answer:
Touch down at proper pitch attitude with minimum sink and no side drift, keep the nosewheel off until elevator effectiveness is lost, maintain elevator as the manufacturer recommends during rollout, and exit the soft area at a speed that prevents sinking.
]]

### 7. What conditions should cause a go-around?

[[answer:
Unstable path/speed/configuration, excessive sink or float, unsafe or uncertain surface, loss of alignment, runway/field conflict, traffic, or any doubt that a safe landing can be completed require a timely go-around.
]]

### 8. What are the AFH common errors for soft-field approaches and landings?

[[answer:
Excessive descent rate, excessive airspeed, unstable approach, high roundout, poor power management during roundout/touchdown, hard touchdown, poor weight transfer from wings to wheels after touchdown, and allowing the nosewheel to fall rather than controlling its descent. Corrections are stabilized on-speed planning, timely power/pitch/trim, minimum-sink touchdown, continued back-elevator, and a timely go-around.
]]
