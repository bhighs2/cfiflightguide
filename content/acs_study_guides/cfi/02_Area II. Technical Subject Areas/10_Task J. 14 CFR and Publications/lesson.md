# Task J. 14 CFR and Publications

**Area II — Technical Subject Areas**

## Objective

Determine that the applicant understands the Code of Federal Regulations and other relevant publications, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate the required skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Mastering the rules and knowing where to find the answer is critical for every pilot — and doubly so for the instructor, who must teach from authoritative sources rather than memory. This lesson introduces the FAA publications pilots use — the regulations, the AIM, advisory circulars, handbooks, the POH/AFM, airworthiness directives, service bulletins, and the ACS — what each contains, how current it must be, and where to find it.

This is a ground-only knowledge Task. The ACS skill element requires the applicant to **teach** at least one of the knowledge elements, so the lesson builds both the content and the ability to present it.

- Student Q&A: 10 minutes
- FAR/AIM (current)

# Teach

Teach the library of FAA documents as a hierarchy of authority: the regulations say **what** is required, the advisory material explains **how**, and the aircraft's own documents govern that specific airplane. The instructor's job is to know which document answers which question and whether that answer is mandatory. The Overview maps the ACS knowledge, risk, and skill elements; the sections below teach the material once, in the order an instructor would present it.

## AI.II.J.K1 — The Federal Aviation Regulations (14 CFR)

The **Code of Federal Regulations Title 14 — Aeronautics and Space** contains the Federal Aviation Regulations (the FARs). These are **law**, not guidance. Three parts matter most to a pilot and instructor.

### 14 CFR Part 1 — Definitions and Abbreviations

The **definitions** that control how every other regulation is read. When a term's meaning matters — "night," "cross-country," "category," "class" — Part 1 is where it is defined. Teach the learner to look up a term before arguing about it.

### 14 CFR Part 61 — Certification of Pilots and Instructors

The **certification rules**: eligibility, aeronautical knowledge, flight proficiency, and aeronautical experience for each certificate and rating; **logging of flight time**; **medical** requirements; **currency** (flight review, recent flight experience, instrument currency); and the privileges and limitations of each certificate. An instructor lives in Part 61 — it defines what training must be given, what may be logged, and what endorsements are required.

### 14 CFR Part 91 — General Operating and Flight Rules

The **operating rules**: PIC authority and responsibility, preflight action (§ 91.103), airworthiness (§ 91.7), compliance with ATC (§ 91.123), inoperative equipment (§ 91.213), the airspace operating rules (§§ 91.126–91.135), VFR weather minimums (§ 91.155), fuel requirements (§ 91.151), cruising altitudes (§ 91.159), and the required equipment and inspections.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
Part 1 defines it, Part 61 certifies the pilot, Part 91 governs the flight. When a learner asks "can I…?", first ask which part of 14 CFR owns the question — then read the actual section, not a summary.
]]

## AI.II.J.K2 — 49 CFR Part 830 (NTSB)

**Part 830** belongs to the NTSB, not the FAA — it governs the **notification and reporting of aircraft accidents, incidents, and overdue aircraft**, and the preservation of wreckage.

Know the definitions, because they decide what must be reported:

- **Aircraft accident:** an occurrence between boarding with the intention of flight and disembarking in which any person suffers **death or serious injury**, or the aircraft receives **substantial damage**.
- **Serious injury:** hospitalization more than 48 hours commencing within 7 days; fracture of any bone except simple fractures of fingers, toes, or nose; severe hemorrhage or nerve, muscle, or tendon damage; internal organ injury; or second/third-degree burns or burns over more than 5 percent of the body.
- **Substantial damage:** damage that adversely affects structural strength, performance, or flight characteristics and normally requires major repair or replacement. **Not** substantial: engine failure limited to one engine, bent fairings or cowling, dented skin, small punctures, ground damage to propeller blades, and damage to landing gear, wheels, tires, flaps, engine accessories, brakes, or wingtips.

**Immediate notification to the NTSB** is required for an accident and for these **serious incidents**: flight-control malfunction or failure; inability of a required crewmember to perform duties from injury or illness; uncontained turbine engine failure; in-flight fire; mid-air collision; damage to non-aircraft property over **$25,000**; release of a propeller blade (excluding ground contact); complete loss of more than 50 percent of cockpit displays; an ACAS resolution advisory on an IFR flight plan; certain large-aircraft system failures; and taxiway/wrong-runway events or runway incursions requiring immediate evasive action by an air carrier. The operator must also **preserve the wreckage** and file a report.

## AI.II.J.K3 — Advisory Circulars, InFOs, and SAFOs

### Advisory Circulars (ACs)

**ACs are nonregulatory guidance** the FAA issues to provide context, detail, and acceptable means of compliance on aviation topics, organized into numbered subject series. They are available at the FAA website. Commonly used ACs include **AC 60-22** (Aeronautical Decision Making), **AC 60-28B** (English Language Skill Standards), **AC 61-65K** (Certification and endorsements — the instructor's endorsement reference), **AC 61-67C** (Stall and Spin Awareness), **AC 61-98** (Flight Review), **AC 61-142** (Cost Sharing), **AC 90-66C** (Non-Towered Airport Operations), and **AC 120-12A** (Private vs. Common Carriage). An AC is not itself mandatory — but where a regulation requires compliance "in accordance with" an AC, or an AC describes the accepted means of compliance, it carries real weight.

### InFOs and SAFOs

- **Information for Operators (InFOs):** information to help operators meet administrative, regulatory, or operational requirements — **relatively low urgency or safety impact**.
- **Safety Alerts for Operators (SAFOs):** **important safety information**, which may include recommended action, usually for air carriers.

### FAA Legal Interpretations

The **FAA Office of the Chief Counsel** issues legal interpretations answering questions about the regulations. These are **legally binding opinions** of the FAA and are published for reference. When a regulation's plain language is disputed, the Chief Counsel interpretation is the controlling reading.

## AI.II.J.K4 — ACS and PTS

The **Airman Certification Standards (ACS)** — and the older **Practical Test Standards (PTS)** they replaced — define the **conduct of the practical test (the checkride)**. The current ACS documents are published at the FAA's ACS page (faa.gov/training_testing/testing/acs). The ACS tells the instructor and the evaluator exactly what must be taught and to what standard.

Teach the ACS structure, because the instructor plans from it:

- **Areas of Operation** — the phases of flight and ground knowledge (for the CFI: Fundamentals of Instructing, Technical Subject Areas, Preflight Preparation, and so on).
- **Tasks** — the individual subjects within each Area (this page is CFI ACS Area II, Task J).
- **Each Task's Objective** — what the evaluator determines about the applicant.
- **Knowledge, Risk Management, and Skills elements** — the coded line items (such as **AI.II.J.K1**) the applicant must describe, explain, and demonstrate.
- **References** — the FAA source documents each Task draws on.

The **PTS** is the predecessor format; where a PTS still applies, it serves the same purpose. **The ACS is the contract between the instructor and the evaluator** — a lesson built from the ACS covers the checkride; a lesson built from memory may not.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
Teach from the ACS, not from memory. Every Task's Knowledge, Risk, and Skill elements are the checkride — build the lesson to cover each coded element, and the applicant is never surprised.
]]

## AI.II.J.K5 — POH / AFM

The **Pilot's Operating Handbook (POH)** or **Airplane Flight Manual (AFM)** is the authoritative document for a specific airplane. Know the distinction and the structure.

### POH vs. AFM vs. Owner's Manual

- **AFM:** developed by the manufacturer and **approved by the FAA**, specific to an individual aircraft (by serial number and registration), containing the procedures and limitations required to operate it safely. **14 CFR part 91 requires the pilot to comply with the limitations in the approved flight manual, markings, and placards.**
- **POH:** for most light aircraft built after 1975, the POH is **also designated as the FAA-approved flight manual** and follows the standardized GAMA format. A title-page statement indicates which sections are FAA-approved as the AFM.
- **Aircraft owner's/information manual:** general information about the make and model, **not FAA-approved and not specific to an individual aircraft** — it cannot substitute for the AFM/POH.

A manual without a specific registration and serial number is for **general study only** — not for operating that aircraft.

### The Nine Sections (GAMA format)

The typical AFM/POH contains nine sections (a manufacturer may add a Safety and Operational Tips section or an index). The first six are the ones a pilot reaches for most:

1. **General** — overview of the airplane: dimensions, engine and propeller, fuel and oil types and capacities, and the abbreviations and conversion tables used in the document.
2. **Limitations** — the **mandatory rules**: weight, CG, airspeed, and powerplant limits; instrument markings and placards; and a **Kinds of Operation Equipment List** showing which items may be inoperative for VFR versus IFR flight.
3. **Emergency Procedures** — cabin fire, engine failure, icing, spins. Memorize the immediate-action items; **read the amplified procedures ahead of time** — they can contain factors (like a propeller-rpm callout) that the bare checklist omits and that change the outcome.
4. **Normal Procedures** — important airspeeds and the preflight and flight routines. Use this section as the basis for the aircraft's checklist, amended for any modifications and added equipment.
5. **Performance** — the charts for takeoff, climb, cruise, and landing. An EFB performance profile must **match the POH**; routinely compare the predicted performance against the actual and keep a safety buffer.
6. **Weight and Balance / Equipment List** — the loading graphs and CG envelope, and the **current weight-and-balance form** signed by a mechanic. As aircraft gain and lose equipment, the empty weight and CG change — always use the most current data.

Sections 7–9 cover **Systems Description**, **Handling, Service, and Maintenance**, and **Supplements**.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
An unfamiliar POH fails checkrides and, worse, fails pilots in real emergencies. Have the learner find a limit, an emergency procedure, and a performance number from the index — not from memory — until the document's organization is second nature.
]]

## AI.II.J.K6 — The Aeronautical Information Manual (AIM)

The **AIM** is the FAA's official guide to basic flight information and **ATC procedures** in the United States. It is **guidance, not regulation** — but it is the accepted "how" behind the regulations and the reference for NAS procedures and phraseology. The AIM is organized by chapter; teach the structure so the learner knows where to look:

- **Chapter 1 — Air Navigation:** navigation aids (VOR, NDB, DME, GPS/WAAS) and performance-based navigation and area navigation (RNAV).
- **Chapter 2 — Aeronautical Lighting and Other Airport Visual Aids:** airport lighting, air navigation and obstruction lighting, and airport marking aids and signs.
- **Chapter 3 — Airspace:** general, controlled airspace, Class G, special use airspace, and other airspace areas.
- **Chapter 4 — Air Traffic Control:** services available to pilots, radio communication phraseology and techniques, and airport operations.
- **Chapter 5 — Air Traffic Procedures:** preflight, departure, en route, arrival, and pilot/controller roles and responsibilities.
- **Chapter 6 — Emergency Procedures:** general, emergency services available to pilots, distress and urgency procedures, two-way radio communications failure, and aircraft rescue and fire fighting communications.
- **Chapter 7 — Safety of Flight:** meteorology, altimeter setting procedures, wake turbulence, bird hazards, potential flight hazards, and safety/accident/hazard reports.
- **Chapter 8 — Medical Facts for Pilots:** fitness for flight.
- **Chapter 9 — Aeronautical Charts and Related Publications:** the types of charts available.
- **Chapter 10 — Helicopter Operations** and **Chapter 11 — Unmanned Aircraft Systems (UAS):** operations specific to those aircraft.

When a question is about **how to operate in the system** — a procedure, a radio call, a light-gun signal, an emergency service — the AIM is the reference. When the question is **whether it is legal**, go to the CFR.

## Airworthiness Directives, Service Bulletins, and SAIBs

Three documents keep an individual aircraft airworthy, and the distinctions matter.

### Airworthiness Directives (ADs)

**The FAA issues ADs.** An AD is a **legally enforceable regulation under 14 CFR part 39** issued to correct an **unsafe condition** in a product (an aircraft, engine, propeller, or appliance). **Compliance is mandatory** for continued airworthiness — an aircraft with an uncomplied applicable AD is not airworthy. ADs are found and tracked in the FAA's Dynamic Regulatory System (DRS); emergency ADs (EADs) require immediate action.

### Service Bulletins (SBs)

**The aircraft manufacturer issues SBs** to inform owners and operators of safety, maintenance, or product-improvement information. For an aircraft operated under **Part 91, an SB is advisory — not mandatory — unless it is incorporated into an AD.** Even so, an SB should never be ignored: it flags recommended inspections, reveals trends and weaknesses, and warns of items wearing faster than anticipated. An SB marked "Mandatory," "Alert," or "Emergency" signals a significant safety concern, and the manufacturer may ask the FAA to issue an AD adopting it.

### Special Airworthiness Information Bulletins (SAIBs)

**A SAIB is an FAA information tool that alerts, educates, and makes recommendations to the aviation community.** A SAIB contains **non-regulatory information and guidance that does not meet the criteria for an AD** — the safety concern is real but does not rise to the level requiring a legally enforceable directive. (Guidance on when and how to issue a SAIB is in FAA Order 8110.100.) Like a non-mandatory SB, a SAIB is **not mandatory — but it should never be ignored**, because it is the FAA's way of passing along a safety concern the community should know about.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
AD = FAA = mandatory regulation. SB = manufacturer = advisory for Part 91 unless an AD adopts it. SAIB = FAA = non-regulatory alert that did not meet the AD threshold. "Advisory" never means "ignore" — an SB or SAIB is still someone telling you something is wearing out.
]]

## AI.II.J.R1 — Risk Management: Expired or Out-of-Date Material

The controlling risk in this Task is simple: **using expired charts, manuals, or publications without current updates** — and acting on information that is no longer true.

### How the Risk Develops

- Navigating with an expired sectional that no longer shows current airspace or obstructions.
- Quoting a superseded regulation, AC, or ACS revision.
- Relying on a POH that does not reflect current supplements or ADs.
- Teaching from memory what should have been looked up.

### Instructor Mitigation

1. **Recognize** — every document has a revision date; find it before relying on the content.
2. **Assess** — is this the current revision? Have NOTAMs, ADs, or a newer AC/ACS changed it?
3. **Mitigate** — replace expired charts and publications; check the current revision before teaching or flying.
4. **Verify** — confirm the answer in the current authoritative document, not a summary or memory.

### Common Errors and Corrections

- **Treating the AIM or an AC as regulation:** they are guidance; the CFRs and ADs are the law.
- **Treating a service bulletin as automatically mandatory:** for Part 91 it is advisory unless adopted by an AD.
- **Ignoring a SAIB because it is non-regulatory:** a SAIB is the FAA alerting you to a safety concern that did not meet the AD threshold — read and heed it.
- **Guessing at a definition or rule:** open Part 1, 61, or 91 and read the section.
- **Using an expired chart or handbook:** check the revision date and replace it.
- **Confusing the POH with generic guidance:** the POH/AFM limitations are regulatory for that specific airplane.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

1. What do 14 CFR Parts 1, 61, and 91 each cover?

[[answer:
Part 1 — definitions and abbreviations that control how the rest of the regulations are read. Part 61 — certification of pilots, flight instructors, and ground instructors: eligibility, experience, logging, medical, currency, and privileges. Part 91 — general operating and flight rules: PIC authority, preflight action, airworthiness, airspace operating rules, weather minimums, fuel, and required equipment and inspections.
]]

2. Under 49 CFR Part 830, what defines an aircraft accident, and what must the operator do?

[[answer:
An accident is an occurrence between boarding with intent of flight and disembarking in which any person suffers death or serious injury, or the aircraft receives substantial damage. The operator must immediately notify the nearest NTSB office and preserve the wreckage. Certain serious incidents — in-flight fire, mid-air collision, flight-control failure, propeller-blade release, property damage over $25,000, and others — also require immediate notification.
]]

3. What is the difference between an Advisory Circular, an InFO, and a SAFO?

[[answer:
An AC is nonregulatory FAA guidance providing context, detail, and acceptable means of compliance on aviation topics. An InFO (Information for Operators) provides information to help operators meet administrative, regulatory, or operational requirements with relatively low urgency. A SAFO (Safety Alert for Operators) contains important safety information and may include recommended action, usually for air carriers.
]]

4. Are FAA legal interpretations binding?

[[answer:
Yes. Legal interpretations are issued by the FAA Office of the Chief Counsel in response to questions about the regulations and are considered the legally binding opinions of the FAA.
]]

5. What is the difference between the ACS/PTS and a handbook like the AFH or PHAK?

[[answer:
The ACS/PTS defines the conduct and standards of the practical test — what the applicant must demonstrate and to what standard. The handbooks (AFH, PHAK, AIH, Weather) are instructional references that explain how to fly and how to teach — they inform training but do not define the test.
]]

6. Why is the POH/AFM different from general FAA guidance?

[[answer:
The POH/AFM is published by the manufacturer for a specific make and model and contains its procedures, limitations, weight-and-balance data, and systems description. Its limitations section is regulatory for that aircraft — the pilot must operate within it — unlike the general, non-aircraft-specific guidance in the handbooks.
]]

7. What is the AIM, and is it regulatory?

[[answer:
The AIM is the FAA's official guide to basic flight information and ATC procedures — air navigation, airspace, airport operations, emergencies, and charting. It is guidance, not regulation, but it is the accepted "how" behind the regulations and the reference for NAS procedures and phraseology.
]]

8. What is the difference between an Airworthiness Directive, a Service Bulletin, and a SAIB?

[[answer:
The FAA issues ADs under 14 CFR part 39 to correct an unsafe condition; compliance is mandatory for continued airworthiness. The manufacturer issues SBs with safety and maintenance information; for Part 91 operations an SB is advisory unless incorporated into an AD. A SAIB is an FAA information tool carrying non-regulatory safety guidance that does not meet the criteria for an AD. None of the advisory material should be ignored — an SB or SAIB flags a real concern even though compliance is not legally required.
]]

9. What is the risk named in this Task, and how do you manage it?

[[answer:
The use of expired charts, manuals, or publications without current updates. Manage it by recognizing the revision date on every document, assessing whether it is current, replacing expired material, and verifying the answer in the current authoritative source before acting or teaching.
]]

## Completion Standard

The learner explains the content, purpose, authority, and availability of the regulations (14 CFR Parts 1, 61, 91; 49 CFR 830) and the publications (ACs, InFOs, SAFOs, legal interpretations, AIM, handbooks, POH/AFM, ADs, SBs, SAIBs, and the ACS/PTS); distinguishes advisory from mandatory material; applies the recognize–assess–mitigate–verify process to the use of expired documents; and teaches at least one knowledge element clearly, pointing the student to the authoritative source.
