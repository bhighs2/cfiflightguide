# Task B. Visual Scanning and Collision Avoidance

**Area II — Technical Subject Areas**

## Objective

To determine the applicant understands visual scanning and collision avoidance, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate pilot-in-command skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

See-and-avoid is an active responsibility. Most midair collisions and reported near-midair incidents occur in good visual weather, often near airports or navigation aids. Pilots must understand visual limitations, deliberately scan, clear maneuvering areas, and use traffic systems only as a supplement to outside visual vigilance.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested Schedule**

- Ground lesson: approximately 45 minutes
- Aircraft or simulator scan demonstration: approximately 15 minutes

**Equipment**

- Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge
- Airplane Flying Handbook
- AC 90-48E
- CFI Airplane ACS
- Aircraft traffic display, if available
- Flight Guide visual aids

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner Actions**

- Explain visual limitations and collision-risk conditions.
- Demonstrate a deliberate outside scan and clearing procedure.
- Identify aircraft-structure blind spots and scan around them.
- Describe how traffic-alert systems support, but do not replace, see-and-avoid.

**Instructor Actions**

- Teach scanning and clearing procedures from the first dual lesson.
- Divide attention between aircraft control, instruments, navigation, and outside traffic.
- Model positive collision avoidance and timely evasive action.
- Guard against fixation, preoccupation, and overreliance on electronic traffic information.

## Completion Standards

The applicant can explain visual scanning and collision avoidance, teach the associated risk management, and demonstrate effective scan, clearing, blind-spot, visual, and available traffic-alert techniques while maintaining aircraft control and situational awareness.

**The main idea in visual scanning and collision avoidance is that see-and-avoid is an active pilot responsibility.**

Traffic avoidance is not something we outsource to:

- ATC.
- ADS-B.
- Traffic displays.
- Another crewmember.

Those resources help, but the pilot still has to maintain an effective outside scan.

The key principle is:

**Scan deliberately. Clear before maneuvering. Recheck whenever attention comes back inside.**

### Why Collision Avoidance Matters

Aircraft can close on each other very quickly.

At high closure rates, the time between first detection and impact may be only seconds.

That means collision avoidance depends on:

- Detecting traffic early.
- Recognizing whether it is a threat.
- Acting before the situation becomes urgent.

Most midair collisions and near-midair encounters do not require bad weather.

They often occur in good visibility, where pilots assume they will see each other.

### See and Avoid

Under **14 CFR §91.113**, when weather conditions permit, each person operating an aircraft must maintain vigilance to **see and avoid other aircraft**.

That applies whether operating:

- VFR.
- IFR.
- With ATC services.
- Without ATC services.

ATC traffic advisories do not remove the pilot's responsibility to see and avoid.

The rule also establishes right-of-way requirements.

Examples include:

- **Head-on or nearly head-on:** both aircraft alter course to the right.
- **Overtaking:** the overtaking aircraft alters course to the right and passes well clear.
- **Converging:** the aircraft to the other's right generally has the right-of-way.
- **Aircraft in distress:** has right-of-way over all other air traffic.

But the most important point is:

**Having the right-of-way does not justify allowing a collision to occur.**

If another aircraft is too close:

**avoid it.**

### How We Actually See Traffic

The eye does not see the entire sky with equal clarity.

The small central portion of the retina called the **fovea** provides the sharpest detail during daylight.

That means effective scanning requires moving the eyes so different portions of the sky come through central vision.

A good daytime scan uses:

- Short eye movements.
- Small sectors.
- A deliberate pause in each sector.

A commonly taught FAA technique is:

- Move the eyes no more than about **10° at a time**.
- Pause for at least **one second** in each area.

The point is not to mechanically count seconds.

The point is:

**Move → pause → focus → move again.**

### Peripheral Vision

Peripheral vision is good at detecting **movement**.

That makes it useful for noticing traffic that was not directly in the center of the scan.

Once movement is detected:

- Shift central vision to the object.
- Identify it.
- Determine whether it is a threat.

At night, peripheral vision becomes even more important because central night vision is relatively poor.

For dim objects, use **off-center viewing** rather than staring directly at them.

### Relaxed Intermediate Focal Distance

One major scanning problem is looking outside without actually focusing on distant objects.

When the visual field is empty or hazy, the eyes may naturally focus only a short distance ahead.

This is sometimes called **relaxed intermediate focal distance** or empty-field myopia.

The result is that the pilot may think they are scanning for traffic while the eyes are not focused far enough away to detect it effectively.

The correction is deliberate:

- Focus on distant terrain.
- Focus on clouds.
- Focus on distant light sources at night.
- Then continue scanning the sky.

### Environmental Conditions That Degrade Vision

Traffic detection becomes more difficult with:

- Haze.
- Smoke.
- Dust.
- Rain.
- Fog.
- Glare.
- Flying toward the sun.
- Darkness.
- Poor contrast.
- Dirty or bug-covered windshields.

Pilot condition matters too.

Vision and scanning degrade with:

- Fatigue.
- Hypoxia.
- Alcohol.
- Certain medications.
- Illness.
- Stress.
- Poor nutrition.

A clean windshield, proper sunglasses, good cockpit lighting, and an alert pilot materially improve the chance of seeing traffic.

### Aircraft Blind Spots

Aircraft structure creates blind areas.

Examples include:

- Wings.
- Windshield posts.
- Engine cowling.
- Door posts.
- Sun visors.

Before turning, actively clear the blind spot.

For a **high-wing airplane**:

**Raise the wing in the direction of the intended turn and look beneath it before turning.**

For a **low-wing airplane**:

**Lower the wing in the direction of the intended turn and look above it before turning.**

The exact technique depends on the airplane.

The principle is:

**Move the obstruction so you can see the area you are about to enter.**

### Clearing Procedures

Clearing is not just two ceremonial clearing turns before a maneuver.

It is a continuous process.

#### Before Takeoff

Before entering the runway:

- Scan final approach.
- Check for aircraft on base or final.
- Check for aircraft departing or crossing.
- Verify the runway environment is clear.

#### Before Turns and Maneuvers

Before changing direction or beginning a training maneuver:

- Clear left.
- Clear right.
- Clear above.
- Clear below.

During instruction, verbalizing this helps build the habit.

#### Climbs and Descents

A straight climb or descent can hide traffic behind the nose.

Use shallow clearing turns or gentle banks as appropriate to improve the view.

#### Straight-and-Level Flight

Continue a deliberate outside scan.

Do not allow cruise flight to become heads-down time.

### Visual Cue of a Collision Course

The classic collision-course cue is:

**No relative movement + increasing size**

If another aircraft appears stationary in the windshield but keeps getting larger:

**you are likely on a collision course.**

An aircraft that moves steadily across the windshield is less likely to be on a direct collision course.

But continue monitoring it.

If there is doubt:

**take early evasive action.**

### High-Risk Collision Environments

Collision risk is greatest where aircraft tend to converge.

Examples include:

- Traffic patterns.
- Non-towered airports.
- Practice areas.
- Navigation fixes.
- VORs.
- Intersections.
- Arrival and departure corridors.
- Areas around Class B, C, and D airspace.
- Climbs and descents.

Traffic-pattern operations deserve special attention because:

- Workload is high.
- Aircraft are close together.
- Speed differences may be significant.
- Some aircraft may not have radios.
- Some traffic may not appear on ADS-B.

### Non-Towered Airport Operations

At non-towered airports, maintaining visual awareness is especially important.

Under **14 CFR §91.126**, when approaching to land at an airport in Class G airspace:

- Airplanes normally make **left turns** in the traffic pattern unless airport markings or approved visual signals indicate right turns.
- Pilots should conform to the established traffic flow.

Under **14 CFR §91.127**, Class E airport operations generally follow the same traffic-flow requirements unless otherwise authorized or required.

From a collision-avoidance standpoint:

- Enter at pattern altitude.
- Scan before joining.
- Watch for aircraft already established.
- Do not descend blindly into the pattern.
- Do not assume every aircraft is making radio calls.

### Traffic Systems and ADS-B

Electronic traffic systems are extremely useful.

They can help with:

- Traffic awareness.
- Relative altitude.
- Trend information.
- Position awareness.

But they have limitations.

Not every aircraft will necessarily appear on the display.

Traffic information may be:

- Delayed.
- Incomplete.
- Dependent on equipment and reception.
- Misinterpreted.

So the correct use is:

**Traffic display → cue the eyes outside**

not:

**Traffic display → replace the outside scan**

Use:

- ADS-B In.
- ATC traffic advisories.
- Flight following.
- CTAF calls.

as supplements to visual scanning.

### Aircraft Lighting

Lighting can improve conspicuity.

Under **14 CFR §91.209**:

- Position lights are required from **sunset to sunrise**.
- An installed anticollision light system generally must be operated when the aircraft is operated, subject to the PIC's authority to turn it off when operating conditions make that safer.

Even when not strictly required, appropriate exterior lighting can help other pilots see the airplane.

### Division of Attention

The cockpit competes constantly with the outside scan.

Common distractions include:

- GPS programming.
- Tablets.
- Charts.
- Checklists.
- Radios.
- Passengers.
- Autopilot management.
- Instrument fixation.

The solution is to keep inside tasks short.

A useful habit is:

**Inside briefly → outside again**

Before a high-workload phase:

- Prepare frequencies early.
- Brief the approach early.
- Organize charts and tablets.
- Configure before workload spikes.

The less time spent heads-down, the more time available for traffic detection.

### Safety Pilot

During simulated instrument flight, the manipulating pilot may have little or no outside visibility.

The safety pilot's primary job is to maintain the traffic lookout and protect collision avoidance.

Do not let:

- Conversation.
- Instruction.
- Navigation.
- Electronics.

distract the safety pilot from the outside scan.

### Vestibular and Visual Illusions

Visual scanning also depends on understanding when the senses can be misleading.

When outside references are unreliable, the vestibular and visual systems can produce false sensations.

Examples include:

- The leans.
- Coriolis illusion.
- Graveyard spiral.
- Somatogravic illusion.
- False horizon.
- Autokinesis.

The response is:

**Trust reliable flight instruments, not bodily sensation.**

### Landing Illusions

Landing illusions can distort perceived height, distance, and approach angle.

#### Runway Width

A **narrow runway** can make the airplane appear higher than it actually is.

That can lead to a **low approach**.

A **wide runway** can make the airplane appear lower than it actually is.

That can lead to a **high approach**.

#### Runway Slope

An **upsloping runway** can create the impression of being high.

A **downsloping runway** can create the impression of being low.

#### Featureless Terrain

Dark terrain, water, snow, or other featureless areas can make the pilot feel higher than actual.

That can lead to a dangerously low approach.

#### Haze and Rain

Haze can make objects appear farther away.

Rain and fog can distort height and pitch perception.

The defense is to:

- Anticipate the illusion.
- Use normal sight pictures.
- Cross-check altitude.
- Use PAPI/VASI or other glidepath information when available.

### Common Errors — Visual Scanning and Collision Avoidance

- Looking outside without actually focusing at distance.
- Staring in one direction too long.
- Moving the eyes too quickly to detect traffic.
- Fixating on instruments.
- Fixating on a tablet or traffic display.
- Assuming ATC will keep traffic separated.
- Assuming ADS-B shows every aircraft.
- Failing to clear before turns or maneuvers.
- Failing to account for wing or structural blind spots.
- Descending into a traffic pattern without adequate visual clearing.
- Failing to recognize a stationary, growing traffic target.
- Poor division of attention between cockpit tasks and outside scanning.
- Allowing dirty windshield or glare to degrade visibility.
- Failure to anticipate night-vision limitations.
- Failure to take timely evasive action.

### Instructor Emphasis

The learner should leave the lesson understanding:

1. **See-and-avoid remains the pilot's responsibility under 14 CFR §91.113.**
2. **ATC and ADS-B support the scan; they do not replace it.**
3. **Use short eye movements and pause long enough to actually see.**
4. **Peripheral vision is important for detecting movement.**
5. **A stationary target that grows larger is a collision warning.**
6. **Clear blind spots before turning.**
7. **Traffic risk is highest where aircraft converge and workload is high.**
8. **§91.126 and §91.127 govern traffic-flow expectations at many non-towered airports.**
9. **§91.209 establishes aircraft-light requirements that also support conspicuity.**
10. **Collision avoidance is a continuous flight-control task, not an occasional scan.**

### Close

Collision avoidance comes down to a disciplined habit:

**Scan → Clear → Recheck**

Use the available tools:

- Your eyes.
- ATC.
- ADS-B.
- CTAF.
- Exterior lights.
- Safety pilots.

But never let any of them replace the basic responsibility:

**Look outside and see the traffic before it becomes a threat.**



## AI.II.B.K1 — Environmental Conditions That Degrade Vision

Vision is the primary sense for safe flight but has limits. **Reduced visibility, haze, smoke, dust, fog, atmospheric lighting, sun position, glare, darkness, and the time required to refocus from instruments to distant traffic** reduce the chance of detecting another aircraft. **Photopic** vision provides best color and detail in high light; **mesopic** vision at dawn, dusk, and moonlight combines rods and cones with reduced acuity; **scotopic** vision in low light has poor detail and no color.

At night and in low light, reduced acuity, loss of color, and central night blind spots further degrade detection. Use proper scanning, give the eyes time to refocus, and do not assume good visual weather means low collision risk.

## AI.II.B.K2 — Vestibular and Visual Illusions

Vestibular, somatosensory, and visual systems can provide conflicting information, causing **spatial disorientation**. When the horizon is unreliable or absent, bodily sensations can create convincing but false impressions of aircraft attitude and movement. Trust the flight instruments when visual references are unreliable.

Visual illusions such as a **false horizon, autokinesis, runway width or slope, featureless terrain, haze, fog, and ground lighting** can distort height, distance, or approach-path perception. Anticipate these conditions, use reliable references, and maintain instrument and landing proficiency.

## AI.II.B.K3 — See and Avoid Responsibilities

**See and avoid** requires constant vigilance for traffic, regardless of whether operating under VFR or IFR, aircraft type, or flight purpose. Right-of-way does not remove the duty to avoid a collision; yield when another aircraft appears too close.

Under **14 CFR §91.113**, when weather permits, each person operating an aircraft must maintain vigilance to see and avoid other aircraft. When another aircraft has the right-of-way, the pilot gives way and does not pass over, under, or ahead of it unless well clear. For head-on or nearly head-on aircraft, each pilot alters course to the right; an overtaking aircraft alters course to the right to pass well clear.

Collision avoidance begins with planned scanning and clearing procedures. Before takeoff, scan approach areas for landing traffic. During climbs and descents, use gentle banks that permit continuous scan. Use periodic clearing procedures in straight-and-level flight and before all turns and training maneuvers.

## AI.II.B.K4 — Visual Scanning Procedure and Peripheral Vision

Effective scanning uses **short, regularly spaced eye movements** that bring successive sky areas into central vision. Each movement should not exceed **10 degrees**, and each area should be observed for **at least one second**. Use a pattern that is comfortable and follow it consistently.

Peripheral vision is important because movement is often the first indication of a collision threat. Avoid staring straight ahead or allowing the eyes to settle at a relaxed intermediate focal distance, where the pilot may be looking without actually seeing. At night, use **off-center viewing** rather than staring directly at a dim target; move the eyes to prevent the target from disappearing in the night blind spot.

## AI.II.B.K5 — Aircraft Blind Spots and Clearing Procedures

Aircraft structure, wing position, windshield posts, sun visors, and aircraft attitude create blind spots. Before a turn, a **high-wing** pilot raises the wing in the intended turn direction; a **low-wing** pilot lowers the wing in that direction. Then look for traffic before turning. **Glareshield reflections, a dirty windshield, and oil residue** further reduce the effectiveness of the visual scan and should be addressed before flight.

Use an effective clearing procedure before every turn and training maneuver. During instruction, verbalize the scan: **clear left, right, above, and below**. Clearing is not a fixed ritual; it is a deliberate check that the next maneuver will not enter another aircraft's flightpath.

## AI.II.B.K6 — Visual Cues of an Impending Mid-Air Collision

An aircraft that shows **no relative lateral or vertical movement while increasing in size** is likely on a collision course. Apparent movement, often detected in peripheral vision, is commonly the first indication of a potential collision. High closure rates limit the time available for detection, decision, and evasive action, so act immediately when the cue is recognized.

## AI.II.B.K7 — Situations That Create the Greatest Collision Risk

The collision risk is greatest where traffic converges and pilots are occupied with other tasks: **near airports, in traffic patterns, near navigation aids and intersections, during climbs and descents, in practice areas, and in high-volume operational environments**. Greater speed differences and closure rates reduce the time available for detection, decision, and evasive action. Midair collisions commonly occur in good visibility and daylight, often at or near uncontrolled airports and below 1,000 feet. Traffic patterns require a continuous visual scan, awareness of aircraft without radios, and attention to blind spots.

At a **non-towered airport**, enter the traffic pattern at pattern altitude, conform to the established traffic flow, scan continuously, and give way to aircraft already established in the pattern. For Class G airports, §91.126 requires airplane turns to the left unless approved signals or markings indicate right turns; under §91.127, Class E airport operations follow §91.126 unless otherwise authorized or required. This lesson addresses the airborne scan and traffic-pattern collision risk; Task C owns taxi, hold-line, and runway-incursion procedures.

Use exterior lights to improve the chance of being seen in the traffic pattern. Under **§91.209**, position lights are required from sunset to sunrise, and an installed anticollision light system must be lighted unless the pilot in command determines that operating conditions make turning it off safer.

## AI.II.B.K8 — Division of Attention Inside and Outside the Aircraft

Visual scanning shares attention with aircraft control, instruments, navigation, radios, tablets, passengers, and flight-deck technology. Concentration on instruments or tablets, distraction by passengers, fatigue, boredom, illness, anxiety, and preoccupation can reduce scan frequency and traffic detection.

Electronic traffic information supports awareness but does not replace outside scanning. ADS-B In displays and alerts should be integrated into the normal scan; not all traffic will appear because some aircraft are not equipped with ADS-B Out or transponders. Use **CTAF or practice-area position reports, ATC traffic advisories, and flight following** to supplement visual awareness. Traffic calls use clock position relative to the aircraft's **ground track**, so confirm the reported traffic location with the outside scan.

Exterior lighting, proper sunglasses when conditions require them, and appropriate use of available traffic systems can improve conspicuity or awareness, but none replaces an effective visual scan.

A safety pilot or instructor must guard against preoccupation, especially during simulated instrument flight and advanced flight-deck technology use. Teach new technology on the ground and maintain constant outside vigilance.

## Instructor Application — Scan and Collision Avoidance Scenario

Before a practice maneuver near a non-towered airport, ask the learner to state the planned clearing procedure and traffic threats. Have the learner scan in 10-degree segments, check high-wing or low-wing blind spots, verbalize **clear left, right, above, and below**, and identify how an ADS-B display or safety pilot supports the scan. Add an instrument or tablet task only after the learner can maintain aircraft control and systematic visual vigilance; stop or reduce the task if the outside scan deteriorates.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. What conditions degrade a pilot's ability to see traffic?

[[answer:
Reduced visibility, haze, fog, atmospheric lighting, sun position, darkness, refocus time, blind spots, and the physical limitations of the human eye all reduce traffic detection.
]]

### 2. What is the visual cue of an impending midair collision?

[[answer:
An aircraft with no relative lateral or vertical motion that increases in size is likely on a collision course and requires immediate evasive action.
]]

### 3. Describe an effective traffic scan.

[[answer:
Use short, regularly spaced eye movements of no more than 10 degrees, observe each area for at least one second, refocus between scan points, and use peripheral vision to detect movement.
]]

### 4. How do high-wing and low-wing pilots clear a blind spot before turning?

[[answer:
A high-wing pilot raises the wing in the intended turn direction; a low-wing pilot lowers the wing in that direction. Both then look for traffic before turning.
]]

### 5. Where is collision risk greatest?

[[answer:
Near airports, in traffic patterns, near navigation aids and intersections, during climbs and descents, and in high-volume environments where traffic converges and workload is high.
]]

### 6. How should ADS-B In be used for collision avoidance?

[[answer:
Integrate its display and alerts into the normal traffic scan for awareness, while remembering not all aircraft appear on the display and it never replaces visual see-and-avoid.
]]

### 7. What is the role of a safety pilot or instructor during simulated instrument flight?

[[answer:
Guard against inside preoccupation, maintain a constant traffic lookout, and ensure technology and simulated-instrument tasks do not displace see-and-avoid responsibilities.
]]
