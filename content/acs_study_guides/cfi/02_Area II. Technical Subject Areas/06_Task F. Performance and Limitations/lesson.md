# Task F. Performance and Limitations

**Area II — Technical Subject Areas**

## Objective

Determine that the applicant understands aircraft performance and limitations, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate the required skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Performance planning answers whether the airplane can safely complete the proposed operation under the actual conditions. Weight, CG, density altitude, wind, runway surface and slope, configuration, obstacle clearance, and pilot technique all affect the outcome. Published AFM/POH data and limitations are the starting point; a safe plan accounts for the difference between calculated results and actual conditions.

## Schedule / Equipment

- Ground: 60–90 minutes
- Current AFM/POH Sections 2, 5, and 6
- Aircraft-specific weight-and-balance form, calculator, and current weather/airport data
- Whiteboard or performance-planning worksheet

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** interpret the aircraft’s charts and limitations, compute weight and balance, identify performance factors, correct an out-of-limits loading condition, and explain a conservative go/no-go decision.

**Instructor:** demonstrate one complete calculation using the actual AFM/POH, challenge assumptions behind the chart inputs, and require the learner to reassess the plan after a load, weather, or runway condition changes.

## Completion Standards

The learner uses the appropriate AFM/POH performance data, computes weight and balance correctly, verifies the aircraft remains within weight and CG limits throughout the flight, corrects an out-of-limits loading condition, and explains the limitations, risks, and safety margin that support the decision.

## Ground Lesson Outline

1. Start with AFM/POH limitations and performance-data assumptions.
2. Compute pressure altitude and density altitude; identify the effect of air density.
3. Determine takeoff, climb, cruise, landing, and fuel performance using actual conditions.
4. Define weight-and-balance terms and calculate total weight, total moment, and CG.
5. Check the applicable loading envelope at departure, arrival, and after fuel burn.
6. Explain aerodynamics: weight, CG, drag, load factor, stall speed, and operating limits.
7. Add a safety margin and make a go/no-go or plan-change decision.

# Teach

## AI.II.F.K1 — Performance Data, Charts, and Limitations

The **AFM/POH performance section** contains the data for takeoff, climb, range, endurance, descent, and landing. Manufacturers present it in tables or graphs and may base it on standard atmospheric conditions, **pressure altitude**, or **density altitude**. The chart is useful only when the pilot understands its basis, uses the correct inputs, applies its notes and corrections, and remains within the aircraft’s published limitations.

Teach a repeatable chart process: identify the required result, select the correct chart and configuration, enter the actual conditions, make each required interpolation or correction, record the result, compare it with the available runway or fuel, and preserve a margin. Use the aircraft’s own AFM/POH, not an example chart from another airplane. Trace the complete mission: takeoff distance and obstacle clearance, climb, cruise power/fuel flow/TAS, endurance or range, landing distance, and required reserves.

The **chart result is not a promise**. Published data relies on its stated configuration, conditions, and procedure. Actual performance can be worse because of runway surface, slope, wind changes, pilot technique, aircraft condition, or an imprecise leaning procedure. A conservative plan leaves room for those differences rather than demanding maximum performance from the airplane.

### Risk and Skill Integration

Use the correct AFM/POH revision and chart for the installed aircraft, planned configuration, and operation. Check units, pressure-versus-density-altitude basis, notes, interpolation, corrections, and the distinction between ground roll and distance over a 50-foot obstacle. Limitations are operating boundaries, not targets: check applicable weights, CG envelope, V-speeds, flap/gear operating limits, operating category, powerplant limits, and the conditions that apply to the planned operation.

Performance data is based on stated conditions and the specified technique. Actual results can be worse because of wind variation, runway condition, aircraft condition, loading, pilot technique, or omitted factors. Preserve a margin that makes the plan resilient to those differences.

**Instructor application — AI.II.F.S1:** Have the learner select the correct AFM/POH chart, collect actual inputs, follow every note and correction, compare the result to available performance, state the safety margin, and explain the plan change required when the margin is inadequate.

## AI.II.F.K2 — Factors Affecting Performance

### AI.II.F.K2a — Atmospheric Conditions

**Pressure altitude** is the altitude indicated when the altimeter is set to **29.92 inches Hg**. **Density altitude** is pressure altitude corrected for nonstandard temperature. Higher density altitude means lower air density, which reduces **engine power, propeller thrust, and airfoil lift**. High elevation, low atmospheric pressure, high temperature, and high humidity contribute to higher density altitude and lower performance.

At a given density altitude, the airplane performs as though it were operating at that altitude in the standard atmosphere. Use the chart or flight computer to determine density altitude, then use the appropriate AFM/POH performance data. Wind must also be evaluated according to the published chart and runway direction; never treat a forecast wind as guaranteed performance.

Density altitude changes true airspeed even when the indicated airspeed used for takeoff or landing remains the same. At higher density altitude, the airplane must travel faster over the ground to achieve the same indicated airspeed, which contributes to greater takeoff and landing distance. Teach the learner to separate field elevation, pressure altitude, density altitude, indicated airspeed, true airspeed, and groundspeed rather than using them interchangeably.

### AI.II.F.K2b — Pilot Technique

Published results depend on the procedure and configuration specified in the AFM/POH. Airspeed control, rotation technique, obstacle-clearance technique, leaning procedure where applicable, touchdown point, braking, and directional control affect actual performance. A calculation does not compensate for an unstable approach, an early rotation, delayed braking, or an imprecise short-field technique.

For a high density-altitude departure in an aircraft for which the AFM/POH calls for leaning, use the **published leaning procedure**. Do not substitute a remembered setting or another airplane's procedure. Proper technique also means **rejecting a takeoff early** when acceleration, control, or runway remaining does not support the plan.

### AI.II.F.K2c — Airplane Configuration

Performance data applies to a stated configuration: flap setting, gear position, power setting, mixture, cowl-flap setting where applicable, and aircraft condition. The wrong configuration can invalidate the calculation. Teach the learner to read chart titles, notes, and footnotes before reading the resulting distance or rate.

### AI.II.F.K2d — Airport Environment

Takeoff and landing charts commonly assume a paved, level, smooth, dry surface. Soft, rough, wet, contaminated, or unpaved surfaces affect acceleration, rolling resistance, braking, and directional control. An upslope impedes takeoff acceleration and typically reduces landing roll; a downslope has the opposite effect. Obstacles may require a different performance chart or a change in the plan.

**Runway length alone is not enough.** Distinguish **ground roll** from **total distance over a 50-foot obstacle**, account for the departure and approach path, and use the Chart Supplement or airport data for surface and slope. Water, slush, snow, ice, or soft ground can reduce acceleration and braking effectiveness; use AFM/POH data when provided and do not invent a correction when it is not.

### AI.II.F.K2e — Loading and Weight and Balance

More weight requires more lift in level flight and affects takeoff, landing, climb, range, and maneuvering performance. Loading also moves the CG. The total weight may be legal while the CG is not, and a legal departure loading may change as fuel is burned or baggage/passengers are moved. Compute the condition for every meaningful phase of flight.

Excess weight affects performance and can exceed the basis on which structural and operating limits were established. An out-of-limit CG can adversely affect controllability, stability, and stall recovery. Secure cargo and anticipate fuel burn, passenger movement, and loading changes. Recompute before a change, then verify departure, en route, and landing conditions against the AFM/POH.

At a high weight, **takeoff and landing distance increase**, **climb performance decreases**, range and maneuverability are reduced, and stall, approach, and landing speeds increase. Weight reduction by lowering fuel is valid only when the resulting mission still meets all fuel and reserve requirements. Shifting a load may solve a CG problem without solving an overweight problem; **every remedy must be recomputed**.

## AI.II.F.K3 — Weight-and-Balance Terms

- **Basic empty weight:** the aircraft’s weight with standard and optional equipment, unusable fuel, and full operating fluids as defined in its approved records; use the current aircraft-specific value.
- **Maximum gross weight:** the maximum approved total aircraft weight for the applicable operation.
- **Useful load:** the difference between maximum gross weight and basic empty weight; it is available for usable fuel, occupants, and cargo.
- **Reference datum:** the imaginary vertical reference line from which arms are measured.
- **Arm:** the horizontal distance from the datum to an item.
- **Moment:** the product of an item’s weight and arm.
- **Center of gravity:** the balance point expressed as an arm or percent of mean aerodynamic chord, depending on the AFM/POH.
- **CG limits:** the approved forward and aft boundaries for the weight and operating category.

Use the aircraft's **current weight-and-balance record**, not a generic sample. Equipment additions, removals, repairs, and modifications can change the basic empty weight and moment. Distinguish **useful load** from **payload**: useful load includes usable fuel, while payload is what remains for occupants, baggage, and cargo after the fuel load is selected.

## AI.II.F.K4 — Computing CG

Use the method published in the AFM/POH. The standard calculation is:

**Moment = Weight x Arm**

**CG = Total Moment / Total Weight**

Begin with the aircraft’s current basic empty weight and moment, then add the approved weights and stations for occupants, usable fuel, and baggage. Sum total weight and total moment, calculate CG as directed by the AFM/POH, and plot or compare the result with the applicable envelope. Recalculate after moving, adding, or removing a load and for later phases when fuel burn materially changes the loading.

When a loading is out of limits, **do not dispatch** and hope the condition improves. Correct it by reducing, moving, adding, or deferring an allowable load only after recomputing the total weight and CG and confirming the change remains within all other limits.

Use the method specified by the manufacturer. A computational sheet may require a direct moment calculation; a loading graph may provide an indexed moment. In either method, check the final point against the correct envelope and operating category. An airplane may be within its normal-category envelope but not within the utility-category envelope needed for a planned operation.

**Instructor application — AI.II.F.S2:** Have the learner start with current approved empty-weight data, enter occupants, usable fuel, and baggage at their approved stations, calculate total weight, total moment, and CG, and verify takeoff and landing loading. Then give them an out-of-limits condition to correct by moving, adding, or removing weight before recomputing it.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
Do not let a learner "fix" loading by instinct. Make them state whether the problem is total weight, forward CG, aft CG, or more than one condition, then predict what each proposed load change does before calculating it.
]]

## AI.II.F.K5 — Aerodynamics, Performance, and Limits

In steady, level flight, lift equals weight and thrust equals drag. At lower speed, induced drag predominates; at higher speed, parasite drag predominates. The power required curve explains why the airplane has a region of reversed command at low airspeeds, where a lower airspeed requires more power to maintain altitude.

Weight and load factor affect **stall speed** and **structural margins**. The **V-g diagram** depicts the relationship among airspeed, load factor, and structural limits. Use the airplane’s published **V-speeds**, never-exceed and operating-range markings, maneuvering-speed guidance, and loading limitations. Maneuvering speed changes with weight; use the value published for the actual loading when the AFM/POH provides one.

Teach the installed airplane's V-speeds and their operational meaning: stall speeds in the stated configurations, best angle and best rate of climb, best glide, maximum flap-extended speed, maximum gear operating/extended speeds if applicable, maneuvering speed, maximum structural cruising speed, and never-exceed speed. **Maximum demonstrated crosswind is not an absolute operating limitation**; the pilot still needs a proficiency- and condition-based limit.

CG location affects stability and controllability. A **forward CG** generally requires more tail-down force and may increase the wing lift required; an **aft CG** reduces longitudinal stability and can impair stall-recovery characteristics. The **AFM/POH envelope establishes the allowable range**, not a pilot’s estimate of whether the airplane "feels balanced."

The performance tradeoffs connect directly to the chart work. Higher weight requires more lift and a higher AOA for the same condition of flight; it therefore increases stall speed and degrades takeoff, climb, and landing performance. A forward CG increases control forces and can make it difficult to raise the nose or flare; an aft CG produces lighter pitch forces, less longitudinal stability, and reduced ability to recover from a stall or spin.

### Scenario — The Hot, Short-Runway Departure

The planned departure is from a high-elevation airport on a hot afternoon. The runway is shorter than usual, slopes uphill, and has a soft shoulder after recent rain. Two passengers arrive with more baggage than planned.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What AFM/POH charts, data, and limitations apply?
- Which actual values must be gathered before calculating performance?
- How do density altitude, runway surface/slope, obstacles, loading, and technique change the margin?
- Compute the departure and landing loading. What could change after fuel burn?
- If the calculated result leaves little margin, what plan changes are available?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

The learner determines that the performance calculation and weight-and-balance envelope must use actual conditions and the specific aircraft’s AFM/POH. They identify a plan change such as reducing or moving baggage, reducing fuel only when reserves and mission allow, delaying for cooler conditions, choosing another runway/airport, or not departing. The learner does not accept a book result as a guarantee.

### Common Errors and Corrections

- **Using a familiar chart from memory:** return to the current aircraft AFM/POH and read the chart title, notes, and conditions.
- **Checking only takeoff weight:** calculate CG and total weight for each meaningful phase, including fuel burn.
- **Treating maximum demonstrated crosswind as an absolute limitation:** distinguish published limitations from demonstrated values, then make a conservative proficiency-based decision.
- **Ignoring runway condition, slope, or obstacles:** gather airport data and use only performance data applicable to those conditions.
- **Assuming the calculation guarantees performance:** add margin and identify what would make actual performance worse.
- **Correcting a CG error by guesswork:** recompute every proposed movement, addition, or removal before loading the aircraft.
