# Task C. Runway Incursion Avoidance

**Area II — Technical Subject Areas**

## Objective

To determine the applicant understands runway incursion avoidance, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate appropriate skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

A runway incursion is a surface event that creates a collision hazard or loss of separation with an aircraft taking off, landing, or intending to land. Preventing incursions depends on **clear communication, airport knowledge, and flight-deck procedures that maintain orientation**.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested Schedule**

- Ground lesson: approximately 45 minutes
- Airport-diagram taxi clearance exercise: approximately 15 minutes

**Equipment**

- Airport diagram for the training airport
- Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge
- Airplane Flying Handbook
- AC 91-73B and AC 90-66C
- CFI Airplane ACS
- Flight Guide visual aids

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner Actions**

- Define a runway incursion and identify its three major contributors.
- Copy, read back, and trace a taxi clearance on an airport diagram.
- Identify hold lines, runway signs, lights, and Hot Spots.
- Explain actions for uncertainty, low visibility, and after-landing taxi.

**Instructor Actions**

- Teach a deliberate airport-surface briefing and taxi procedure.
- Require accurate clearance readbacks and positive runway-crossing confirmation.
- Model sterile-flight-deck discipline and continuous position awareness.
- Stop the aircraft and clarify any uncertainty before an incursion occurs.

## Completion Standards

The applicant can teach the elements and techniques for runway-incursion avoidance, including airport diagrams, taxi instructions, markings, signs, lights, Hot Spots, ATC coordination, non-towered operations, and appropriate risk management.

# Teach

## AI.II.C.K1 — Runway Incursion Definition

A **runway incursion** is any occurrence at an airport involving an aircraft, vehicle, person, or object on the ground that creates a collision hazard or results in loss of separation with an aircraft taking off, landing, or intending to land.

The three major contributors are **communications with ATC, airport knowledge, and flight-deck procedures for maintaining orientation**. Incursions develop quickly during routine surface operations, leaving little time for corrective action.

Common examples include **taxiing past a runway hold line without authorization, making a wrong turn into a runway area, entering a runway without clearance, or continuing beyond a hold line after landing toward an intersecting runway**. These events may begin with a small position, communication, or attention error but can create an immediate conflict with landing or departing traffic.

### Runway Incursion Categories

- **Category A:** a serious incident in which a collision was narrowly avoided. *Illustrative example:* an aircraft enters an active runway as another aircraft begins its takeoff roll, requiring immediate evasive action to avoid a collision.
- **Category B:** separation decreases with significant collision potential and a time-critical evasive response may be needed. *Illustrative example:* an aircraft crosses a runway ahead of landing traffic, leaving the arriving pilot little time to go around or avoid the conflict.
- **Category C:** there is ample time and/or distance to avoid a collision. *Illustrative example:* a pilot turns onto a runway without authorization, but conflicting traffic is far enough away that the conflict is resolved without a time-critical response.
- **Category D:** the event meets the runway-incursion definition but has no immediate safety consequence. *Illustrative example:* an aircraft, vehicle, or person is incorrectly present in a protected runway area while no conflicting traffic is immediately involved.

The categories describe the **severity of the event**, not the pilot's intent. Every category is a runway-incursion event and should be examined for the communication, airport-knowledge, or orientation breakdown that allowed it to occur.

## AI.II.C.K2 — Taxi Instructions and Clearances

At a towered airport, taxi safety depends on a communication loop: ATC issues a clearance, the pilot understands it, reads back required restrictions, and follows the route while monitoring position. **Read back every hold-short instruction** and use standard phraseology with the complete aircraft call sign.

Before entering or crossing a runway, be positive that the clearance authorizes that action. If there is confusion about the clearance, airplane position, or traffic, **stop and ask ATC to clarify**. A line-up-and-wait clearance requires particular vigilance; scan the runway and final approach, and contact ATC if the situation or expected communication is unclear.

A taxi clearance identifies a **destination and route**. Before departure, obtain the route before entering the movement area. After landing, remain clear of the runway, then obtain and follow the taxi route to parking. Do not treat a clearance to taxi as authority to cross a runway unless the runway crossing is explicitly included in the clearance.

## AI.II.C.K3 — Recording Taxi Instructions and Reviewing the Airport Diagram

Keep a current airport diagram available and review the complete taxi plan before moving. For complex or unfamiliar routes, record the clearance and trace it on the diagram. Mark or otherwise identify the destination, turns, runway crossings, hold-short restrictions, complex intersections, and Hot Spots.

Written taxi instructions support accurate readback and reduce misunderstanding or forgetting. The level of detail should fit airport complexity and familiarity, but a diagram remains a continuous position-reference tool during taxi.

### Airport Hot Spots

Airport Hot Spot information forewarns pilots and others operating in a movement area about **confusing airport geometry and intersections**. Before taxiing, locate any Hot Spots along the planned route and identify where the aircraft must hold short of runways not cleared for crossing. This reduces heads-down time, supports outside scanning, and helps the pilot comply with the taxi clearance.

When a Hot Spot is reached, slow or stop as needed, verify the aircraft position against the airport diagram, identify the clearance limit and runway boundary, and clarify with ATC before proceeding if any uncertainty remains. The FAA Hot Spot resource in Prepare provides current standardized symbology information as a supplemental reference.

## AI.II.C.K4 — Airport Markings, Signs, and Lights

Runway-holding position markings identify the boundary for a runway or protected area. At a controlled airport, do not cross from the solid-line side toward the dashed-line side without the required clearance. Learn the signs, markings, and lighting that identify runways, taxiways, holding positions, and routes, then use them with the airport diagram and ATC clearance to confirm position.

The **red runway-holding-position sign** is a visual cue that a holding-position marking is nearby. Use the sign, pavement marking, airport diagram, and clearance together rather than relying on one cue alone.

### Runway Holding Position

**Runway holding position markings are four yellow lines: two solid and two dashed.** They extend across the taxiway, align with the runway holding-position sign, and show where the aircraft must stop when approaching a runway.

- **Approaching a runway:** the double solid lines face the aircraft. Stop before any part of the aircraft crosses the first solid line. At a controlled airport, do not cross the double solid lines until ATC clears the aircraft to enter or cross the runway.
- **Exiting a runway:** the double dashed lines face the aircraft. The entire aircraft, including the tail, must cross both the dashed and solid lines to be clear of the runway safety area. An ATC clearance is not required to cross the marking while exiting the runway.
- **Non-towered or closed-tower operation:** do not taxi onto or across a runway until it is clear and no aircraft are on final approach. Use extreme caution and look both ways.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
The solid side means stop. Before crossing it, identify the runway, confirm the clearance, scan the runway and final approach, then verify the entire aircraft is clear before resuming nonessential tasks after landing.
]]

Taxiway edge lights are **blue**, taxiway centerline lights are **green**, and runway guard lights are flashing or in-pavement **yellow** lights that increase the conspicuity of taxiway/runway intersections. Stop bars are **red** and are used with ATC procedures in low-visibility operations.

**Runway Status Lights (RWSL)** are red warnings that it is unsafe to enter, cross, take off, or land on a runway; they indicate runway status, not an ATC clearance. **Runway Entrance Lights (REL)** warn aircraft approaching or crossing from an intersecting taxiway that traffic conflicts with runway entry. **Takeoff Hold Lights (THL)** warn an aircraft in position for takeoff that the runway is occupied or unsafe for takeoff.

## AI.II.C.K5 — Flight Deck Activities During Taxiing

Taxiing is controlled movement on the airport surface and requires awareness of the ramp, parking areas, taxiways, runway environment, people, equipment, and other aircraft. Keep eyes outside, scan near and far, follow the aircraft's progress on the airport diagram, and update the next location requiring increased attention.

Plan and brief Hot Spots, complex intersections, runway crossings, expected runway exits, and taxiways between parallel runways before taxi or landing. Hot Spots are areas with a history or potential risk of collision or runway incursion; treat them as additional decision points.

### Sterile Flight Deck and Workload Management

Maintain a **sterile flight deck** during taxi, takeoff, climb to cruise, descent, approach, landing, and taxi after landing. Limit conversation to safety-essential matters; silence or turn off cell phones and other personal devices; do not text; and ask passengers to avoid unnecessary conversation. Complete programming, EFB setup, checklists, and other heads-down work before taxi whenever practical.

Taxi requires continuous outside attention. Airplanes have limited ground maneuverability, cannot move in reverse under their own power, and may have wing, tail, or propeller clearance limitations that are not obvious from the pilot seat. Maintain a mental picture of the aircraft's position and the movement of other aircraft, vehicles, and people; slow or stop when visibility, clearance, or position is uncertain.

### Taxi Route and Runway-Crossing Discipline

The pilot must not let **expectation or confirmation bias** substitute a familiar expected clearance for the clearance actually issued. Compare each sign, marking, and intersection with the diagram and clearance. When approaching a runway holding position, refer to the clearance and positively verbalize the authorization, for example: **“Cleared to cross Runway 31.”** If the clearance or aircraft position is uncertain, request clarification or progressive taxi instructions before moving.

### After Landing, Parallel Runways, Night, and Low Visibility

Brief the expected runway exit before landing, especially when an exit leads near another runway or when operating between parallel runways. After landing, remain alert until the **entire aircraft** is clear of the landing runway; do not begin nonessential communication, frequency changes, or configuration changes while still in the runway environment.

At night or in low visibility, signs, markings, hold lines, other aircraft, and vehicles are more difficult to identify. Use the diagram and available lighting, slow or stop when needed, and clarify any instruction that does not match the planned or briefed route. On taxiways between parallel runways, verify the specific hold line and clearance limit before crossing either runway.

### Surface Conspicuity and LAHSO

Before entering or crossing a runway, scan the runway and final approach even when cleared. Use appropriate exterior lighting to improve conspicuity, maintain eyes outside while in runway areas, and taxi clear of the runway without unnecessary delay. If offered a **LAHSO** clearance, accept it only when able to comply; after accepting, brief the landing and the hold-short point so the rollout does not continue past the assigned limit.

## AI.II.C.K6 — Communication and Operations at Uncontrolled Airports

At an uncontrolled airport, the absence of ATC requires increased vigilance. Use the current airport diagram and Chart Supplement, determine the runway and traffic pattern, monitor the CTAF, announce intentions using clear and specific language, and keep scanning because not all aircraft are radio-equipped.

Review the airport diagram before taxi, takeoff, and landing so the runway entry, expected exit, and surface route are known. During taxi and before entering or crossing a runway, monitor and communicate on the appropriate CTAF. Scan the full length of the runway and the final-approach and departure paths. Before takeoff, announce the runway, intended departure, initial altitude, and direction of flight. On arrival and after landing, continue to communicate and scan until clear of the runway environment.

An ATC IFR release is not itself authorization to take off from a non-towered airport; resolve traffic conflicts first. If unfamiliarity, traffic, or visibility leaves doubt about runway status or aircraft position, stop and reassess rather than continuing on assumption.

## Instructor Application — Teaching Runway Incursion Avoidance

Give the learner a complex taxi clearance on an airport diagram. Require the learner to copy the route, read back the hold-short restrictions, highlight the route and Hot Spots, and verbalize each runway-crossing decision. Introduce a last-minute route change, a similar call sign, low visibility, or an after-landing parallel-runway exit. The learner must stop, verify position and clearance, clarify with ATC when needed, and continue only when the route and runway status are understood.
