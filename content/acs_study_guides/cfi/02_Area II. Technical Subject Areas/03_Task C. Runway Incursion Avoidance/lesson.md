# Task C. Runway Incursion Avoidance

**Area II — Technical Subject Areas**

## Objective

To determine the applicant understands runway incursion avoidance, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate appropriate skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

A runway incursion is a surface event that creates a collision hazard or loss of separation with an aircraft taking off, landing, or intending to land. Preventing incursions depends on **clear communication, airport knowledge, and flight-deck procedures that maintain orientation**.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested Schedule**

- Ground lesson: approximately 45 minutes
- Airport-diagram taxi clearance exercise: approximately 15 minutes

**Equipment**

- Airport diagram for the training airport
- Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge
- Airplane Flying Handbook
- AC 91-73B and AC 90-66C
- CFI Airplane ACS
- Flight Guide visual aids

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner Actions**

- Define a runway incursion and identify its three major contributors.
- Copy, read back, and trace a taxi clearance on an airport diagram.
- Identify hold lines, runway signs, lights, and Hot Spots.
- Explain actions for uncertainty, low visibility, and after-landing taxi.

**Instructor Actions**

- Teach a deliberate airport-surface briefing and taxi procedure.
- Require accurate clearance readbacks and positive runway-crossing confirmation.
- Model sterile-flight-deck discipline and continuous position awareness.
- Stop the aircraft and clarify any uncertainty before an incursion occurs.

## Completion Standards

The applicant can teach the elements and techniques for runway-incursion avoidance, including airport diagrams, taxi instructions, markings, signs, lights, Hot Spots, ATC coordination, non-towered operations, and appropriate risk management.

## ACS Elements

### Knowledge

- **AI.II.C.K1** — Runway incursion definition.

- **AI.II.C.R2** — Confirmation or expectation bias as related to taxi instructions.
- **AI.II.C.R3** — Entering or crossing runways.
- **AI.II.C.R4** — Night taxi operations.
- **AI.II.C.R5** — Low visibility taxi operations.
- **AI.II.C.R6** — Runway incursion after landing.

**There is one main idea in this lesson:** runway incursions are prevented by maintaining **positive control of the airplane, positive awareness of where we are, and positive understanding of what we are cleared to do.**

The FAA repeatedly comes back to three contributing areas:

**Communication · Airport Knowledge · Flight Deck Procedures for Maintaining Orientation**

So the goal is not simply to recognize a hold-short line. The learner should be able to **understand the clearance, follow the route, recognize runway boundaries, maintain position awareness, manage workload, and stop when uncertainty develops.**

---

### 1. Runway Incursion — Know What We Are Preventing

- **A runway incursion is the incorrect presence of an aircraft, vehicle, or person on the protected area of a surface designated for the landing and takeoff of aircraft.**

- An incursion may begin with something small:
    - A misunderstood clearance.
    - A wrong turn.
    - A missed sign.
    - A distraction.
    - An assumption about position.

- **Once the airplane enters a protected runway area incorrectly, there may be very little time to correct the error.**

- The three major areas that repeatedly contribute to runway incursions are:

    - **Communication:** Did we correctly hear, understand, read back, and comply with the instruction?
    - **Airport knowledge:** Do we understand the airport layout, signs, markings, lighting, Hot Spots, and runway relationships?
    - **Orientation:** Do we continuously know where the airplane is and what comes next?

- **That gives us the framework for the lesson. The next question is how we make sure the taxi instruction we received is the instruction we actually execute.**

---

### 2. Taxi Instructions and Clearances

- **At a towered airport, safe taxi begins with a clear communication loop.**

- ATC issues the instruction.

- The pilot:
    - Listens.
    - Understands the route.
    - Reads back required runway restrictions.
    - Follows the clearance while continuously checking position.

- **Read back runway crossing and hold-short instructions.**

- Use the complete aircraft call sign and standard phraseology.

- **A taxi clearance gives us a route and a clearance limit.**

- Do not allow familiarity with the airport or an expected route to replace the clearance actually issued.

- **Before entering or crossing a runway, be certain the clearance authorizes that action.**

- A taxi clearance does not automatically authorize crossing every runway encountered along the route.

- **If there is uncertainty about the clearance or the airplane's position, stop.**

- Ask ATC to clarify.

- Request progressive taxi instructions when appropriate.

- **Aviation example:**  
  ATC gives:

  **“Taxi to Runway 17 via Alpha, Bravo, hold short Runway 12.”**

  The learner should:
    - Identify Alpha and Bravo.
    - Read back the hold-short restriction.
    - Locate Runway 12.
    - Know where the airplane must stop before moving.

- **Even a correctly understood clearance can be forgotten or misapplied if the pilot is trying to hold the entire route in memory. That is why the FAA emphasizes recording taxi instructions and reviewing the route on the airport diagram.**

---

### 3. Record the Clearance and Use the Airport Diagram

- **The airport diagram should be part of the taxi operation, not something we look at only during preflight planning.**

- Before taxi:
    - Review the route.
    - Identify the destination.
    - Identify runway crossings.
    - Identify hold-short restrictions.
    - Locate complex intersections.
    - Identify Hot Spots.

- **For complex or unfamiliar taxi instructions, write them down.**

- Then trace or follow the route on the airport diagram.

- As we taxi, we should be able to answer:

    - Where am I?
    - What taxiway am I on?
    - What intersection is next?
    - Where is the next runway?
    - Where is my clearance limit?

#### Hot Spots

- **A Hot Spot identifies airport geometry or an intersection with a history or potential risk of collision or runway incursion.**

- Brief Hot Spots before movement whenever possible.

- At a Hot Spot:
    - Slow down.
    - Verify position.
    - Compare the diagram with the signs and markings outside.
    - Confirm the clearance.
    - Stop if anything does not agree.

- **The goal is to solve the confusing part before the airplane reaches it.**

- **The diagram tells us what we expect to see. The next part of the lesson is recognizing those same boundaries outside the airplane.**

---

### 4. Airport Markings, Signs, and Lights

- **Airport signs, pavement markings, and lights provide the visual language that keeps the pilot oriented on the surface.**

- We use those cues together with:
    - The airport diagram.
    - The taxi clearance.
    - The outside scan.

#### Runway Holding-Position Markings

- **A runway holding-position marking consists of four yellow lines—two solid and two dashed.**

- Approaching a runway from the taxiway side:
    - The **solid lines face the aircraft**.
    - Stop before crossing the marking when authorization is required.

- Exiting a runway:
    - The **dashed lines face the aircraft**.
    - Continue until the **entire aircraft**, including the tail, is clear of the marking.

- **Do not treat the nose crossing the line as being clear of the runway.**

#### Signs and Lighting

- **Mandatory instruction signs are red with white inscriptions.**

- Common lighting cues include:
    - **Blue:** Taxiway edge lights.
    - **Green:** Taxiway centerline lights.
    - **Yellow:** Runway guard lights.
    - **Red:** Stop bars where installed.

- **Runway Status Lights are warning systems—not ATC clearances.**

- If red Runway Entrance Lights or Takeoff Hold Lights are illuminated:
    - Do not enter.
    - Do not cross.
    - Do not begin takeoff.
    - Resolve the conflict with ATC.

- **Before entering or crossing any runway, use more than one cue.**

- Identify:
    - The runway.
    - The clearance.
    - The sign and marking.
    - Traffic on the runway.
    - Final approach when appropriate.

- **Knowing the signs and markings is necessary, but it is not enough if the pilot is distracted, heads-down, or assuming the next turn. That brings us to flight-deck activity during taxi.**

---

### 5. Flight Deck Activities During Taxi

- **Taxi is an active phase of flight.**

- The pilot is controlling an aircraft in an environment containing:
    - Other aircraft.
    - Vehicles.
    - People.
    - Runways.
    - Taxiways.
    - Signs.
    - Lighting.
    - ATC instructions.

- **Maintain an outside scan and continuous position awareness.**

- Keep the airport diagram available.

- Continuously compare:
    - What we expected.
    - What ATC actually cleared.
    - What the diagram shows.
    - What we see outside.

#### Sterile Flight Deck

- **Limit nonessential activity during taxi.**

- Complete as much heads-down work as practical before movement:
    - EFB setup.
    - Avionics programming.
    - Checklist preparation.
    - Route review.

- Passengers should understand that unnecessary conversation during taxi can create risk.

- **If a heads-down task becomes necessary and position awareness begins to degrade, stop the airplane in an appropriate location before continuing the task.**

#### Expectation and Confirmation Bias

- **Expectation bias occurs when we perceive what we expected rather than what was actually communicated or displayed.**

- A familiar airport creates special risk because the pilot may expect the usual route or runway.

- **Confirmation bias can reinforce the mistake.**
- Once the pilot believes the route is correct, signs or instructions may be interpreted as supporting that belief.

- The defense is active verification:
    - Listen to the actual clearance.
    - Compare it with the diagram.
    - Read the signs.
    - Verify the intersection.
    - Re-brief if the route or runway changes.

#### Runway Crossing

- **Treat every runway crossing as a deliberate decision point.**

- Before crossing:
    - Confirm the specific runway.
    - Confirm the clearance.
    - Scan for traffic.
    - Verify that entering the runway is safe.

- **The practical rule is simple: if the clearance, diagram, and outside picture do not agree, stop and resolve the disagreement before continuing.**

- **At a towered airport, ATC can help clarify the route. At a non-towered airport, the same surface awareness is required without a controller managing the movement.**

---

### 6. Non-Towered Airport Operations

- **At a non-towered airport, increased vigilance is required because there is no ground controller separating surface traffic.**

- Before operating:
    - Review the airport diagram.
    - Review the Chart Supplement and applicable airport information.
    - Determine the runway and traffic pattern.
    - Monitor the CTAF.
    - Understand runway and taxiway relationships.

- **Communicate clearly, but never assume every aircraft is transmitting or even equipped with a radio.**

- Radio calls supplement the visual scan; they do not replace it.

- During taxi and before entering or crossing a runway:
    - Monitor the CTAF.
    - Announce intentions as appropriate.
    - Scan the runway.
    - Scan final approach.
    - Look for departing and landing traffic.

- **Before takeoff, verify the runway is clear and the approach path does not contain a conflict.**

- **An IFR release from ATC is not itself authorization to take off from a non-towered airport.**

- The pilot still has to resolve local traffic and runway conflicts.

- **The same principle applies here as at a towered airport: if runway status, position, or traffic is uncertain, stop and reassess rather than continuing on assumption.**

- **Those procedures work well when the pilot maintains awareness. The ACS also asks us to understand the specific situations where that awareness is most likely to break down.**

---

### 7. Runway-Incursion Risk Management

#### Distractions, Task Prioritization, Loss of Situational Awareness, or Disorientation

- **Taxi requires continuous orientation.**

- Distractions and excessive heads-down activity can cause the pilot to:
    - Miss a turn.
    - Cross a hold line.
    - Lose track of position.
    - Forget a restriction.

- **Prioritize movement of the aircraft first.**

- If another task competes with safe taxi:
    - Slow down.
    - Stop if necessary.
    - Reestablish orientation before continuing.

#### Confirmation or Expectation Bias

- **Never substitute the route you expected for the route ATC actually issued.**

- This risk increases:
    - At familiar airports.
    - During routine operations.
    - When a clearance changes at the last moment.

- A route or runway change should trigger a deliberate re-brief.

#### Entering or Crossing Runways

- **A runway boundary deserves an intentional pause in the pilot's thinking.**

- Confirm:
    - Which runway is ahead.
    - Whether the clearance authorizes entry or crossing.
    - Whether the runway and approach path are clear.

- **Do not rely on memory alone.**

#### Night Taxi Operations

- **Night reduces visual cues and can make signs, intersections, and pavement geometry more difficult to interpret.**

- Counter that risk by:
    - Reviewing the route in advance.
    - Using the airport diagram.
    - Slowing down.
    - Using lighting and signs deliberately.
    - Stopping when position is uncertain.

#### Low-Visibility Taxi Operations

- **Low visibility reduces the distance available to recognize signs, markings, traffic, and intersections.**

- Use:
    - The airport diagram.
    - Available lighting.
    - ATC instructions.
    - Progressive taxi when appropriate.

- **Slow or stop rather than trying to maintain normal taxi speed while uncertain.**

#### Runway Incursion After Landing

- **The risk does not end at touchdown.**

- After landing, workload remains high and pilots may begin:
    - Configuration changes.
    - Frequency changes.
    - Checklist actions.
    - Passenger conversation.

- **First clear the runway completely.**

- The entire aircraft must be beyond the runway holding-position marking.

- Then establish the taxi route before allowing nonessential activity to compete for attention.

#### Taxiways Between Parallel Runways

- **Taxiways between parallel runways create multiple runway boundaries close together.**

- Clearing one runway does not automatically authorize entering or crossing the next.

- Know:
    - Which runway was just exited.
    - Which runway lies ahead.
    - Which hold line applies.
    - The exact clearance limit.

- **Treat each runway as a separate decision and separate clearance requirement.**

---

### Close

- **Runway-incursion avoidance comes back to three things:**

**Communication · Airport Knowledge · Orientation**

- **Communication:** Understand and comply with the clearance actually issued.
- **Airport knowledge:** Know the diagram, Hot Spots, signs, markings, lights, and runway relationships.
- **Orientation:** Always know where the airplane is, what comes next, and where the airplane must stop.

- The practical process is:

**Plan the route → Copy and understand the clearance → Maintain position awareness → Verify every runway boundary → Stop when uncertain**

- **The goal is not a learner who can merely identify a hold-short line on a picture.**

- It is a learner who approaches every runway boundary deliberately, maintains orientation throughout surface movement, and stops before uncertainty becomes a runway incursion.



## AI.II.C.K1 — Runway Incursion Definition

A **runway incursion** is any occurrence at an airport involving an aircraft, vehicle, person, or object on the ground that creates a collision hazard or results in loss of separation with an aircraft taking off, landing, or intending to land.

The three major contributors are **communications with ATC, airport knowledge, and flight-deck procedures for maintaining orientation**. Incursions develop quickly during routine surface operations, leaving little time for corrective action.

Common examples include **taxiing past a runway hold line without authorization, making a wrong turn into a runway area, entering a runway without clearance, or continuing beyond a hold line after landing toward an intersecting runway**. These events may begin with a small position, communication, or attention error but can create an immediate conflict with landing or departing traffic.

### Runway Incursion Categories

- **Category A:** a serious incident in which a collision was narrowly avoided. *Illustrative example:* an aircraft enters an active runway as another aircraft begins its takeoff roll, requiring immediate evasive action to avoid a collision.
- **Category B:** separation decreases with significant collision potential and a time-critical evasive response may be needed. *Illustrative example:* an aircraft crosses a runway ahead of landing traffic, leaving the arriving pilot little time to go around or avoid the conflict.
- **Category C:** there is ample time and/or distance to avoid a collision. *Illustrative example:* a pilot turns onto a runway without authorization, but conflicting traffic is far enough away that the conflict is resolved without a time-critical response.
- **Category D:** the event meets the runway-incursion definition but has no immediate safety consequence. *Illustrative example:* an aircraft, vehicle, or person is incorrectly present in a protected runway area while no conflicting traffic is immediately involved.

The categories describe the **severity of the event**, not the pilot's intent. Every category is a runway-incursion event and should be examined for the communication, airport-knowledge, or orientation breakdown that allowed it to occur.

## AI.II.C.K2 — Taxi Instructions and Clearances

At a towered airport, taxi safety depends on a communication loop: ATC issues a clearance, the pilot understands it, reads back required restrictions, and follows the route while monitoring position. **Read back every hold-short instruction** and use standard phraseology with the complete aircraft call sign.

Before entering or crossing a runway, be positive that the clearance authorizes that action. If there is confusion about the clearance, airplane position, or traffic, **stop and ask ATC to clarify**. A line-up-and-wait clearance requires particular vigilance; scan the runway and final approach, and contact ATC if the situation or expected communication is unclear.

A taxi clearance identifies a **destination and route**. Before departure, obtain the route before entering the movement area. After landing, remain clear of the runway, then obtain and follow the taxi route to parking. Do not treat a clearance to taxi as authority to cross a runway unless the runway crossing is explicitly included in the clearance.

## AI.II.C.K3 — Recording Taxi Instructions and Reviewing the Airport Diagram

Keep a current airport diagram available and review the complete taxi plan before moving. For complex or unfamiliar routes, record the clearance and trace it on the diagram. Mark or otherwise identify the destination, turns, runway crossings, hold-short restrictions, complex intersections, and Hot Spots.

Written taxi instructions support accurate readback and reduce misunderstanding or forgetting. The level of detail should fit airport complexity and familiarity, but a diagram remains a continuous position-reference tool during taxi.

### Airport Hot Spots

Airport Hot Spot information forewarns pilots and others operating in a movement area about **confusing airport geometry and intersections**. Before taxiing, locate any Hot Spots along the planned route and identify where the aircraft must hold short of runways not cleared for crossing. This reduces heads-down time, supports outside scanning, and helps the pilot comply with the taxi clearance.

When a Hot Spot is reached, slow or stop as needed, verify the aircraft position against the airport diagram, identify the clearance limit and runway boundary, and clarify with ATC before proceeding if any uncertainty remains. The FAA Hot Spot resource in Prepare provides current standardized symbology information as a supplemental reference.

## AI.II.C.K4 — Airport Markings, Signs, and Lights

Runway-holding position markings identify the boundary for a runway or protected area. At a controlled airport, do not cross from the solid-line side toward the dashed-line side without the required clearance. Learn the signs, markings, and lighting that identify runways, taxiways, holding positions, and routes, then use them with the airport diagram and ATC clearance to confirm position.

The **red runway-holding-position sign** is a visual cue that a holding-position marking is nearby. Use the sign, pavement marking, airport diagram, and clearance together rather than relying on one cue alone.

### Runway Holding Position

**Runway holding position markings are four yellow lines: two solid and two dashed.** They extend across the taxiway, align with the runway holding-position sign, and show where the aircraft must stop when approaching a runway.

- **Approaching a runway:** the double solid lines face the aircraft. Stop before any part of the aircraft crosses the first solid line. At a controlled airport, do not cross the double solid lines until ATC clears the aircraft to enter or cross the runway.
- **Exiting a runway:** the double dashed lines face the aircraft. The entire aircraft, including the tail, must cross both the dashed and solid lines to be clear of the runway safety area. An ATC clearance is not required to cross the marking while exiting the runway.
- **Non-towered or closed-tower operation:** do not taxi onto or across a runway until it is clear and no aircraft are on final approach. Use extreme caution and look both ways.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
The solid side means stop. Before crossing it, identify the runway, confirm the clearance, scan the runway and final approach, then verify the entire aircraft is clear before resuming nonessential tasks after landing.
]]

Taxiway edge lights are **blue**, taxiway centerline lights are **green**, and runway guard lights are flashing or in-pavement **yellow** lights that increase the conspicuity of taxiway/runway intersections. Stop bars are **red** and are used with ATC procedures in low-visibility operations.

**Runway Status Lights (RWSL)** are red warnings that it is unsafe to enter, cross, take off, or land on a runway; they indicate runway status, not an ATC clearance. **Runway Entrance Lights (REL)** warn aircraft approaching or crossing from an intersecting taxiway that traffic conflicts with runway entry. **Takeoff Hold Lights (THL)** warn an aircraft in position for takeoff that the runway is occupied or unsafe for takeoff.

## AI.II.C.K5 — Flight Deck Activities During Taxiing

Taxiing is controlled movement on the airport surface and requires awareness of the ramp, parking areas, taxiways, runway environment, people, equipment, and other aircraft. Keep eyes outside, scan near and far, follow the aircraft's progress on the airport diagram, and update the next location requiring increased attention.

Plan and brief Hot Spots, complex intersections, runway crossings, expected runway exits, and taxiways between parallel runways before taxi or landing. Hot Spots are areas with a history or potential risk of collision or runway incursion; treat them as additional decision points.

### Sterile Flight Deck and Workload Management

Maintain a **sterile flight deck** during taxi, takeoff, climb to cruise, descent, approach, landing, and taxi after landing. Limit conversation to safety-essential matters; silence or turn off cell phones and other personal devices; do not text; and ask passengers to avoid unnecessary conversation. Complete programming, EFB setup, checklists, and other heads-down work before taxi whenever practical.

Taxi requires continuous outside attention. Airplanes have limited ground maneuverability, cannot move in reverse under their own power, and may have wing, tail, or propeller clearance limitations that are not obvious from the pilot seat. Maintain a mental picture of the aircraft's position and the movement of other aircraft, vehicles, and people; slow or stop when visibility, clearance, or position is uncertain.

### Taxi Route and Runway-Crossing Discipline

The pilot must not let **expectation or confirmation bias** substitute a familiar expected clearance for the clearance actually issued. Compare each sign, marking, and intersection with the diagram and clearance. When approaching a runway holding position, refer to the clearance and positively verbalize the authorization, for example: **“Cleared to cross Runway 31.”** If the clearance or aircraft position is uncertain, request clarification or progressive taxi instructions before moving.

### After Landing, Parallel Runways, Night, and Low Visibility

Brief the expected runway exit before landing, especially when an exit leads near another runway or when operating between parallel runways. After landing, remain alert until the **entire aircraft** is clear of the landing runway; do not begin nonessential communication, frequency changes, or configuration changes while still in the runway environment.

At night or in low visibility, signs, markings, hold lines, other aircraft, and vehicles are more difficult to identify. Use the diagram and available lighting, slow or stop when needed, and clarify any instruction that does not match the planned or briefed route. On taxiways between parallel runways, verify the specific hold line and clearance limit before crossing either runway.

### Surface Conspicuity and LAHSO

Before entering or crossing a runway, scan the runway and final approach even when cleared. Use appropriate exterior lighting to improve conspicuity, maintain eyes outside while in runway areas, and taxi clear of the runway without unnecessary delay. If offered a **LAHSO** clearance, accept it only when able to comply; after accepting, brief the landing and the hold-short point so the rollout does not continue past the assigned limit.

## AI.II.C.K6 — Communication and Operations at Uncontrolled Airports

At an uncontrolled airport, the absence of ATC requires increased vigilance. Use the current airport diagram and Chart Supplement, determine the runway and traffic pattern, monitor the CTAF, announce intentions using clear and specific language, and keep scanning because not all aircraft are radio-equipped.

Review the airport diagram before taxi, takeoff, and landing so the runway entry, expected exit, and surface route are known. During taxi and before entering or crossing a runway, monitor and communicate on the appropriate CTAF. Scan the full length of the runway and the final-approach and departure paths. Before takeoff, announce the runway, intended departure, initial altitude, and direction of flight. On arrival and after landing, continue to communicate and scan until clear of the runway environment.

An ATC IFR release is not itself authorization to take off from a non-towered airport; resolve traffic conflicts first. If unfamiliarity, traffic, or visibility leaves doubt about runway status or aircraft position, stop and reassess rather than continuing on assumption.

## Instructor Application — Teaching Runway Incursion Avoidance

Give the learner a complex taxi clearance on an airport diagram. Require the learner to copy the route, read back the hold-short restrictions, highlight the route and Hot Spots, and verbalize each runway-crossing decision. Introduce a last-minute route change, a similar call sign, low visibility, or an after-landing parallel-runway exit. The learner must stop, verify position and clearance, clarify with ATC when needed, and continue only when the route and runway status are understood.

### 1. Define a runway incursion and name its three major contributors.

[[answer:
A runway incursion involves an aircraft, vehicle, person, or object on the ground that creates a collision hazard or loss of separation with an aircraft taking off, landing, or intending to land. The contributors are communication, airport knowledge, and flight-deck procedures that maintain orientation.

### 2. What should a pilot do before crossing a runway hold line?

[[answer:
Be positive that the clearance authorizes entering or crossing, verify the airplane position and route, scan the runway and final approach as appropriate, and stop to clarify with ATC if any uncertainty remains.

### 3. Why should taxi clearances be recorded and traced on an airport diagram?

[[answer:
Written instructions and diagram review support accurate readback, confirm the route and restrictions, and reduce misunderstanding or forgetting, especially on complex or unfamiliar airport layouts.

[[answer:
They are red warning lights that indicate it is unsafe to enter, cross, take off, or land on a runway. They show runway status and do not constitute an ATC clearance.

[[answer:
A pilot may expect a familiar route or clearance and perceive it as expected rather than as actually issued. Compare the received clearance with the diagram and stop to clarify when it does not match the planned route.
]]

### 6. What should a pilot do after landing before beginning nonessential tasks?

[[answer:
Ensure the entire aircraft has crossed the runway hold-short line and is clear of all runways. Keep nonessential actions and communications deferred until clear of the runway environment.
]]

### 7. What extra precautions apply at night or in low visibility?

[[answer:
Brief the route and low-visibility requirements, use the airport diagram and available lights and signs, maintain enhanced position awareness, stop when uncertain, and obtain ATC clarification before continuing.
