# Task M. Night Operations

**Area II — Technical Subject Areas**

## Objective

Determine that the applicant understands night operations, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate the required skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Night flying offers real utility — smooth air, good visibility, favorable weather — but it carries distinct hazards. The eye is built for daylight, outside references disappear, illusions multiply, and a runway can hide in a sea of lights. A flight instructor must understand night vision, night illusions, aircraft and airport lighting, and the procedures that keep a night flight safe, and must be able to teach them.

This is a ground-only knowledge Task. The ACS skill element requires the applicant to **teach** at least one of the knowledge elements.

## Schedule / Equipment

- Ground: 20–30 minutes
- Student Q&A: 10 minutes
- Whiteboard / markers (optional)
- The night-vision, lighting, and landing figures used in this lesson

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** explain the factors that affect night vision and how to preserve it, the night illusions, the aircraft and airport lighting, and the procedures for preflight, taxi, takeoff, en route, approach, and landing at night; interpret traffic from position lights; and teach one element aloud.

**Instructor:** deliver the ground lesson, have the learner explain the night-vision physiology and the illusions with the figures, walk the night procedures, and require the learner to teach one element back.

## Completion Standards

The learner can explain:

- The physiology of night vision — the function of **rods and cones** and the **night blind spot**.
- How to **preserve night vision** and adjust cockpit lighting.
- The night **optical and spatial illusions** — false horizon, autokinesis, and the black-hole approach.
- **Aircraft equipment and lighting** requirements and **personal equipment** for night flight.
- **Airport and navigation lighting** — beacons, runway and taxiway lighting, VASI/PAPI, and pilot-controlled lighting.
- The procedures for **night preflight, taxi, takeoff, climb, orientation, approach, and landing**, and **night emergencies**.
- The importance of **increased reliance on flight instruments** at night.

# Teach

Teach night operations as a physiology problem before a procedures problem: the eye is built for daylight, so night flight demands preserving what little night vision the rods provide, compensating with the instruments and the lights, and flying procedures that assume fewer outside references. The Overview maps the ACS knowledge, risk, and skill elements; the sections below teach the material once, in the order an instructor would present it.

## AI.II.M.K1 — Night Vision: Rods, Cones, and the Blind Spot

The retina contains two kinds of photosensitive cells:

- **Cones** are concentrated in the center (the **fovea**) and provide **color vision and fine detail** in daylight. They are ineffective at night.
- **Rods** are distributed across the **periphery** — an area about 10,000 times more sensitive to light than the fovea. Rods provide **night vision and peripheral/motion detection**, in shades of gray, but no color or detail.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Eye_Rods_Cones.png" alt="Rods and cones of the retina" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>Rods and cones — cones cluster in the fovea for day detail; rods dominate the periphery for night vision.</figcaption>
</figure>

Vision shifts by light level: **photopic** (day — central cone vision), **mesopic** (dawn/dusk/full moon — a mix), and **scotopic** (night — peripheral rod vision only). This produces the **night blind spot**: because the fovea has almost no rods, an object looked at **directly** at night may fade or vanish. The blind spot covers the central 5–10 degrees. The countermeasure is **off-center viewing** — scan for traffic and obstacles with the peripheral vision, not the center.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Night_Blind_Spot.png" alt="The night blind spot in central vision" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>The night blind spot — with few rods in the fovea, an object viewed directly can disappear. Use off-center scanning.</figcaption>
</figure>

### Preserving night vision

**Dark adaptation** is the eye becoming more sensitive in darkness. Full adaptation takes up to **30 minutes**; exposure to a bright light for **1 second or more** temporarily impairs it (brief anti-collision flashes do not). Protect it:

- **Close one eye** when a bright light is unavoidable, preserving adaptation in the other.
- **Adjust cockpit lighting** to the minimum that allows reading instruments, and avoid reflections on the windshield.
- **Do not wear sunglasses after dark;** sunglasses during the day speed later dark adaptation.
- **Scan slowly,** blink if the eyes blur, and **use off-center viewing.**
- **Maintain physical condition** — avoid alcohol, tobacco, and self-medication; manage fatigue and hypoglycemia.

**Red cockpit lighting** preserves night vision but **severely distorts chart colors** (magenta and yellow read as red; cyan reads as black) and makes focusing difficult — use it only when optimum outside night vision is essential, and keep dim white light available for map and instrument reading.

### What degrades night vision

**Hypoxia** sharply reduces night vision — the rods lose function in low oxygen. Use **supplemental oxygen above 5,000 feet at night** (versus 10,000 feet by day). Carbon monoxide (smoking, exhaust) raises the pilot's physiological altitude; **fatigue**, self-imposed stresses, refractive errors, dirty windscreens, and flickering lights all degrade vision further.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
The night blind spot is the one fact a night pilot must act on: an object stared at directly can vanish. Teach the learner to scan for traffic with the peripheral vision and never fix the center on a dim target.
]]

## AI.II.M.K7 — Night Illusions

Darkness and few references make the pilot vulnerable to visual illusions. Teach the three that matter most:

- **False horizon** — ground lights confused with stars, a sloping cloud, or an aurora can be mistaken for the real horizon, leading to a misaligned, banked aircraft. Trust the instruments.
- **Autokinesis** — a stationary light stared at for many seconds appears to move; a pilot who follows it can lose control. Shift the gaze; do not fixate.
- **Featureless terrain / black-hole approach** — over water or unlit terrain with only the runway lights visible, the lack of peripheral cues makes the runway seem out of position (up- or down-sloping) and can lead to landing short. Use the VASI/PAPI or an electronic glide path, and **go around** if position or altitude is in doubt.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
At night the eyes lie. The answer to every night illusion is the same — believe the instruments, not the feeling. False horizon, autokinesis, and the black hole are all resolved on the attitude indicator and the glide path reference, not out the window.
]]

## AI.II.M.K3, K4 — Airplane and Personal Equipment

**Airplane equipment and lighting for night VFR** — the aircraft must carry the required night equipment (position/anti-collision lights per § 91.209, operable landing light if for hire, adequate source of electrical energy, spare fuses, and the day VFR instruments). Check **all** aircraft lights during preflight by turning them on momentarily; tap position lights for loose connections.

**Personal equipment** — within easy reach: a **white flashlight for the preflight** (to see true colors and leaks) and a **red flashlight for the cockpit** (to preserve night vision), with brightness set low; current charts; and spare batteries. Organize the cockpit before engine start.

## AI.II.M.K2 — Airport and Navigation Lighting

- **Rotating beacons** identify the airport: **alternating white/green** = lighted civilian land airport; **white/yellow** = water airport; **dual-peaked white flashes then green** = military. **Red flashes** mark obstructions or hazards.
- **Runway edge lights** are aviation white (aviation yellow in the last 2,000 feet — the caution zone); **threshold lights green, runway-end lights red**; **taxiway edge lights blue**.
- **Pilot-controlled lighting** is activated by radio clicks — typically **7 clicks for maximum intensity, then 5 or 3 to step down**; all lights stay on for **15 minutes**. Key the mike again on approach to ensure activation and a full lighting period.
- **VASI/PAPI** — a visual glide-path reference that is indispensable at night.

## AI.II.M.K5, K6, K8 — Night Procedures

### Preparation and preflight

Night flight demands more preflight care: review the weather with attention to the **temperature/dew-point spread** (a narrow spread warns of fog) and the **wind** (harder to detect at night). Draw course lines in **black** for low-light visibility, and use beacons, lighted obstructions, city lights, and highway traffic as checkpoints. Confirm the GPS works, with waypoints loaded and the database current.

### Taxi (K8)

Taxi **slowly**, especially in congested areas; follow the taxi lines, signs, and markings. Use the **taxi/landing light** to see, but avoid blinding other pilots, and limit the landing light if it overheats or overloads the electrical system. Do the instrument check while taxiing. **Verify position, route, and runway** against the airport diagram before takeoff — a wrong turn or wrong-surface takeoff is catastrophic. **Hold the brakes during the run-up** — the airplane can creep forward unnoticed at night.

### Takeoff and climb (instruments — K6)

The defining difference at night is the **loss of outside references** — so **fly the instruments more**. Line up on the centerline, note the heading indicator matches the runway direction, and advance power smoothly. On the climb, the **attitude indicator, VSI, and altimeter must all show a positive climb** — it is hard to tell height above the dark surface without them. **Do not turn below a safe maneuvering altitude.** An inexperienced pilot may relax right-rudder pressure after takeoff and veer left. Turn the landing light off once the climb is established (unless used for collision avoidance) — its reflection off haze or cloud distorts vision.

### Orientation and navigation

Avoid flying into clouds — the first sign is **ground lights disappearing**; a **halo or glow around lights** warns of ground fog. Crossing water at night risks a forced ditching and a horizon lost against the water. Lights seen from different altitudes can appear as a solid mass — an illusion that worsens with altitude change.

### Approach and landing

Identify the **runway and airport lighting early** — fly toward the beacon until the runway lights are distinguishable, and keep the airport in sight. **Distance and altitude are deceptive at night** — rely on the **altimeter and airspeed indicator**, and fly a **stabilized approach**, never a low, shallow one. Use the **VASI/PAPI** where available. Turn the **landing light on** about halfway through final (knowing it may reflect off haze). For the **round-out**, begin when the **landing lights reflect off the runway and the tire marks become visible**; without landing lights, begin the round-out when the **far-end runway lights first appear to rise above the nose**, and feel for the runway with power and pitch.

### Night emergencies and the go-around

A complete engine failure at night is the great concern. Maintain control and the **best glide**, turn toward an airport or away from congestion, troubleshoot (fuel selector, magnetos, primer), declare the emergency on the frequency in use, and if the terrain is known and suitable, plan the forced landing into an **unlighted area** — near public access if possible. Keep the wind in mind to avoid a downwind landing, and land at the **slowest possible airspeed** in the normal attitude. A **night go-around** is demanding — a black-hole or unstabilized approach is a reason to go around, not to salvage.

## AI.II.M.K9 — Traffic from Position Lights

Teach the learner to read another aircraft's **position lights** for its direction of flight: **red on the left (port) wingtip, green on the right (starboard) wingtip, white on the tail.** Seeing **red and green** together means the other aircraft is coming **toward** you; **red** alone means it is crossing right-to-left (you see its left side); **green** alone means left-to-right; **white** alone means it is heading away.

    <figcaption>Position lights — red left, green right, white tail. Read another aircraft's direction from which lights you see.</figcaption>
</figure>

[[mnemonic:
Red Right Returning — Reversed for the Other Guy |
Your own lights are red-left, green-right. But on another aircraft, red-and-green together mean it is coming toward you. Read the direction from the lights you see, not the ones you carry.
]]

## Risk Management — AI.II.M.R1 through R7

Teach the night risks with the recognize–assess–mitigate–verify sequence.

- **R1 — Inoperative equipment:** a failed landing light, position light, or instrument at night. Mitigate by checking all lights on preflight and having a backup flashlight; do not depart night VFR with required lighting inoperative.
- **R2 — Weather at night:** fog (narrow temperature/dew-point spread), obscured horizon, and hard-to-see clouds. Mitigate with a thorough briefing, conservative minimums, and avoiding VFR into marginal conditions.
- **R3 — Collision hazards:** fewer visible references and the night blind spot. Mitigate with off-center scanning, position-light interpretation, and the landing/anti-collision lights.
- **R4 — Distraction and disorientation:** illusions and lost references. Mitigate by trusting the instruments and recognizing autokinesis and false horizon early.
- **R5 — Visual illusions and night adaptation:** protect dark adaptation, use the VASI/PAPI and the instruments, and go around when in doubt.
- **R6 — Runway incursion:** at night, verify position and runway against the airport diagram before takeoff and before crossing any runway.
- **R7 — Night currency vs. proficiency:** being legal (recent night landings) is not the same as being proficient. Mitigate with regular night practice and honest self-assessment.

### Common Errors and Corrections

- **Failing to protect night vision** during taxi and preflight — dim the cockpit, use the red light, avoid bright light.
- **Operating with inoperative lighting or equipment** at night — check all lights before departure.
- **Flying by outside references alone** — increase reliance on the flight instruments.
- **A low, shallow, or black-hole approach flown too far** — use the VASI/PAPI and go around when position or altitude is in doubt.
- **Misreading another aircraft's lights** — red-and-green together means head-on.

## Oral / Checkride Review

1. What is the difference between rods and cones, and what is the night blind spot?

[[answer:
Cones concentrate in the fovea (center) and provide color and detail by day; rods dominate the periphery and provide night vision and motion detection in shades of gray, but no color or detail. Because the fovea has almost no rods, an object viewed directly at night can fade or vanish — the night blind spot, covering the central 5–10 degrees. The countermeasure is off-center viewing.
]]

2. How long does dark adaptation take, and how do you preserve it?

[[answer:
Full dark adaptation takes up to 30 minutes; a bright light of 1 second or more can erase it. Preserve it by dimming the cockpit, avoiding bright light, closing one eye when bright light is unavoidable, not wearing sunglasses after dark, scanning slowly, and using off-center viewing. Hypoxia (above 5,000 feet at night), carbon monoxide, fatigue, and self-imposed stresses all degrade it.
]]

3. When should you use supplemental oxygen at night, and why?

[[answer:
Above 5,000 feet at night (versus 10,000 feet by day), because hypoxia sharply reduces the rods' night-vision function. Carbon monoxide from smoking or exhaust raises the physiological altitude further.
]]

4. Describe the three hazardous night illusions and the countermeasure for each.

[[answer:
False horizon — ground lights, a sloping cloud, or an aurora mistaken for the horizon; trust the attitude indicator. Autokinesis — a stationary light stared at appears to move; shift the gaze, do not fixate. Black-hole approach — over water or unlit terrain with only the runway lights, the runway seems out of position and can cause landing short; use the VASI/PAPI or electronic glide path and go around if in doubt. In each case, believe the instruments over the feeling.
]]

5. How do you identify a civilian land airport, a water airport, and a military airport by beacon?

[[answer:
Lighted civilian land airport: alternating white and green. Civilian water airport: alternating white and yellow. Military: dual-peaked (two quick) white flashes then green. Red flashes mark obstructions or hazards.
]]

6. How does pilot-controlled lighting work?

[[answer:
Key the microphone on the airport frequency — typically 7 clicks for maximum intensity, then 5 or 3 to step down. All controlled lights stay on for 15 minutes from the most recent activation. Key again on approach to ensure the system is activated and a full lighting period is available.
]]

7. Why do you rely more on the flight instruments at night, especially on takeoff and approach?

[[answer:
Outside references are limited at night. On the climb, the attitude indicator, VSI, and altimeter confirm a positive climb that the dark surface cannot. On approach, distance and altitude are deceptive, so the altimeter and airspeed indicator keep the approach stabilized. The instruments are the truth when the outside references lie or disappear.
]]

8. What is the cue for the round-out on a night landing, with and without landing lights?

[[answer:
With landing lights, begin the round-out when the landing lights reflect on the runway and the tire marks become clearly visible. Without landing lights, begin when the far-end runway lights first appear to rise above the nose, then feel for the runway with power and pitch. A low, shallow approach is inappropriate at night.
]]

9. How do you read another aircraft's direction from its position lights?

[[answer:
Red is the left (port) wingtip, green the right (starboard), white the tail. Red and green together mean the aircraft is coming toward you; red alone means it is crossing right-to-left; green alone means left-to-right; white alone means it is heading away.
]]
