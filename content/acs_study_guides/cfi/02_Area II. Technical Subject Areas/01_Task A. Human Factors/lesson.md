# Task A. Human Factors

**Area II — Technical Subject Areas**

## Objective

To determine the applicant understands personal health, flight physiology, aeromedical and human factors, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate appropriate skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Human factors affect perception, judgment, performance, and decision-making. A pilot must recognize physiological limitations, comply with alcohol and drug regulations, complete an honest fitness-for-flight assessment, and use risk-management tools before and during flight.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested Schedule**

- Ground lesson: approximately 75 minutes
- Scenario and self-assessment application: approximately 20 minutes

**Equipment**

- Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge
- Risk Management Handbook
- 14 CFR Part 91
- CFI Airplane ACS
- Flight Guide visual aids

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner Actions**

- Associate symptoms, causes, effects, and corrective actions for aeromedical conditions.
- Complete an IMSAFE and personal-minimums self-assessment for a flight scenario.
- Apply PAVE, 3P, and TEAM to identify, assess, and mitigate risk.
- Recognize human-factor risks that degrade situational awareness and decision-making.

**Instructor Actions**

- Use clear aeromedical examples and require learner self-assessment.
- Connect physiological condition, hazardous attitudes, workload, and decision-making.
- Teach the legal limitations for alcohol and drugs from 14 CFR §91.17.
- Emphasize that safe operation requires action before performance is impaired.

## Completion Standards

The applicant can explain the symptoms, causes, effects, and corrective actions for at least three ACS aeromedical conditions; conduct a fitness-for-flight and personal-minimums self-assessment; explain alcohol and drug restrictions; and teach ADM, CRM/SRM, and risk management in the context of a flight scenario.

**Human factors are the physical, physiological, and psychological conditions that affect how well a pilot perceives, thinks, decides, and performs.**

The airplane can be completely airworthy and the weather can be acceptable, but if the pilot is not fit for the flight, the operation can still be unsafe.

The goal is to recognize these problems **before they degrade performance**, understand what they feel like in flight, and know the correct action.

### Fitness for Flight

Before every flight, the pilot needs to make an honest assessment of physical and mental condition.

A useful self-check is **IMSAFE**:

- **Illness**
- **Medication**
- **Stress**
- **Alcohol**
- **Fatigue**
- **Emotion**

This is not just a good operating practice.

Under **14 CFR §61.53**, a person who holds a medical certificate may not act as pilot in command or as a required pilot flight crewmember when that person knows or has reason to know of a medical condition, or is taking medication or receiving treatment, that would make the person unable to meet the requirements for the medical certificate necessary for the operation.

So the practical rule is:

**Having a medical certificate does not mean you are automatically fit to fly today.**

The pilot has to continually self-evaluate.

### Hypoxia

**Hypoxia is inadequate oxygen available to body tissues.**

The brain is especially sensitive to oxygen deprivation, so judgment and coordination may begin deteriorating before the pilot realizes anything is wrong.

Common symptoms include:

- Euphoria.
- Headache.
- Impaired judgment.
- Slower reaction time.
- Visual impairment.
- Drowsiness.
- Lightheadedness.
- Tingling or numbness.
- Poor coordination.

One of the most dangerous symptoms is **euphoria**, because the pilot may feel good while performance is getting worse.

#### Types of Hypoxia

**Hypoxic hypoxia** is caused by insufficient oxygen pressure reaching the lungs.

In aviation, the common cause is altitude.

The percentage of oxygen in the atmosphere remains approximately the same, but atmospheric pressure decreases with altitude, so the partial pressure of oxygen decreases.

**Hypemic hypoxia** occurs when the blood cannot carry enough oxygen.

Examples include:

- Blood loss.
- Anemia.
- Carbon monoxide poisoning.

**Stagnant hypoxia** occurs when oxygenated blood is not circulating adequately.

Examples include:

- Shock.
- Reduced cardiac output.
- Excessive positive G loading.

**Histotoxic hypoxia** occurs when oxygen reaches the tissues but the cells cannot use it effectively.

Alcohol and certain drugs can contribute to this condition.

#### Corrective Action

If hypoxia is suspected:

- **Use supplemental oxygen immediately**, if available.
- **Descend to a lower altitude.**
- Verify the oxygen system is operating properly.

Do not wait for severe symptoms.

#### 14 CFR §91.211 — Supplemental Oxygen

For an unpressurized or cabin-pressure-altitude operation:

- **Above 12,500 feet MSL through 14,000 feet MSL:** required minimum flightcrew must use oxygen for the portion of the flight at those altitudes lasting **more than 30 minutes**.
- **Above 14,000 feet MSL:** required minimum flightcrew must use oxygen during the **entire time** at those altitudes.
- **Above 15,000 feet MSL:** each occupant must be **provided** supplemental oxygen.

Those are regulatory minimums, not necessarily good personal minimums.

### Hyperventilation

**Hyperventilation is excessive rate and depth of breathing that removes too much carbon dioxide from the body.**

It is commonly associated with:

- Stress.
- Anxiety.
- Fear.
- Pain.
- High workload.

Symptoms can look very similar to hypoxia:

- Dizziness.
- Tingling.
- Visual disturbance.
- Hot or cold sensations.
- Muscle spasms.
- Lightheadedness.
- Possible unconsciousness.

That similarity matters.

If symptoms occur at altitude and there is any doubt whether the cause is hypoxia or hyperventilation:

- Check the oxygen system.
- Use oxygen if appropriate.
- Begin a descent if hypoxia is possible.
- Consciously slow and normalize breathing.

The key distinction is:

**Hypoxia = not enough usable oxygen.**  
**Hyperventilation = too much carbon dioxide being expelled.**

### Middle Ear and Sinus Problems

The middle ear and sinuses contain trapped air that has to equalize with changing atmospheric pressure.

During climbs and descents, congestion can prevent that pressure from equalizing.

#### Middle Ear

The **Eustachian tube** connects the middle ear to the back of the throat.

Normally, pressure can equalize by:

- Swallowing.
- Yawning.
- Chewing.

If the tube is blocked, pressure differences can cause:

- Ear pain.
- Reduced hearing.
- In severe cases, eardrum damage.

Problems are often worse during descent because outside pressure is increasing.

#### Sinuses

The sinuses also equalize through small openings into the nasal passages.

Congestion can block those openings and cause:

- Facial or sinus pain.
- Upper-tooth pain.
- Bloody nasal discharge.

The best prevention is simple:

**Do not fly with significant congestion, an upper-respiratory infection, or a sinus condition that prevents pressure equalization.**

If an illness or medication may affect fitness for flight, **14 CFR §61.53** applies.

### Spatial Disorientation

**Spatial disorientation is the inability to correctly determine the airplane's position, attitude, or movement in space.**

The body uses three primary systems for orientation:

- **Visual system** — the eyes.
- **Vestibular system** — the inner ear.
- **Somatosensory system** — pressure and movement sensations from the skin, muscles, and joints.

In normal visual conditions, the eyes provide the strongest orientation information.

When outside visual references disappear, the other systems can provide convincing but false sensations.

#### Semicircular Canals

The semicircular canals sense **angular acceleration**.

During a prolonged constant-rate turn, the fluid inside eventually catches up with the motion of the canals.

The sensation of turning can disappear.

When the pilot rolls back to level flight, the fluid moves again and can create the false sensation of turning in the opposite direction.

That is why a pilot in IMC must:

**Trust the flight instruments, not bodily sensation.**

#### Otolith Organs

The otolith organs sense **linear acceleration and gravity**.

Rapid acceleration can create the illusion that the nose is pitching up.

Deceleration can create the illusion that the nose is pitching down.

This is one reason a pilot can make a dangerous pitch correction when visual references are poor.

### Optical Illusions

Visual information can also be misleading.

Common approach illusions include:

- **Narrow runway** — can make the airplane seem higher than it actually is, encouraging a low approach.
- **Wide runway** — can make the airplane seem lower than it actually is, encouraging a high approach.
- **Upsloping runway** — can create the impression of being high.
- **Downsloping runway** — can create the impression of being low.
- **Black-hole or featureless terrain** — can create an unsafe low approach.
- Rain, haze, fog, and lighting can distort distance and height perception.

The defense is to anticipate the illusion and use reliable references:

- Published airport information.
- Altimeter.
- Normal sight picture.
- VASI/PAPI or other glidepath guidance.
- Instruments when appropriate.

### Motion Sickness

**Motion sickness occurs when the brain receives conflicting sensory information about motion and orientation.**

Symptoms include:

- General discomfort.
- Nausea.
- Dizziness.
- Paleness.
- Sweating.
- Vomiting.

It is common in early training and often improves with experience.

Helpful actions include:

- Fresh air.
- Looking outside at a stable reference.
- Avoiding unnecessary head movement.
- Smooth aircraft control.
- Shorter flights when needed.

If the student becomes significantly impaired:

**Aircraft control comes first.**

### Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

**Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless.**

It binds strongly to hemoglobin and prevents the blood from carrying oxygen effectively, producing **hypemic hypoxia**.

In a piston airplane, a leaking exhaust system can allow CO into the cabin through:

- Cabin heat.
- Defrost systems.
- Other openings.

Symptoms include:

- Headache.
- Dizziness.
- Blurred vision.
- Drowsiness.
- Weakness.
- Impaired judgment.

If CO is suspected:

- Turn off cabin heat.
- Open fresh-air vents and windows as appropriate.
- Use supplemental oxygen if available.
- Land as soon as practical.

Do not rely on smelling exhaust to detect CO.

### Stress

**Stress is the body's response to physical and psychological demands.**

Some stress is normal.

Too much stress reduces performance.

Stress may be:

- **Physical** — noise, vibration, temperature.
- **Physiological** — fatigue, illness, hunger.
- **Psychological** — work, family, financial, or operational pressure.

**Acute stress** is short-term and associated with an immediate demand.

**Chronic stress** persists over time and can significantly degrade:

- Judgment.
- Attention.
- Memory.
- Decision-making.
- Communication.

If stress has reached the point where safe performance is doubtful:

**Do not fly.**

### Fatigue

**Fatigue degrades attention, coordination, communication, judgment, and decision-making.**

Causes include:

- Inadequate sleep.
- Long duty periods.
- Physical exertion.
- Stress.
- Prolonged mental workload.

Fatigue can make a pilot:

- Miss traffic.
- Skip checklist items.
- React slowly.
- Fixate.
- Make poor decisions.
- Fly less smoothly.

There is no cockpit technique that replaces adequate sleep.

If significantly fatigued:

**Stay on the ground.**

### Dehydration and Nutrition

Dehydration can cause:

- Fatigue.
- Headache.
- Dizziness.
- Weakness.
- Nausea.
- Cramps.
- Reduced concentration.

Risk increases with:

- Hot cockpits.
- Dry air at altitude.
- Long flights.
- Caffeine.
- Alcohol.
- Poor fluid intake.

The best prevention is to hydrate **before** becoming thirsty and maintain adequate nutrition.

A hungry, dehydrated pilot may technically be legal but still not be safe.

That belongs in the IMSAFE assessment.

### Hypothermia

**Hypothermia is excessive loss of body heat.**

Cold exposure can degrade:

- Coordination.
- Dexterity.
- Judgment.
- Reaction time.

The defense is preparation:

- Appropriate clothing.
- Cabin-heat planning.
- Survival equipment appropriate to the route and environment.

### Alcohol

Alcohol degrades:

- Judgment.
- Coordination.
- Reaction time.
- Vision.
- Memory.
- Reasoning.
- Attention.

Altitude can compound those effects.

A hangover can also impair pilot performance after the blood alcohol level has fallen.

#### 14 CFR §91.17 — Alcohol or Drugs

A person may not act or attempt to act as a crewmember:

- **Within 8 hours after consuming alcohol.**
- While **under the influence of alcohol**.
- While using any drug that affects the person's faculties in a way contrary to safety.
- With an alcohol concentration of **0.04 or greater** in blood or breath.

The regulation is the legal minimum.

**Eight hours bottle-to-throttle does not automatically mean fit to fly.**

IMSAFE still applies.

### Drugs and Medications

Prescription and over-the-counter medications can affect:

- Alertness.
- Judgment.
- Vision.
- Coordination.
- Memory.
- Reaction time.

The underlying illness may also make the pilot unsafe even if the medication itself does not.

Under **14 CFR §61.53**, a pilot may not act as PIC or required flightcrew when medication, treatment, or a medical condition makes the pilot unable to meet the applicable medical requirements.

Under **14 CFR §91.17**, a crewmember may not use a drug that affects faculties contrary to safety.

So ask two separate questions:

- **Is the medication safe for flight?**
- **Is the condition I am treating safe for flight?**

When uncertain, consult an AME or current FAA aeromedical guidance before flying.

### Dissolved Nitrogen After Scuba Diving

During scuba diving, increased pressure causes additional nitrogen to dissolve in body tissues.

If atmospheric pressure is reduced too quickly afterward, nitrogen can form bubbles and produce **decompression sickness**.

Symptoms can include:

- Joint pain.
- Weakness.
- Neurological symptoms.
- Serious incapacitation.

FAA guidance recommends waiting:

- At least **12 hours** after a dive that did not require a controlled ascent before flight up to 8,000 feet.
- At least **24 hours** after a dive requiring a controlled ascent.
- At least **24 hours after any scuba dive** before flight above 8,000 feet.

These are aeromedical recommendations rather than a Part 91 scuba-diving regulation.

### Aeronautical Decision-Making

Human factors are not useful unless they change the decision.

**Aeronautical Decision-Making, or ADM, is the process of consistently choosing the best course of action for a given set of circumstances.**

Before flight, the pilot identifies hazards.

During flight, the pilot continually reevaluates them.

For a single pilot, **Single-Pilot Resource Management — SRM** means using all available resources:

- Aircraft systems.
- Automation.
- ATC.
- Passengers.
- Checklists.
- Weather information.
- Outside assistance.

For a crew, **Crew Resource Management — CRM** applies the same concept across multiple crewmembers.

### Hazardous Attitudes

Five hazardous attitudes can distort decisions:

- **Anti-authority** — “Don't tell me.”
- **Impulsivity** — “Do something now.”
- **Invulnerability** — “It won't happen to me.”
- **Macho** — “I can do it.”
- **Resignation** — “What's the use?”

The important point is not simply memorizing the names.

The pilot needs to recognize when one of these attitudes is influencing a real decision and deliberately replace it with a safer response.

### Confirmation and Expectation Bias

**Confirmation bias** is the tendency to favor information that supports the conclusion we already want.

**Expectation bias** occurs when we see or hear what we expect rather than what is actually there.

Examples:

- Expecting a runway to be clear and overlooking traffic.
- Hearing the clearance we anticipated instead of the clearance ATC actually issued.
- Interpreting worsening weather as “still good enough” because we want to complete the trip.

The defense is deliberate verification:

- Recheck the data.
- Use checklists.
- Read back clearances.
- Seek contradictory information.
- Be willing to change the plan.

### Risk Management and Personal Minimums

The ACS requires the pilot to perform a self-assessment for an actual or evaluator-provided scenario.

Use **IMSAFE** to evaluate the pilot.

Then consider the broader flight:

- Pilot.
- Aircraft.
- Environment.
- External pressures.

Personal minimums should be more conservative than regulatory limits when the pilot's:

- Experience.
- Proficiency.
- Health.
- Stress.
- Fatigue.
- Aircraft familiarity.

justify a larger safety margin.

The final question is not:

**“Can I legally fly?”**

It is:

**“Am I fit, proficient, and prepared enough to safely fly this flight today?”**

### Instructor Emphasis

The learner should leave the lesson understanding:

1. **A legal pilot is not automatically a fit pilot.**
2. **14 CFR §61.53 requires continual self-grounding when a medical condition, medication, or treatment makes the pilot medically unqualified for the operation.**
3. **Hypoxia can impair judgment before the pilot recognizes the problem.**
4. **14 CFR §91.211 establishes the regulatory oxygen requirements, but personal oxygen use may need to begin earlier.**
5. **Spatial disorientation is defeated by reliable visual references or flight instruments—not bodily sensation.**
6. **Carbon monoxide can produce hypemic hypoxia without warning.**
7. **Stress, fatigue, dehydration, and poor nutrition directly degrade pilot performance.**
8. **14 CFR §91.17 establishes the alcohol/drug prohibitions, but legality is only the minimum standard.**
9. **IMSAFE is a practical preflight fitness check.**
10. **Good ADM means recognizing when human factors require changing or cancelling the flight.**

### Close

Human factors are about recognizing that the pilot is part of the aircraft system.

Before flight:

**Assess yourself honestly.**

During flight:

**Recognize changes early.**

When something begins degrading your ability to fly safely:

**Act before performance deteriorates further.**

The most important question is:

**Am I fit to safely conduct this flight right now?**


## AI.II.A.K1 — Aeromedical and Physiological Issues

### AI.II.A.K1a — Hypoxia

**Hypoxia** is reduced oxygen available to body tissue. In flight, oxygen deprivation of the brain can create life-threatening errors. Symptoms include **euphoria, headache, slowed response, impaired judgment, visual impairment, drowsiness, lightheadedness, tingling, and numbness**. Corrective action is to **descend and/or use supplemental oxygen**; all pilots are susceptible, regardless of endurance or acclimatization.

### AI.II.A.K1b — Hyperventilation

**Hyperventilation** is excessive rate and depth of breathing that causes abnormal loss of carbon dioxide. It can produce **dizziness, tingling, visual impairment, hot and cold sensations, muscle spasms, and unconsciousness**. Its symptoms can resemble hypoxia. **Check oxygen equipment** when oxygen is in use; then restore normal breathing. Talking aloud or breathing into a paper bag can help slow breathing and restore carbon-dioxide balance.

### AI.II.A.K1c — Middle Ear and Sinus Problems

Pressure changes during climbs and descents can cause **ear or sinus pain, temporary hearing reduction, and injury** when trapped gas cannot equalize. Chewing, yawning, or swallowing opens the **Eustachian tube**. **Do not fly** with an upper-respiratory infection or nasal allergic condition that prevents equalization. Medication may have adverse effects, so **consult an AME** before use.

### AI.II.A.K1d — Spatial Disorientation

**Spatial disorientation** is loss of orientation regarding aircraft position, attitude, or movement in space. The vestibular, somatosensory, and visual systems can provide conflicting information; without reliable outside visual references, false sensations can rapidly disorient the pilot. Prevent it through **training, awareness, adequate rest and nutrition, minimal head movement, instrument proficiency, and reliance on flight instruments rather than bodily sensation**.

### AI.II.A.K1e — Motion Sickness

**Motion sickness** results when the brain receives conflicting messages about body state. Symptoms include **discomfort, nausea, dizziness, paleness, sweating, and vomiting**. It commonly improves after early lessons. Inform the instructor, use shorter flights initially, avoid turbulent conditions until more comfortable, use fresh air, look outside, and avoid unnecessary head movement.

### AI.II.A.K1f — Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

**Carbon monoxide** is colorless and odorless and prevents hemoglobin from carrying oxygen. It can enter the cabin through a damaged exhaust system or heater/defrost vents. Symptoms include **headache, blurred vision, dizziness, drowsiness, and loss of muscle power**. If exhaust odor or symptoms occur, **turn off the heater, open fresh-air vents and windows, use oxygen if available, and take immediate corrective action**.

### AI.II.A.K1g — Stress

**Stress** is the body's response to physical and psychological demands. **Acute stress** is short-term; **chronic stress** can exceed a pilot's ability to cope and sharply reduce performance. When stress reaches that level, the pilot is **not safe to exercise airman privileges** and should consult a physician.

### AI.II.A.K1h — Fatigue

**Fatigue** degrades **attention, concentration, coordination, communication, and decision-making**. Acute fatigue may result from sleep loss, exertion, or stress; chronic fatigue often requires a prolonged solution and medical attention. If suffering from fatigue, **stay on the ground**. Adequate rest, sleep, and diet are primary defenses.

### AI.II.A.K1i — Dehydration and Nutrition

**Dehydration** causes fatigue and can progress to **dizziness, weakness, nausea, tingling, cramps, and thirst**. Heat, wind, humidity, and diuretic beverages increase risk. **Maintain hydration before thirst is felt**, limit caffeine and alcohol, eat regular balanced meals, and bring water and a snack for the flight.

### AI.II.A.K1j — Hypothermia

**Hypothermia** is a loss of body heat that degrades performance. Prevent exposure through **appropriate clothing and preparation**; recognize that cold conditions can also reduce circulation and contribute to physiological impairment.

### AI.II.A.K1k — Optical Illusions

**Runway width, slope, featureless terrain, rain, haze, fog, and ground lighting** can create a false perception of height, distance, or approach path. Anticipate illusions at **unfamiliar airports, at night, and in adverse weather**. Use airport information, frequent altimeter checks, an aerial visual inspection when practical, and visual or electronic glidepath guidance when available.

### AI.II.A.K1l — Dissolved Nitrogen After Scuba Diving

Scuba diving increases dissolved nitrogen in body tissues. Reducing pressure during flight can cause **decompression sickness**. Before flight altitudes up to 8,000 feet, wait **at least 12 hours** after a nondecompression dive and **at least 24 hours** after a decompression-stop dive. Before flight altitudes above 8,000 feet, wait **at least 24 hours after any scuba dive**.

## AI.II.A.K2 — Regulations Regarding Use of Alcohol and Drugs

Under **14 CFR §91.17**, a person may not act or attempt to act as a crewmember **within 8 hours after consuming alcohol**, while under the influence of alcohol, while using a drug that affects faculties contrary to safety, or with an alcohol concentration of **0.04 or greater** in blood or breath.

Except in an emergency, a pilot also may not allow a person who appears intoxicated or shows physical or behavioral indications of drug influence to be carried in the aircraft, except a medical patient under proper care. The regulation also requires a crewmember to furnish or authorize release of certain timely alcohol or drug test results when requested by the FAA under the conditions stated in the rule.

## AI.II.A.K3 — Effects of Alcohol, Drugs, and Over-the-Counter Medications

Alcohol is a central nervous system depressant. Even small amounts can impair judgment, sense of responsibility, coordination, visual field, memory, reasoning, attention, reflexes, and vision or hearing. Altitude compounds alcohol effects, and a hangover can still impair motor and mental performance. The legal limits are a minimum boundary, not a measure of fitness to fly.

Prescription, over-the-counter, herbal, dietary, and other products can cause side effects that impair judgment, memory, alertness, coordination, vision, calculation, or response. The condition being treated may also be unsafe for flight. At altitude, fatigue, stress, dehydration, poor nutrition, and multiple medications can increase susceptibility to adverse effects. Do not fly while using a medication that affects safety; when there is doubt, consult an AME before flying.

Common over-the-counter antihistamines and decongestants can cause drowsiness and cognitive deficits. Do not mix drugs with alcohol because the effects can be unpredictable. The PHAK advises waiting to determine whether a new medication causes adverse effects before flying.

## AI.II.A.K4 — Aeronautical Decision-Making, CRM, and SRM

**ADM** is a structured, systematic approach for analyzing flight changes and their effect on safe outcome. It develops risk assessment, resource use, stress management, recognition of hazardous attitudes, and evaluation of decision-making effectiveness.

**Risk management** identifies hazards, assesses risk, analyzes controls, makes control decisions, uses controls, and monitors results. **CRM** applies resource-management principles to crew operations. **SRM** applies those principles to a single pilot using all available onboard and outside resources, including automation and ATC, to manage workload and maintain situational awareness.

**Hazardous attitudes** can distort risk decisions. The five hazardous attitudes are **anti-authority, impulsivity, invulnerability, macho, and resignation**; their antidotes are to **follow rules, think first, recognize personal vulnerability, avoid chance-taking, and take responsibility for action**.

## Instructor Application — Human Factors Scenario

Assign a VFR cross-country scenario with a fatigued pilot, recent use of an over-the-counter medication, marginal visibility, a scheduled arrival pressure, and unfamiliar avionics. Have the learner complete IMSAFE and PAVE, identify likelihood and severity, define personal minimums, select a TEAM response, and explain what condition would require a new decision. Assess the learner's use of resources, task prioritization, situational awareness, and resistance to hazardous attitudes or expectation bias.

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. Describe hypoxia: symptoms, cause, effects, and corrective action.

[[answer:
Hypoxia is reduced oxygen available to tissue. Symptoms can include euphoria, headache, impaired judgment, visual impairment, drowsiness, dizziness, and tingling. At altitude, descend and/or use supplemental oxygen.
]]

### 2. How do hypoxia and hyperventilation differ?

[[answer:
Hypoxia is inadequate oxygen available to tissue. Hyperventilation is excessive breathing that causes abnormal carbon-dioxide loss. They can have similar symptoms; if oxygen is in use, first check the oxygen system, then restore normal breathing for hyperventilation.
]]

### 3. What does 14 CFR §91.17 prohibit?

[[answer:
Acting as a crewmember within 8 hours after alcohol, while under alcohol influence, while using a drug that affects faculties contrary to safety, or with an alcohol concentration of 0.04 or greater.
]]

### 4. What is the primary defense against spatial disorientation?

[[answer:
Training and awareness, followed by reliance on flight instruments rather than false bodily sensations when visual references are unreliable.
]]

### 5. How can optical illusions lead to landing error?

[[answer:
Runway, terrain, weather, and lighting conditions can create false impressions of height, distance, or approach path. Anticipate them and use reliable aircraft and airport references, including altimeter and glidepath guidance.
]]

### 6. What are the recommended wait times after scuba diving?

[[answer:
At least 12 hours after a nondecompression dive before flight up to 8,000 feet; 24 hours after a decompression-stop dive; and 24 hours after any dive before flight above 8,000 feet.
]]

### 7. What do ADM and SRM add to a pilot's decision-making?

[[answer:
ADM provides a structured approach to analyze changes, risk, and decisions. SRM applies ADM, risk management, task management, automation management, and situational awareness to all resources available to a single pilot.
]]

### 8. Complete a fitness-for-flight assessment for the scenario using IMSAFE, PAVE, personal minimums, and a TEAM response.

[[answer:
Identify the pilot condition and external factors, assess their effect on safe flight, compare conditions with personal minimums, then transfer, eliminate, accept, or mitigate the remaining risk. Reassess whenever conditions change.
]]
