# Task A. Human Factors

**Area II — Technical Subject Areas**

## Objective

To determine the applicant understands personal health, flight physiology, aeromedical and human factors, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate appropriate skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Human factors affect perception, judgment, performance, and decision-making. A pilot must recognize physiological limitations, comply with alcohol and drug regulations, complete an honest fitness-for-flight assessment, and use risk-management tools before and during flight.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested Schedule**

- Ground lesson: approximately 75 minutes
- Scenario and self-assessment application: approximately 20 minutes

**Equipment**

- Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge
- Risk Management Handbook
- 14 CFR Part 91
- CFI Airplane ACS
- Flight Guide visual aids

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner Actions**

- Associate symptoms, causes, effects, and corrective actions for aeromedical conditions.
- Complete an IMSAFE and personal-minimums self-assessment for a flight scenario.
- Apply PAVE, 3P, and TEAM to identify, assess, and mitigate risk.
- Recognize human-factor risks that degrade situational awareness and decision-making.

**Instructor Actions**

- Use clear aeromedical examples and require learner self-assessment.
- Connect physiological condition, hazardous attitudes, workload, and decision-making.
- Teach the legal limitations for alcohol and drugs from 14 CFR §91.17.
- Emphasize that safe operation requires action before performance is impaired.

## Completion Standards

The applicant can explain the symptoms, causes, effects, and corrective actions for at least three ACS aeromedical conditions; conduct a fitness-for-flight and personal-minimums self-assessment; explain alcohol and drug restrictions; and teach ADM, CRM/SRM, and risk management in the context of a flight scenario.

# Teach

## AI.II.A.K1 — Aeromedical and Physiological Issues

### AI.II.A.K1a — Hypoxia

**Hypoxia** is reduced oxygen available to body tissue. In flight, oxygen deprivation of the brain can create life-threatening errors. Symptoms include **euphoria, headache, slowed response, impaired judgment, visual impairment, drowsiness, lightheadedness, tingling, and numbness**. Corrective action is to **descend and/or use supplemental oxygen**; all pilots are susceptible, regardless of endurance or acclimatization.

### AI.II.A.K1b — Hyperventilation

**Hyperventilation** is excessive rate and depth of breathing that causes abnormal loss of carbon dioxide. It can produce **dizziness, tingling, visual impairment, hot and cold sensations, muscle spasms, and unconsciousness**. Its symptoms can resemble hypoxia. **Check oxygen equipment** when oxygen is in use; then restore normal breathing. Talking aloud or breathing into a paper bag can help slow breathing and restore carbon-dioxide balance.

### AI.II.A.K1c — Middle Ear and Sinus Problems

Pressure changes during climbs and descents can cause **ear or sinus pain, temporary hearing reduction, and injury** when trapped gas cannot equalize. Chewing, yawning, or swallowing opens the **Eustachian tube**. **Do not fly** with an upper-respiratory infection or nasal allergic condition that prevents equalization. Medication may have adverse effects, so **consult an AME** before use.

### AI.II.A.K1d — Spatial Disorientation

**Spatial disorientation** is loss of orientation regarding aircraft position, attitude, or movement in space. The vestibular, somatosensory, and visual systems can provide conflicting information; without reliable outside visual references, false sensations can rapidly disorient the pilot. Prevent it through **training, awareness, adequate rest and nutrition, minimal head movement, instrument proficiency, and reliance on flight instruments rather than bodily sensation**.

### AI.II.A.K1e — Motion Sickness

**Motion sickness** results when the brain receives conflicting messages about body state. Symptoms include **discomfort, nausea, dizziness, paleness, sweating, and vomiting**. It commonly improves after early lessons. Inform the instructor, use shorter flights initially, avoid turbulent conditions until more comfortable, use fresh air, look outside, and avoid unnecessary head movement.

### AI.II.A.K1f — Carbon Monoxide Poisoning

**Carbon monoxide** is colorless and odorless and prevents hemoglobin from carrying oxygen. It can enter the cabin through a damaged exhaust system or heater/defrost vents. Symptoms include **headache, blurred vision, dizziness, drowsiness, and loss of muscle power**. If exhaust odor or symptoms occur, **turn off the heater, open fresh-air vents and windows, use oxygen if available, and take immediate corrective action**.

### AI.II.A.K1g — Stress

**Stress** is the body's response to physical and psychological demands. **Acute stress** is short-term; **chronic stress** can exceed a pilot's ability to cope and sharply reduce performance. When stress reaches that level, the pilot is **not safe to exercise airman privileges** and should consult a physician.

### AI.II.A.K1h — Fatigue

**Fatigue** degrades **attention, concentration, coordination, communication, and decision-making**. Acute fatigue may result from sleep loss, exertion, or stress; chronic fatigue often requires a prolonged solution and medical attention. If suffering from fatigue, **stay on the ground**. Adequate rest, sleep, and diet are primary defenses.

### AI.II.A.K1i — Dehydration and Nutrition

**Dehydration** causes fatigue and can progress to **dizziness, weakness, nausea, tingling, cramps, and thirst**. Heat, wind, humidity, and diuretic beverages increase risk. **Maintain hydration before thirst is felt**, limit caffeine and alcohol, eat regular balanced meals, and bring water and a snack for the flight.

### AI.II.A.K1j — Hypothermia

**Hypothermia** is a loss of body heat that degrades performance. Prevent exposure through **appropriate clothing and preparation**; recognize that cold conditions can also reduce circulation and contribute to physiological impairment.

### AI.II.A.K1k — Optical Illusions

**Runway width, slope, featureless terrain, rain, haze, fog, and ground lighting** can create a false perception of height, distance, or approach path. Anticipate illusions at **unfamiliar airports, at night, and in adverse weather**. Use airport information, frequent altimeter checks, an aerial visual inspection when practical, and visual or electronic glidepath guidance when available.

### AI.II.A.K1l — Dissolved Nitrogen After Scuba Diving

Scuba diving increases dissolved nitrogen in body tissues. Reducing pressure during flight can cause **decompression sickness**. Before flight altitudes up to 8,000 feet, wait **at least 12 hours** after a nondecompression dive and **at least 24 hours** after a decompression-stop dive. Before flight altitudes above 8,000 feet, wait **at least 24 hours after any scuba dive**.

## AI.II.A.K2 — Regulations Regarding Use of Alcohol and Drugs

Under **14 CFR §91.17**, a person may not act or attempt to act as a crewmember **within 8 hours after consuming alcohol**, while under the influence of alcohol, while using a drug that affects faculties contrary to safety, or with an alcohol concentration of **0.04 or greater** in blood or breath.

Except in an emergency, a pilot also may not allow a person who appears intoxicated or shows physical or behavioral indications of drug influence to be carried in the aircraft, except a medical patient under proper care. The regulation also requires a crewmember to furnish or authorize release of certain timely alcohol or drug test results when requested by the FAA under the conditions stated in the rule.

## AI.II.A.K3 — Effects of Alcohol, Drugs, and Over-the-Counter Medications

Alcohol is a central nervous system depressant. Even small amounts can impair judgment, sense of responsibility, coordination, visual field, memory, reasoning, attention, reflexes, and vision or hearing. Altitude compounds alcohol effects, and a hangover can still impair motor and mental performance. The legal limits are a minimum boundary, not a measure of fitness to fly.

Prescription, over-the-counter, herbal, dietary, and other products can cause side effects that impair judgment, memory, alertness, coordination, vision, calculation, or response. The condition being treated may also be unsafe for flight. At altitude, fatigue, stress, dehydration, poor nutrition, and multiple medications can increase susceptibility to adverse effects. Do not fly while using a medication that affects safety; when there is doubt, consult an AME before flying.

Common over-the-counter antihistamines and decongestants can cause drowsiness and cognitive deficits. Do not mix drugs with alcohol because the effects can be unpredictable. The PHAK advises waiting to determine whether a new medication causes adverse effects before flying.

## AI.II.A.K4 — Aeronautical Decision-Making, CRM, and SRM

**ADM** is a structured, systematic approach for analyzing flight changes and their effect on safe outcome. It develops risk assessment, resource use, stress management, recognition of hazardous attitudes, and evaluation of decision-making effectiveness.

**Risk management** identifies hazards, assesses risk, analyzes controls, makes control decisions, uses controls, and monitors results. **CRM** applies resource-management principles to crew operations. **SRM** applies those principles to a single pilot using all available onboard and outside resources, including automation and ATC, to manage workload and maintain situational awareness.

**Hazardous attitudes** can distort risk decisions. The five hazardous attitudes are **anti-authority, impulsivity, invulnerability, macho, and resignation**; their antidotes are to **follow rules, think first, recognize personal vulnerability, avoid chance-taking, and take responsibility for action**.

## Instructor Application — Human Factors Scenario

Assign a VFR cross-country scenario with a fatigued pilot, recent use of an over-the-counter medication, marginal visibility, a scheduled arrival pressure, and unfamiliar avionics. Have the learner complete IMSAFE and PAVE, identify likelihood and severity, define personal minimums, select a TEAM response, and explain what condition would require a new decision. Assess the learner's use of resources, task prioritization, situational awareness, and resistance to hazardous attitudes or expectation bias.

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. Describe hypoxia: symptoms, cause, effects, and corrective action.

[[answer:
Hypoxia is reduced oxygen available to tissue. Symptoms can include euphoria, headache, impaired judgment, visual impairment, drowsiness, dizziness, and tingling. At altitude, descend and/or use supplemental oxygen.
]]

### 2. How do hypoxia and hyperventilation differ?

[[answer:
Hypoxia is inadequate oxygen available to tissue. Hyperventilation is excessive breathing that causes abnormal carbon-dioxide loss. They can have similar symptoms; if oxygen is in use, first check the oxygen system, then restore normal breathing for hyperventilation.
]]

### 3. What does 14 CFR §91.17 prohibit?

[[answer:
Acting as a crewmember within 8 hours after alcohol, while under alcohol influence, while using a drug that affects faculties contrary to safety, or with an alcohol concentration of 0.04 or greater.
]]

### 4. What is the primary defense against spatial disorientation?

[[answer:
Training and awareness, followed by reliance on flight instruments rather than false bodily sensations when visual references are unreliable.
]]

### 5. How can optical illusions lead to landing error?

[[answer:
Runway, terrain, weather, and lighting conditions can create false impressions of height, distance, or approach path. Anticipate them and use reliable aircraft and airport references, including altimeter and glidepath guidance.
]]

### 6. What are the recommended wait times after scuba diving?

[[answer:
At least 12 hours after a nondecompression dive before flight up to 8,000 feet; 24 hours after a decompression-stop dive; and 24 hours after any dive before flight above 8,000 feet.
]]

### 7. What do ADM and SRM add to a pilot's decision-making?

[[answer:
ADM provides a structured approach to analyze changes, risk, and decisions. SRM applies ADM, risk management, task management, automation management, and situational awareness to all resources available to a single pilot.
]]

### 8. Complete a fitness-for-flight assessment for the scenario using IMSAFE, PAVE, personal minimums, and a TEAM response.

[[answer:
Identify the pilot condition and external factors, assess their effect on safe flight, compare conditions with personal minimums, then transfer, eliminate, accept, or mitigate the remaining risk. Reassess whenever conditions change.
]]
