# Task E. Aircraft Flight Controls and Operation of Systems

**Area II — Technical Subject Areas**

## Objective

To determine the applicant understands flight controls and systems on the airplane provided for the flight test, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate appropriate skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

A pilot operates the airplane through its flight controls and depends on its systems for thrust, control, navigation, information, safety, and environmental support. A CFI must teach the system installed in the aircraft, recognize abnormal indications, and use the AFM/POH procedures before a malfunction becomes an emergency.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested Schedule**

- Ground lesson: approximately 75–90 minutes
- Aircraft walk-around, cockpit systems review, and control demonstration: approximately 20 minutes

**Equipment**

- Aircraft-specific AFM/POH
- Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge
- CFI Airplane ACS
- Model airplane or whiteboard
- Flight Guide system and instrument visuals

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner Actions**

- Identify and operate at least three installed systems.
- Explain how primary and secondary controls affect aircraft movement.
- Describe each installed system's normal indications, limitations, and emergency procedures.
- Recognize the difference between a system symptom and a system cause.

**Instructor Actions**

- Use the aircraft AFM/POH as the controlling source for the airplane flown.
- Demonstrate control movement, system checks, and normal operating procedures.
- Require cross-checking of indications rather than reliance on one instrument.
- Teach automation and avionics on the ground before adding in-flight workload.

## Completion Standards

The applicant can explain all applicable `K1a`–`K1l` systems and `K2` abnormal/failure management for the test aircraft, identify associated risks, and simultaneously explain while appropriately operating at least three applicable systems.

**\*\*The main idea in aircraft flight controls and systems is that the pilot must understand what each control and system does, how it normally behaves, how to recognize when it is not behaving normally, and what procedure applies when something fails.\*\***

For the airplane actually being flown:

**\*\*The AFM/POH is the controlling source.\*\***

This lesson is not about memorizing generic system descriptions and then assuming every airplane works the same way.

It is about understanding the installed airplane well enough to:

\- Operate it correctly.

\- Recognize abnormal indications.

\- Manage failures.

\- Teach the system accurately.

**### Regulatory Foundation**

Several regulations connect directly to aircraft-system operation.

Under **\*\*14 CFR §91.9\*\***, no person may operate a civil aircraft without complying with the operating limitations specified in the approved AFM, markings, and placards.

That is why:

**\*\*The AFM/POH controls aircraft-specific procedures and limitations.\*\***

Under **\*\*14 CFR §91.7\*\***, the pilot in command is responsible for determining whether the aircraft is in condition for safe flight and must discontinue the flight when unairworthy mechanical, electrical, or structural conditions occur.

Under **\*\*14 CFR §91.205\*\***, certain instruments and equipment are required depending on the type of operation.

Under **\*\*14 CFR §91.213\*\***, inoperative instruments or equipment may be operated with only when the applicable requirements of that regulation are met.

So system knowledge is not just technical knowledge.

It directly supports the PIC's legal and operational responsibilities.

**### Primary Flight Controls**

The three primary flight controls are:

\- **\*\*Ailerons\*\***

\- **\*\*Elevator or stabilator\*\***

\- **\*\*Rudder\*\***

They control the airplane around its three axes.

\- **\*\*Ailerons → roll → longitudinal axis\*\***

\- **\*\*Elevator → pitch → lateral axis\*\***

\- **\*\*Rudder → yaw → vertical axis\*\***

The primary controls work by changing airflow and pressure distribution around the control surfaces.

**### Ailerons**

Ailerons are normally located near the outboard trailing edges of the wings.

They move in opposite directions.

For a right roll:

\- Right aileron moves up.

\- Left aileron moves down.

The up aileron:

\- Reduces camber.

\- Reduces lift on that wing.

The down aileron:

\- Increases camber.

\- Increases lift on that wing.

The lift difference produces roll.

**### Adverse Yaw**

The down-going aileron produces more lift.

More lift also means more induced drag.

That extra drag tends to yaw the nose **\*\*opposite the direction of the desired roll\*\***.

That is **\*\*adverse yaw\*\***.

So when rolling into or out of a turn:

**\*\*Aileron establishes bank. Rudder maintains coordination.\*\***

Adverse yaw is generally more noticeable with:

\- Larger aileron deflections.

\- Lower airspeed.

\- Higher AOA.

**### Aileron Design Features**

Manufacturers use different designs to reduce adverse yaw.

Examples include:

\- Differential ailerons.

\- Frise ailerons.

\- Coupled aileron-rudder systems.

\- Flaperons.

The important teaching point is not memorizing every design.

It is understanding:

**\*\*The airplane may be designed to reduce adverse yaw, but the pilot still has to maintain coordination.\*\***

**### Elevator**

The elevator controls pitch about the lateral axis.

Pulling aft on the control:

\- Deflects the elevator upward.

\- Changes the aerodynamic force on the tail.

\- Moves the tail downward.

\- Raises the nose.

Pushing forward does the opposite:

\- Tail moves upward.

\- Nose moves downward.

The amount of control force required changes with:

\- Airspeed.

\- CG.

\- Power.

\- Configuration.

\- Trim.

**### Stabilator**

Some airplanes use a **\*\*stabilator\*\*** instead of a separate fixed stabilizer and elevator.

A stabilator is an all-moving horizontal tail.

Because the entire surface moves, it can be very effective.

Many stabilators use an **\*\*antiservo tab\*\*** to:

\- Increase control force.

\- Reduce sensitivity.

\- Provide trim.

**### Rudder**

The rudder controls yaw about the vertical axis.

Pressing the left rudder pedal moves the rudder left.

The resulting aerodynamic force moves the tail right and yaws the nose left.

Rudder is used to:

\- Maintain coordination.

\- Counter adverse yaw.

\- Counter left-turning tendencies.

\- Maintain directional control.

\- Control yaw during crosswind operations.

\- Assist during engine-out operations in multiengine airplanes.

Rudder effectiveness generally increases with airflow over the surface.

At low airspeed, larger rudder deflections may be required.

**### Secondary Flight Controls**

Secondary controls improve performance or reduce pilot workload.

They can include:

\- Flaps.

\- Leading-edge devices.

\- Spoilers.

\- Trim systems.

The exact installed equipment depends on the airplane.

**### Flaps**

Flaps change the shape of the wing.

They generally increase:

\- Camber.

\- Lift.

\- Drag.

That allows:

\- Lower takeoff or landing speeds.

\- Steeper approaches.

\- Shorter landing distances.

\- Different climb or descent characteristics.

The tradeoff is drag.

Different flap types include:

\- Plain.

\- Split.

\- Slotted.

\- Fowler.

The important operational point is:

**\*\*Use the flap system exactly as described in the AFM/POH and respect VFE.\*\***

**### Leading-Edge Devices**

Leading-edge devices may include:

\- Fixed slots.

\- Slats.

\- Leading-edge flaps.

\- Leading-edge cuffs.

Their purpose is generally to:

\- Delay airflow separation.

\- Improve high-AOA performance.

\- Increase maximum lift.

Again, teach the installed system rather than generic assumptions.

**### Spoilers**

Spoilers reduce lift and increase drag.

They may be used to:

\- Increase descent rate.

\- Reduce airspeed.

\- Assist roll control.

\- Improve wheel braking after touchdown by reducing wing lift.

**### Trim**

**\*\*Trim relieves control pressure.\*\***

The correct sequence is:

1\. Establish the desired power.

2\. Establish the desired attitude.

3\. Establish the desired configuration.

4\. Hold the airplane where you want it.

5\. Trim away the control pressure.

The important rule is:

**\*\*Fly the airplane first. Trim second.\*\***

Trim should not be used as the primary method of changing pitch attitude.

**### Trim Tab Direction**

On a conventional elevator with a trim tab:

\- **\*\*Nose-up trim → trim tab moves down\*\***

\- **\*\*Nose-down trim → trim tab moves up\*\***

The tab creates an aerodynamic force that moves the elevator in the opposite direction.

That relieves the pilot's control pressure.

The exact mechanical implementation may differ by airplane.

**### Powerplant and Propeller**

The powerplant produces the mechanical energy used to create thrust.

For a typical reciprocating engine:

\- Fuel and air enter the cylinders.

\- The mixture is compressed.

\- Ignition occurs.

\- Combustion drives the piston.

\- The crankshaft turns the propeller.

The major engine-supporting systems include:

\- Fuel.

\- Ignition.

\- Induction.

\- Lubrication.

\- Cooling.

\- Exhaust.

Normal engine indications must be interpreted together.

Do not diagnose an engine problem from one indication alone if other indications can confirm or contradict it.

**### Fixed-Pitch Propeller**

A fixed-pitch propeller has one blade angle.

Its RPM changes with:

\- Power.

\- Airspeed.

\- Aircraft attitude.

A climb and a descent at the same throttle setting can produce different RPM.

**### Constant-Speed Propeller**

A constant-speed propeller changes blade angle to maintain a selected RPM.

The governor senses RPM and adjusts oil flow to the propeller system.

The pilot normally controls:

\- Manifold pressure with throttle.

\- RPM with propeller control.

The exact sequence and limitations are aircraft-specific.

**### Landing Gear**

Landing gear may be:

\- Fixed.

\- Retractable.

\- Tricycle.

\- Tailwheel.

Retractable gear reduces drag but introduces:

\- Gear-position indications.

\- Warning systems.

\- Hydraulic or electrical components.

\- Emergency extension procedures.

For retractable gear:

**\*\*Verify gear position using the installed indication—not assumption or sound alone.\*\***

**### Fuel System**

The fuel system must provide uninterrupted fuel flow to the engine.

Depending on the airplane, it may include:

\- Fuel tanks.

\- Selector valves.

\- Strainers.

\- Vents.

\- Fuel pumps.

\- Carburetor or fuel-injection components.

Key pilot responsibilities include:

\- Correct tank selection.

\- Fuel quantity awareness.

\- Proper use of boost pumps.

\- Fuel contamination checks.

\- Understanding usable versus total fuel.

\- Monitoring fuel flow and imbalance where applicable.

**### Oil System**

Oil:

\- Lubricates.

\- Cools.

\- Cleans.

\- Seals.

\- Protects engine components.

The pilot monitors:

\- Oil pressure.

\- Oil temperature.

\- Oil quantity.

A low oil pressure indication, especially when paired with rising oil temperature, may indicate a serious engine problem.

Use the AFM/POH checklist.

**### Hydraulic Systems**

Hydraulic pressure may operate:

\- Brakes.

\- Landing gear.

\- Flaps.

\- Propeller systems.

\- Other components.

Hydraulic systems use fluid pressure to transmit force.

A leak or pressure loss may affect more than one component if they share the same system.

**### Electrical System**

A typical light-airplane electrical system includes:

\- Battery.

\- Alternator or generator.

\- Buses.

\- Circuit breakers or fuses.

\- Switches.

\- Ammeters or loadmeters.

\- Voltage indications.

The alternator normally supplies the electrical load and recharges the battery.

If the alternator fails:

**\*\*the airplane may continue operating only on battery power.\*\***

That makes load management important.

Turn off nonessential equipment and follow the AFM/POH procedure.

**### Circuit Breakers**

A circuit breaker protects wiring and equipment from excessive current.

Do not repeatedly reset a tripped circuit breaker.

A tripped breaker may indicate:

\- Short circuit.

\- Overload.

\- Equipment failure.

Follow the manufacturer's procedure.

**### Avionics**

Avionics may provide:

\- Communication.

\- Navigation.

\- Traffic.

\- Weather.

\- Surveillance.

\- Flight display information.

\- Automation.

Modern avionics are powerful, but they create new risks:

\- Mode confusion.

\- Heads-down fixation.

\- Incorrect data entry.

\- Overreliance.

\- Failure to cross-check.

The rule is:

**\*\*Know what mode is active, what mode is armed, and what the airplane should do next.\*\***

If the system does something unexpected:

**\*\*Regain direct control and reassess.\*\***

**### Automation**

Automation should reduce workload.

If it increases workload or confusion:

**\*\*disconnect it and fly the airplane.\*\***

Monitor:

\- Active mode.

\- Armed mode.

\- Flight director command.

\- Autopilot engagement.

\- Navigation source.

\- Altitude capture.

\- Lateral and vertical guidance.

Automation does not relieve the pilot of aircraft-control responsibility.

**### Pitot-Static System**

The pitot-static system provides pressure information to several instruments.

The **\*\*airspeed indicator\*\*** uses:

\- Pitot pressure.

\- Static pressure.

The **\*\*altimeter\*\*** uses:

\- Static pressure.

The **\*\*vertical-speed indicator\*\*** uses:

\- Static pressure.

A blockage can produce different failure indications depending on what is blocked.

The important lesson is:

**\*\*Recognize the pattern across multiple instruments.\*\***

**### Vacuum / Pressure Systems and Electronic Instruments**

Traditional gyroscopic instruments may use:

\- Vacuum.

\- Pressure.

\- Electrical power.

Modern airplanes may use:

\- AHRS.

\- ADC.

\- Electronic attitude systems.

\- Backup electronic instruments.

Teach the actual airplane.

Know:

\- What powers each display.

\- What sensors feed it.

\- What the backup source is.

\- What failure indication appears.

**### Environmental Systems**

Environmental systems may include:

\- Cabin heat.

\- Ventilation.

\- Defrost.

\- Air conditioning.

\- Pressurization.

In many piston airplanes, cabin heat uses air warmed around the exhaust system.

That creates a carbon-monoxide risk if the exhaust system leaks.

If CO is suspected:

\- Turn cabin heat off.

\- Open fresh-air vents.

\- Use oxygen if available.

\- Land as soon as practical.

**### Deicing and Anti-Icing**

**\*\*Anti-icing\*\*** prevents or delays ice formation.

**\*\*Deicing\*\*** removes ice after it forms.

Systems may include:

\- Pitot heat.

\- Carburetor heat.

\- Propeller heat.

\- Windshield heat or defrost.

\- Pneumatic boots.

\- Heated leading edges.

\- Fluid systems.

The important distinction is:

**\*\*Installed ice-protection equipment does not automatically make an airplane approved for flight into known icing.\*\***

Use the aircraft limitations and AFM/POH.

**### Carburetor Heat**

Carburetor ice can form because of:

\- Pressure drop in the venturi.

\- Fuel vaporization.

\- Moisture in the air.

Carburetor heat routes warm air into the induction system.

Applying carb heat can initially:

\- Reduce engine power.

\- Cause roughness as ice melts.

Follow the installed-aircraft procedure.

**### Oxygen Systems**

Installed oxygen systems may include:

\- Continuous-flow.

\- Diluter-demand.

\- Pressure-demand.

\- Pulse-demand systems.

The pilot must understand:

\- Supply quantity.

\- Flow indication.

\- Mask or cannula limitations.

\- Servicing precautions.

\- Fire and contamination hazards.

Under **\*\*14 CFR §91.211\*\***:

\- Above **\*\*12,500 through 14,000 feet MSL\*\***, required flightcrew must use oxygen for the portion of the flight exceeding 30 minutes.

\- Above **\*\*14,000 feet MSL\*\***, required flightcrew must use oxygen continuously.

\- Above **\*\*15,000 feet MSL\*\***, each occupant must be provided supplemental oxygen.

These are regulatory minimums.

**### System Abnormalities and Failures**

A system failure may first appear as:

\- An annunciation.

\- Abnormal gauge indication.

\- Unexpected sound.

\- Smell.

\- Vibration.

\- Control-force change.

\- Performance change.

\- Multiple instrument disagreements.

The first priority is always:

**\*\*Maintain aircraft control.\*\***

Then:

1\. Identify what changed.

2\. Cross-check independent indications.

3\. Determine which system is affected.

4\. Use the AFM/POH checklist.

5\. Manage the remaining system capability.

6\. Decide whether continued flight is safe.

**### Symptom vs. Cause**

An instrument indication is often a **\*\*symptom\*\***, not the cause.

For example:

\- Low voltage may indicate alternator failure.

\- Several abnormal instruments may indicate a shared pitot-static or electrical problem.

\- Engine roughness may be ignition, fuel, induction, icing, or another cause.

Do not fixate on the first explanation.

**\*\*Cross-check the system.\*\***

**### Inoperative Equipment**

If equipment is inoperative before flight, determine whether the airplane may legally and safely be operated.

**\*\*14 CFR §91.213\*\*** governs operation with inoperative instruments and equipment.

The pilot must determine whether the item is required by:

\- The aircraft type design.

\- The equipment list or KOEL, if applicable.

\- An AD.

\- **\*\*§91.205\*\*** for the type of operation.

\- Another applicable rule.

If it is not required and the regulation permits continued operation, the item must be handled as required by §91.213.

Do not simply say:

**\*\*"It isn't needed today, so we'll go."\*\***

**### ACS Standards — Aircraft Flight Controls and Operation of Systems**

The applicant should be able to explain the systems applicable to the test airplane, including:

\- Primary flight controls.

\- Secondary flight controls.

\- Powerplant and propeller.

\- Landing gear.

\- Fuel, oil, and hydraulic systems.

\- Electrical system.

\- Avionics.

\- Pitot-static, vacuum/pressure, and associated flight instruments.

\- Environmental systems.

\- Deicing and anti-icing.

\- Water rudders, if applicable.

\- Oxygen systems.

The applicant should also explain:

\- Indications of system abnormalities or failures.

\- Procedures for managing those failures.

The applicant should manage risks associated with:

\- Detecting system malfunctions.

\- Managing failures.

\- Monitoring automated systems.

\- Providing instruction in unfamiliar aircraft.

\- Operating unfamiliar displays or avionics.

The applicant must appropriately operate at least **\*\*three applicable systems\*\***.

**### Common Errors — Flight Controls and Systems**

\- Confusing roll, pitch, and yaw axes.

\- Using rudder to create the primary turning force in normal coordinated turns.

\- Failing to explain adverse yaw.

\- Flying with trim instead of trimming after establishing attitude.

\- Misunderstanding trim-tab direction.

\- Exceeding flap operating limitations.

\- Assuming all flap systems behave the same way.

\- Failing to monitor engine indications together.

\- Mismanaging fuel selection.

\- Ignoring oil-pressure or temperature trends.

\- Repeatedly resetting a circuit breaker.

\- Failing to recognize alternator failure and remaining battery endurance.

\- Overreliance on automation.

\- Mode confusion.

\- Fixation on avionics during high workload.

\- Failing to recognize a common-source instrument failure.

\- Treating anti-icing equipment as approval for known icing.

\- Failing to know what powers the installed instruments.

\- Failing to use the AFM/POH during an abnormal condition.

\- Continuing flight with a system failure that makes the aircraft unsafe.

\- Assuming inoperative equipment is legal without applying §91.213.

**### Instructor Emphasis**

The learner should leave the lesson understanding:

1\. **\*\*Primary controls move the airplane; secondary controls improve performance or reduce workload.\*\***

2\. **\*\*Aileron controls roll, elevator controls pitch, and rudder controls yaw.\*\***

3\. **\*\*Adverse yaw requires coordinated rudder.\*\***

4\. **\*\*Fly the airplane first and trim second.\*\***

5\. **\*\*The AFM/POH controls aircraft-specific system operation and limitations under §91.9.\*\***

6\. **\*\*§91.7 makes the PIC responsible for determining that the aircraft is safe for flight.\*\***

7\. **\*\*§91.205 identifies required instruments and equipment for various operations.\*\***

8\. **\*\*§91.213 controls operation with inoperative equipment.\*\***

9\. **\*\*§91.211 contains the regulatory supplemental-oxygen requirements.\*\***

10\. **\*\*During a malfunction: maintain control, cross-check, use the checklist, and make the conservative decision.\*\***

**### Close**

Aircraft systems are easiest to understand by asking four questions:

**\*\*What does it do?\*\***  

**\*\*What should normal look like?\*\***  

**\*\*How will I know when it fails?\*\***  

**\*\*What does the AFM/POH tell me to do next?\*\***

The most important rule is:

**\*\*Fly the airplane, identify the system, and use the approved procedure.\*\***


## AI.II.E.K1 — Airplane Systems

### AI.II.E.K1a — Primary Flight Controls

The **ailerons, elevator or stabilator, and rudder** are the primary controls required for safe flight. Ailerons control **roll** about the longitudinal axis, elevator controls **pitch** about the lateral axis, and rudder controls **yaw** about the vertical axis. Their movement changes airflow and pressure distribution, changing lift and drag around each control surface.

Ailerons move oppositely. The down aileron increases lift and drag on its wing, producing **adverse yaw** toward that wing; coordinated rudder pressure counters it. In a turn, ailerons establish bank, rudder keeps the nose aligned with the relative wind, and elevator back pressure increases AOA to maintain the vertical lift needed for altitude. As the desired bank is reached, relax aileron and rudder pressure; reduce elevator back pressure as the airplane rolls out.

Elevator and rudder effectiveness changes with airspeed. Control inputs should be smooth and appropriate to aircraft response; control stops limit deflection to help prevent overstress.

### AI.II.E.K1b — Secondary Flight Controls

Secondary controls improve performance or reduce pilot control force. They include **flaps, leading-edge devices, spoilers, and trim systems**. Flaps attach to the trailing edge and increase lift and induced drag for a given AOA, allowing the wing to serve both higher-speed cruise and lower-speed landing.

**Flap types:**

- **Plain flap:** the simplest type. It deflects as a trailing-edge portion of the airfoil, increasing camber, lift coefficient, and drag. It also moves the center of pressure aft, producing a nose-down pitching moment.
- **Split flap:** only the lower surface deflects. It produces slightly more lift than a plain flap but more drag because of the turbulent airflow behind the airfoil. At full extension, both plain and split flaps produce high drag with little additional lift.
- **Slotted flap:** a gap or duct allows high-energy air from below the wing to flow over the flap upper surface. This energizes the boundary layer, delays separation, and produces a greater maximum lift coefficient than plain or split flaps. Large aircraft may use double- or triple-slotted systems.
- **Fowler flap:** a slotted flap that slides aft on tracks before deflecting. It increases both wing area and camber. Early extension produces a large lift increase with little drag increase; farther extension adds drag with little additional lift.

**Leading-edge device types:**

- **Fixed slot:** a permanent opening that directs airflow to the upper wing surface and delays separation at higher AOA.
- **Movable slat:** a leading-edge segment that moves forward, either automatically as AOA increases or by pilot command. The opening lets air flow over the upper wing surface and delays separation.
- **Leading-edge flap:** increases wing camber and maximum lift. A small extension increases lift more than drag, and it can reduce the nose-down pitching moment of trailing-edge flaps when used with them.
- **Leading-edge cuff:** a fixed extension, usually down and forward, that improves upper-surface airflow attachment at high AOA and lowers stall speed. Its fixed shape can reduce maximum cruise airspeed.

Spoilers reduce lift and increase drag; they may assist roll control, descent, or braking after touchdown. Flap and leading-edge device extension may cause a nose-up or nose-down pitching moment depending on the airplane, so teach the installed aircraft's AFM/POH limitations and expected trim change.

Trim relieves the pilot of continuous control pressure. Set the desired power, pitch, and configuration, then trim until the airplane is balanced. Trim is not a substitute for control, and trim setting or effectiveness must be understood from the AFM/POH.

### AI.II.E.K1c — Powerplant and Propeller

The powerplant produces thrust and may drive supporting systems. A reciprocating engine converts fuel energy to rotary power; the propeller is an airfoil that converts this rotary power into thrust. The fuel, ignition, induction, cooling, lubrication, and exhaust systems must operate together within AFM/POH limits.

A fixed-pitch propeller is efficient at one intended airspeed/RPM combination. A constant-speed propeller uses a governor and oil pressure to change blade angle and maintain selected RPM. Teach the installed system: normal indications, operating limitations, and the checklist response to abnormal oil pressure, temperature, RPM, manifold pressure, or engine roughness.

Superchargers and turbosuperchargers compress induction air to increase density and engine power. A supercharger uses an engine-driven compressor; a turbocharger uses exhaust flow through a turbine to drive its compressor. FADEC-equipped aircraft use a digital computer to monitor and adjust engine operation, so electrical-system status and manufacturer procedures are especially significant.

### AI.II.E.K1d — Landing Gear

Landing gear supports the aircraft on the surface. Fixed gear remains extended; retractable gear reduces drag but adds position indications, warning systems, extension/retraction procedures, and emergency-extension considerations. Tricycle and tailwheel gear have different directional-control and visibility characteristics. Verify gear condition and configuration using the installed indications and AFM/POH procedures.

### AI.II.E.K1e — Fuel, Oil, and Hydraulic

The fuel system provides uninterrupted fuel flow to the engine. The oil system lubricates, cools, cleans, and protects the engine. Hydraulic systems use fluid pressure to operate components such as brakes, retractable landing gear, and some constant-speed propellers. Fuel quantity, selected tank, oil pressure/temperature, and hydraulic indications must be checked against the aircraft procedures and limitations.

### AI.II.E.K1f — Electrical

An aircraft electrical system supplies electrical power from a battery and an engine-driven generator or alternator. It supports starting, lighting, avionics, instruments, and other installed equipment. Buses, fuses, circuit breakers, switches, and meters distribute and protect that power. An alternator failure can leave the aircraft operating on battery power only, making load management and AFM/POH procedures essential.

### AI.II.E.K1g — Avionics

Avionics provide communication, navigation, surveillance, weather, traffic, display, and automation capability. Learn the installed avionics on the ground before relying on them in flight. Verify mode, source, selected course, frequency, annunciations, and data validity; cross-check automated information with aircraft position, outside conditions, and other available instruments.

Automation reduces workload only when it is correctly programmed, monitored, and used at an appropriate time. Fixation, overreliance, mode confusion, and heads-down programming during critical phases create risk.

Flight Guide Instructor Principle |

### AI.II.E.K1h — Pitot-Static, Vacuum/Pressure, and Flight Instruments

The **airspeed indicator** compares pitot and static pressure. The **altimeter** and **vertical-speed indicator** use static pressure. A blockage changes instrument indications in characteristic ways, so diagnose the system rather than trusting a single gauge.

Gyroscopic instruments use rigidity in space and precession. Depending on the aircraft, attitude and heading information may come from vacuum/pressure gyros, electrical gyros, or an electronic attitude and heading reference system. The turn coordinator is commonly electrically powered. The magnetic compass requires no electrical power but has acceleration and turning errors.

### AI.II.E.K1i — Environmental

Environmental systems may provide cabin heat, ventilation, defrost, pressurization, or air conditioning. Their operation and hazards vary by aircraft. Cabin heat and defrost systems require awareness of exhaust-system integrity and carbon monoxide risk. Use the AFM/POH for normal operation, limitations, and emergency actions.

### AI.II.E.K1j — Deicing and Anti-Icing

Deicing removes ice after formation; anti-icing prevents or delays formation. Installed systems may include pitot heat, carburetor heat, windshield defrost, propeller heat, boots, fluid, or heated leading edges. Use only the aircraft-approved procedure and understand that ice contamination changes lift, drag, weight, stall characteristics, and control response.

### AI.II.E.K1k — Water Rudders

On seaplanes and amphibians, water rudders improve directional control at low speed on the water. Their use, extension, and retraction are aircraft-specific; teach the installed procedure and ensure the water rudders are retracted before takeoff as required by the AFM/POH.

### AI.II.E.K1l — Oxygen System

Installed oxygen systems provide supplemental oxygen through masks or cannulas. Systems can be continuous-flow, diluter-demand, pressure-demand, or electrical pulse-demand. Use the applicable AFM/POH equipment and servicing procedures; oxygen-system fire, contamination, and compatibility hazards require disciplined handling.

## AI.II.E.K2 — System Abnormalities and Failures

A system abnormality is recognized by a change from expected indications, sounds, smells, handling, annunciations, or performance. First **maintain aircraft control**, then use the applicable AFM/POH procedure, identify the affected system, and manage the remaining capability. Do not reset a circuit breaker or continue a flight based on assumption; use the manufacturer procedure and make a conservative decision when the system affects safety.

Cross-check information. For example, a pitot-static or vacuum failure affects a group of instruments in a recognizable pattern; an alternator failure changes electrical indications and requires load management; an engine or fuel-system abnormality requires immediate attention to the applicable checklist and available landing options.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
A failed indication is a symptom. Maintain control, verify with independent indications, use the checklist, and decide early whether the remaining system capability supports continued safe flight.
]]

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. What are the primary flight controls and what does each control?

[[answer:
Ailerons control roll, elevator or stabilator controls pitch, and rudder controls yaw. Each acts about a different aircraft axis.
]]

### 2. Why is rudder used when ailerons are applied?

[[answer:
The down aileron produces increased lift and drag, causing adverse yaw. Coordinated rudder counters that yaw while the ailerons establish bank.
]]

### 3. What is the purpose of trim?

[[answer:
Trim relieves continuous control pressure after the desired power, attitude, and configuration are established. It does not replace primary flight control.
]]

### 4. How does a fixed-pitch propeller differ from a constant-speed propeller?

[[answer:
A fixed-pitch propeller is efficient at one intended RPM and airspeed combination. A constant-speed propeller changes blade angle through its governing system to maintain selected RPM.
]]

### 5. What instruments use pitot and static pressure?

[[answer:
The airspeed indicator uses pitot and static pressure. The altimeter and vertical-speed indicator use static pressure.
]]

### 6. How should a pilot respond to an unfamiliar avionics display or automation mode?

[[answer:
Learn it on the ground before flight, verify active mode and expected response, cross-check the result, and regain direct control if the system does not perform as expected.
]]

### 7. What is the first priority during a system malfunction?

[[answer:
Maintain aircraft control, then identify the affected system, use the applicable AFM/POH procedure, manage the remaining capability, and make a conservative safety decision.
]]
