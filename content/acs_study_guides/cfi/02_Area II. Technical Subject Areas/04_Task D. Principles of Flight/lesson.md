# Task D. Principles of Flight

**Area II — Technical Subject Areas**

## Objective

To determine the applicant understands aerodynamics appropriate to the desired instructor certificate, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate appropriate skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Principles of flight connect aircraft design and the four forces to the pilot's everyday control of **angle of attack, power, attitude, bank, coordination, and energy**. A CFI must teach these concepts accurately enough that a learner can recognize aerodynamic risk before it becomes a stall, structural overload, loss of control, or wake-turbulence encounter.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested Schedule**

- Ground lesson: approximately 60–75 minutes
- Whiteboard/model-airplane demonstration and questions: approximately 20 minutes

**Equipment**

- Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge
- CFI Airplane ACS
- Aircraft POH/AFM for aircraft-specific limitations
- Model airplane or whiteboard
- Flight Guide visual aids

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner Actions**

- Explain the relationship between the four forces, AOA, lift, drag, and thrust.
- Describe aircraft stability, controllability, and left-turning tendencies.
- Relate bank angle and airspeed to load factor and stall speed.
- State practical wake-turbulence avoidance actions.

**Instructor Actions**

- Use diagrams and a model to make force vectors and aircraft axes visible.
- Connect each aerodynamic concept to an operational decision.
- Use the aircraft POH/AFM for specific limitations and speeds.
- Correct misconceptions before they become unsafe control habits.

## Completion Standards

The applicant can deliver instruction on at least three Knowledge elements from `K1` through `K6`, accurately explain the associated aerodynamic principles, and identify practical risk-management actions for stalls, excessive load factors, and wake turbulence.

## AI.II.D.K1 — Airfoil Design Characteristics

An **airfoil** is a surface designed to produce lift. Its key features include the **leading edge, trailing edge, chord line, camber, thickness, span, and aspect ratio**. **Angle of attack (AOA)** is the acute angle between the chord line and relative wind.

Lift is produced by the dynamic action of air on an airfoil. As AOA increases, lift increases to the critical AOA; beyond that point, lift decreases rapidly and the airfoil stalls. Lift also depends on air density, velocity, wing area, and coefficient of lift. A pilot controls lift primarily through **AOA and airspeed**.

Drag opposes motion. **Parasite drag** includes form, interference, and skin-friction drag and increases with airspeed. **Induced drag** is inseparable from lift production and increases as AOA increases. The total-drag curve has a minimum point, corresponding to maximum lift-to-drag ratio.

### Airflow, Stalls, and Flaps

At low AOA, airflow remains attached to the wing surface. As AOA increases, the wing produces more lift and induced drag until airflow separates at the **critical AOA**. A stall is the rapid loss of lift caused by that separation; the wing still produces some lift, but not enough to sustain the required flight path. A wing stalls at its critical AOA, not at one fixed pitch attitude or airspeed.

Flaps change wing camber and chord-line geometry. They normally increase lift and drag for a given AOA, affecting low-speed performance and the AOA at which the wing stalls. Ice, snow, frost, or other contamination disrupts airflow, increases drag, reduces lift, and can cause separation at a lower AOA than expected.

An installed **AOA indicator** displays the margin between current AOA and the critical AOA. It supplements airspeed because published stall speeds apply only to specified conditions; the pilot must understand the particular installed system and its limitations.

## AI.II.D.K2 — Airplane Stability, Maneuverability, and Controllability

**Stability** is an aircraft's inherent tendency to return to, or continue on, its original flight path after disturbance. **Static stability** describes the initial response; **dynamic stability** describes the motion over time. Positive stability returns toward equilibrium, neutral stability remains in the displaced condition, and negative stability continues away from equilibrium.

**Maneuverability** is the aircraft's ability to be maneuvered and withstand maneuver stresses. **Controllability** is its ability to respond to pilot control inputs for flight path and attitude. These characteristics are influenced by aircraft design, weight, inertia, controls, structural strength, and powerplant.

Longitudinal stability acts in pitch, lateral stability in roll, and directional stability in yaw. CG location, horizontal and vertical tail surfaces, dihedral, sweepback, keel effect, and weight distribution affect stability. A forward CG relative to the center of lift provides a natural nose-down tendency that supports longitudinal stability.

### Stability About Each Axis

**Longitudinal stability** is pitch stability about the lateral axis. The relationship of the wing, CG, and horizontal tail gives most conventional airplanes a restoring nose-down tendency when speed decreases or AOA increases. An aft CG reduces this restoring tendency and can make slow flight, stall recovery, and spin recovery more difficult.

**Lateral stability** is roll stability about the longitudinal axis. Dihedral, sweepback, keel effect, and weight distribution tend to restore wings-level flight after a sideslip. **Directional stability** is yaw stability about the vertical axis; the vertical fin and fuselage area aft of the CG act like a weather vane and turn the nose toward the relative wind.

Aircraft design involves compromise. Greater stability can require more control effort and reduce maneuverability; less stability can make an airplane more responsive but can demand more active control from the pilot.

## AI.II.D.K3 — Turning Tendency

A conventional airplane with a clockwise-turning propeller, viewed from the pilot seat, commonly exhibits left-turning tendencies that are strongest at high power and low airspeed.

- **Torque reaction:** the aircraft reacts opposite the propeller rotation, tending to roll left.
- **Spiraling slipstream:** rotating propeller airflow strikes the vertical stabilizer and tends to yaw the nose left.
- **P-factor:** at high AOA, the descending propeller blade produces more thrust than the ascending blade, tending to yaw the nose left.
- **Gyroscopic precession:** a force applied to a rotating propeller is felt 90 degrees ahead in the direction of rotation; pitch changes can therefore create yawing forces.

Maintain coordinated flight with correct rudder pressure as required by the aircraft and condition. The exact magnitude of each tendency varies with aircraft design, power, airspeed, and attitude.

### Why the Tendencies Change

Torque reaction is normally most noticeable during high-power, low-speed conditions and also adds a left-yaw tendency during the takeoff roll through unequal main-gear loading. Spiraling slipstream is most compact and most effective against the vertical stabilizer at high propeller speed and low forward speed. P-factor increases at positive AOA because the descending blade has greater resultant velocity and produces more thrust. Gyroscopic precession is especially apparent in tailwheel airplanes when raising the tail, but it occurs whenever a force changes the propeller plane.

## AI.II.D.K4 — Forces Acting on an Airplane

The four forces are **thrust, drag, lift, and weight**. Thrust acts generally forward and opposes drag. Drag acts rearward and opposes thrust. Lift acts perpendicular to the flight path through the center of lift and opposes weight in level flight. Weight acts vertically downward through the CG.

In steady flight, the opposing force components are in equilibrium. This applies in stabilized climbs and descents as well as level flight, though the force vectors have different components. In a climb, thrust has an upward component; in a glide, weight has a forward component. The pilot coordinates **power and AOA** to control airspeed, altitude, and flight path.

### Applying the Forces

To accelerate in level flight, thrust must exceed drag; to maintain a constant airspeed, thrust and drag balance. At lower airspeed, a higher AOA is required to maintain lift, which increases induced drag. In a stabilized climb, excess thrust is required to overcome drag plus a component of weight. In a stabilized descent, weight has a forward component along the flight path, so power is reduced to maintain the desired airspeed.

In a coordinated turn, banking tilts lift. The **horizontal component of lift** turns the airplane, while the **vertical component of lift** supports weight. To maintain altitude as bank increases, the pilot increases AOA and power as required. Rudder coordinates the nose with the relative wind; it does not supply the primary turning force.

## AI.II.D.K5 — Load Factors in Airplane Design

A **load factor** is the ratio of total load supported by the aircraft structure to aircraft weight, expressed in Gs. Load factor matters because a pilot can impose structural overload and because increasing load factor increases stall speed.

In a coordinated level turn, banking tilts lift and requires more total lift to maintain altitude. Load factor increases rapidly beyond about 45–50 degrees of bank: at 60 degrees it is 2 Gs, and at 80 degrees it is 5.76 Gs. Stalling speed increases with the square root of load factor, so an aircraft can stall at an apparently high airspeed when the load factor is increased.

Limit load is the maximum expected loading without structural damage; ultimate load includes the required safety factor beyond limit load. **Va** must come from the aircraft's POH/AFM. Full, abrupt, or combined control inputs can cause structural damage, including below Va; reduce speed in rough air and avoid abrupt pull-ups or large alternating control inputs.

### Flight Envelope and CG

The **Vg diagram** depicts the permitted combinations of airspeed and load factor. Its curved boundaries are the airplane's aerodynamic stall limits; its straight boundaries are structural load limits. The maneuvering-speed point is where the positive stall boundary meets the positive limit-load boundary. Operate inside the envelope for the actual aircraft weight and conditions.

Weight and CG affect the loads and flight characteristics behind the diagram. Increased gross weight lengthens takeoff, reduces climb, increases landing speed and roll, and raises the lift required in maneuvering. A forward CG generally increases tail downforce, wing loading, drag, and stall speed; an aft CG reduces longitudinal stability and can make recovery from a stall or spin progressively more difficult. Use the aircraft-specific AFM/POH calculation and limits rather than a loading rule of thumb.

## AI.II.D.K6 — Wingtip Vortices and Appropriate Precautions

Wingtip vortices form because high-pressure air below a lifting wing flows around the tips toward lower pressure above the wing. The resulting rotating wake produces downwash and induced drag. Vortices are strongest when the generating aircraft is **heavy, clean, and slow**, a condition common on approach and departure.

Avoid wake turbulence by avoiding the preceding aircraft's flight path, rotating before its rotation point when departing behind it, and approaching above its path with touchdown beyond its touchdown point when landing behind it. Consider wind direction because vortices drift with the wind. Do not follow another aircraft on a similar flight path within 1,000 feet of altitude; when uncertain of its takeoff or landing point, allow time for the wake to dissipate.

### Wake Behavior and Planning

Wake vortices descend and move outward behind the generating aircraft while drifting with the wind. A crosswind can move one vortex away from the runway while causing the upwind vortex to remain near or drift across it. Vortices begin as lift is produced and diminish after landing, but wake avoidance is still planned around the larger aircraft's actual flight path, not simply the runway centerline.

The hazard is greatest when a smaller airplane encounters the wake of a larger aircraft close to the ground, where recovery space is limited. Before takeoff or approach, identify the generating aircraft, its rotation or touchdown point, wind direction, and the vertical and lateral path that preserves a safe margin.

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. What is angle of attack, and why is it important?

[[answer:
AOA is the acute angle between the airfoil chord line and relative wind. It controls lift and drag, and a wing stalls when critical AOA is exceeded regardless of airspeed.
]]

### 2. What are the four forces and how do they act in steady flight?

[[answer:
Thrust, drag, lift, and weight. In steady flight, opposing force components are in equilibrium; their individual directions and components change for climbs and descents.
]]

### 3. Distinguish stability, maneuverability, and controllability.

[[answer:
Stability is the inherent tendency to return toward or continue on a flight path after disturbance. Maneuverability is the ability to maneuver and withstand the resulting stresses. Controllability is the response to pilot inputs for flight path and attitude.
]]

### 4. Name the four common left-turning tendencies.

[[answer:
Torque reaction, spiraling slipstream, P-factor, and gyroscopic precession.
]]

### 5. How do bank angle and load factor affect stall speed?

[[answer:
In a coordinated level turn, more bank requires more lift and increases load factor. Stall speed increases with the square root of load factor, so a wing can stall at an apparently high airspeed.
]]

### 6. What does Va protect against, and what does it not protect against?

[[answer:
Va is the design maneuvering speed established in the POH/AFM. It provides protection for one full control input in one axis in smooth air, but does not protect against multiple or combined abrupt inputs or all turbulence loads.
]]

### 7. When are wingtip vortices strongest and how are they avoided?

[[answer:
They are strongest when the generating aircraft is heavy, clean, and slow. Avoid its flight path, rotate before its rotation point, approach above its path, touch down beyond its touchdown point, and account for wind drift and altitude separation.
]]
