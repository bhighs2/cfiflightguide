# Task A. Communications, Light Signals, and Runway Lighting Systems

**Area VI — Airport and Seaplane Base Operations**

## Objective

Determine that the applicant understands communications, ATC light signals, and runway lighting systems and can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate the required skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Whether at a busy towered airport or a quiet non-towered one, radio communication is essential to every flight — and when the radios fail, the light gun and the runway status lights still speak. A flight instructor must communicate with correct phraseology, read back what must be read back, recognize the light signals, and teach a learner to do the same and to manage lost communications.

This is a ground-focused Task. The ACS skill elements require the applicant to **select and activate frequencies, transmit with standard phraseology, acknowledge and comply with ATC, and analyze and correct common errors**.

## Schedule / Equipment

- Ground: 20 minutes
- Student Q&A: 10 minutes
- Airport diagrams, a VFR sectional chart, and the Chart Supplement
- Whiteboard / markers (optional)

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** explain the types of ATC and self-announce frequencies and their purpose, proper phraseology and the phonetic alphabet and required readbacks, self-announcing procedures, how to determine the correct frequency, lost-communication and light-signal procedures, and the runway status lights — then select and activate frequencies, transmit with proper phraseology, and comply with ATC instructions.

**Instructor:** deliver the ground lesson, have the learner make example radio calls and read back clearances, and require the learner to recognize the light signals and the lost-communication procedures.

## Completion Standards

The learner can explain and apply:

- The **types of ATC and self-announce frequencies** and their purpose.
- **Proper phraseology, the phonetic alphabet, and required readbacks**, and proper self-announcing procedures.
- How to **determine the correct frequency** on the ground and in the air.
- **Lost procedures, lost-communication procedures, ATC light-gun signals, and the runway status lights.**
- Before each flight: **select and activate the appropriate frequencies**, communicate with proper phraseology, and comply with ATC instructions.

### Purpose

Communication is part of aircraft control.

The objective is to communicate clearly, use the correct frequency and phraseology, comply with ATC instructions, recognize light signals and runway status lights, and know what to do when communication fails.

A good radio transmission should answer:

- Who am I calling?
- Who am I?
- Where am I?
- What do I want or intend to do?

But the priority is always:

**understanding.**

Brevity is useful only if both sides understand the message.

### Obtain the Correct Frequency

Before flight, identify the frequencies likely to be used.

Common sources include:

- Chart Supplement.
- Airport diagram.
- Sectional.
- Terminal Area Chart.
- EFB.
- ATIS or other published airport information.

Common frequencies include:

- Clearance Delivery.
- Ground.
- Tower.
- CTAF.
- UNICOM.
- ATIS.
- ASOS/AWOS.
- Approach.
- Departure.
- Center.
- Flight Service.
- 121.5 MHz emergency frequency.

Plan frequencies before the high-workload portions of the flight.

### Listen Before Transmitting

Before keying the microphone:

- Listen to the frequency.
- Make sure another transmission is not in progress.
- Know what you intend to say.
- Then transmit clearly.

Do not change frequencies and immediately begin talking.

Give yourself enough time to hear whether the frequency is already in use.

### Radio Technique

Good radio technique includes:

- Speak clearly.
- Use normal conversational volume.
- Hold the microphone close enough for clear audio.
- Avoid long pauses while holding the microphone keyed.
- Release the microphone when finished.
- Use standard terms whenever possible.
- Include the aircraft identification.

If unsure whether the transmission was received:

wait briefly, then try again.

If the radio seems silent:

check:

- Volume.
- Squelch.
- Correct frequency.
- Audio-panel selection.
- Headset connection.
- Push-to-talk switch.

### Initial Contact

A typical initial call contains:

- Facility being called.
- Aircraft identification.
- Position or location.
- Request or intention.

Example:

**“Conroe Ground, Skyhawk 12345, north ramp, with Alpha, ready to taxi.”**

For flight following:

**“Houston Approach, Skyhawk 12345, five miles north of Conroe, three thousand five hundred, VFR to College Station, request flight following.”**

### Non-Towered Airport Calls

At a nontowered airport, calls should clearly identify:

- Airport.
- Aircraft.
- Position.
- Runway.
- Intentions.

Example:

**“Conroe Traffic, Skyhawk 12345, five miles west, one thousand five hundred, entering left downwind Runway 14, Conroe Traffic.”**

Use the airport name at both the beginning and end when appropriate.

Remember:

**a radio call does not create separation.**

Aircraft without radios may also be operating.

### Standard Phraseology

Use the phraseology in:

- AIM.
- Pilot/Controller Glossary.

Standard phraseology reduces ambiguity.

But if standard terminology does not communicate the message clearly:

**use plain language.**

The objective is accurate communication.

### Phonetic Alphabet

Use the ICAO phonetic alphabet for:

- Call signs.
- Taxiways.
- Alphanumeric identifiers.

Examples:

- N123AB — “November One Two Three Alpha Bravo.”
- Taxiway B — “Taxiway Bravo.”

Runway numbers are spoken by individual digits:

- Runway 17 — “Runway One Seven.”
- Runway 35 — “Runway Three Five.”

### ATC Clearance vs. Instruction

An **ATC clearance** authorizes an aircraft to proceed under specified conditions.

An **ATC instruction** directs a specific action.

Neither relieves the PIC of responsibility for:

- Aircraft control.
- Regulatory compliance.
- Safe operation.

Under **14 CFR §91.3**, the PIC remains directly responsible for and the final authority as to the operation of the aircraft.

### Compliance With ATC — 14 CFR §91.123

Once an ATC clearance has been accepted:

the pilot may not deviate from it unless:

- An amended clearance is obtained.
- An emergency exists.
- The deviation is in response to a TCAS resolution advisory, when applicable.

If an instruction is unclear:

**ask immediately.**

Do not guess.

### Required and Important Readbacks

Read back items that are critical to safety and controller verification.

These include:

- Runway assignments.
- Runway crossing clearances.
- Hold-short instructions.
- Line-up-and-wait instructions.
- Altitude assignments.
- Heading or vector assignments.
- LAHSO clearances when accepted.

Include the aircraft call sign in the readback.

Example:

ATC:

**“Skyhawk 12345, taxi Runway 14 via Alpha, hold short Runway 1.”**

Pilot:

**“Runway 14 via Alpha, hold short Runway 1, Skyhawk 12345.”**

### Readback Is a Cross-Check

A readback is not simply radio etiquette.

It allows:

- Pilot.
- Controller.

to compare what was issued with what was understood.

If ATC corrects the readback:

**accept the correction and restate the clearance correctly.**

### Unable

The pilot may respond:

**“Unable.”**

when an ATC instruction or clearance cannot be safely or legally accepted.

The pilot should then:

- State the limitation when useful.
- Request an alternative.

Do not accept an unsafe clearance merely because ATC issued it.

### Frequency Changes

When ATC directs a frequency change:

- Acknowledge.
- Note the frequency if necessary.
- Change frequency.
- Listen before transmitting.

If the new frequency does not respond:

- Verify the frequency.
- Try again.
- Return to the previous controller if necessary.

### Appropriate Use of the Transponder

Use the assigned transponder code when ATC provides one.

Common codes include:

- **1200** — VFR when no discrete code is assigned.
- **7500** — unlawful interference.
- **7600** — radio communication failure.
- **7700** — emergency.

Use altitude reporting when required.

When ATC says:

**“Squawk ident.”**

press IDENT once.

### Lost Communication — First Actions

If communication is lost:

1. Maintain aircraft control.
2. Continue navigation.
3. Troubleshoot the communication system.
4. Attempt alternate methods of communication.
5. Use the transponder as appropriate.
6. Watch for ATC light signals if operating near a towered airport.

Do not let a radio problem become an aircraft-control problem.

### Troubleshooting Communication Failure

Check the simple items first:

- Correct frequency.
- Volume.
- Squelch.
- Audio panel.
- Headset plugs.
- Microphone or push-to-talk.
- Radio selection.
- Circuit breaker only as permitted by the aircraft procedure.
- Electrical-system status.

Try:

- Another radio.
- Another headset.
- Another frequency.
- 121.5 MHz.

If receiving but unable to transmit:

ATC may be able to give instructions and ask for:

- IDENT.
- Specific transponder codes.
- Predictable aircraft actions.

### Transponder Code for Lost Communications

If two-way communications cannot be restored:

**squawk 7600.**

This alerts ATC to the communication failure.

### 121.5 MHz

121.5 MHz is the emergency frequency.

If normal communications fail:

attempt contact on 121.5 when appropriate.

Other aircraft or ATC facilities may relay the message.

### ATC Light Signals — 14 CFR §91.125

A tower can communicate with an aircraft using a light gun when radio communication is unavailable.

The meaning depends on whether the airplane is:

- In flight.
- On the ground.

### Steady Green

**In flight:**
- Cleared to land.

**On the ground:**
- Cleared for takeoff.

### Flashing Green

**In flight:**
- Return for landing.
- A steady green will normally follow when cleared to land.

**On the ground:**
- Cleared to taxi.

### Steady Red

**In flight:**
- Give way to other aircraft and continue circling.

**On the ground:**
- Stop.

### Flashing Red

**In flight:**
- Airport unsafe.
- Do not land.

**On the ground:**
- Taxi clear of the runway in use.

### Flashing White

**On the ground:**
- Return to the starting point on the airport.

There is no normal airborne meaning for flashing white in the standard light-gun table.

### Alternating Red and Green

**In flight or on the ground:**
- Exercise extreme caution.

### Acknowledging Light Signals

During daylight, an aircraft in flight may acknowledge by:

- Rocking the wings.

On the ground, acknowledge as appropriate without creating a hazard.

At night, acknowledgement may be made by:

- Flashing landing lights.
- Flashing navigation lights.

The priority remains safe aircraft control.

### Limitations of Light Signals

Light signals are limited because they cannot communicate:

- Complex instructions.
- Explanations.
- Detailed sequencing.

The pilot still needs to:

- Observe traffic.
- Understand the airport flow.
- Maintain situational awareness.

### Radio Failure Near a Towered Airport

If airborne and unable to communicate:

- Determine runway and traffic flow.
- Remain predictable.
- Watch the tower for light signals.
- Maintain visual separation.
- Enter the pattern only when safe.

If conditions or airport complexity make this unsafe:

consider diverting to a suitable nontowered airport.

### Radar Assistance

ATC radar may assist a VFR pilot with:

- Position identification.
- Traffic advisories.
- Vectors.
- Assistance to an airport.
- Emergency support.

If lost or uncertain of position:

**ask early.**

Do not wait until fuel, weather, or terrain turns uncertainty into an emergency.

### Declaring an Emergency

A pilot should declare an emergency when immediate assistance or priority is needed.

Examples may include:

- Lost communications in difficult conditions.
- Lost or disoriented flight near terrain or weather.
- Mechanical problem.
- Fuel emergency.
- Medical issue.

Early declaration preserves options.

Do not delay because of embarrassment or concern about paperwork.

### Runway Status Light System

Runway Status Lights are automated red in-pavement lights that warn pilots and vehicle operators when a runway operation is unsafe.

The system uses airport surveillance information and activates independently of a controller's spoken clearance.

The two primary components are:

- **Runway Entrance Lights — REL**
- **Takeoff Hold Lights — THL**

### Runway Entrance Lights

RELs are red in-pavement lights installed at runway entrances and crossings.

When illuminated, they indicate:

**it is unsafe to enter or cross the runway.**

If the pilot has an ATC clearance but the RELs are illuminated:

- Stop.
- Remain clear of the runway when possible.
- Contact ATC and resolve the conflict.

Do not assume the clearance overrides the red lights.

### Takeoff Hold Lights

THLs are red in-pavement lights visible to an aircraft positioned for departure.

When illuminated, they indicate:

**it is unsafe to begin or continue the takeoff because another aircraft or vehicle is occupying or about to occupy the runway.**

If THLs illuminate before takeoff:

- Remain stopped.

If they illuminate after the takeoff roll has begun:

- Use best judgment.
- Stop if safely feasible.
- Contact ATC.

### Runway Status Lights Are Not a Clearance

When RELs or THLs extinguish:

**that does not constitute an ATC clearance.**

The pilot still needs the appropriate ATC clearance to:

- Enter.
- Cross.
- Take off.

The lights are an independent warning system.

### Runway Edge Lights

Runway edge lights identify the runway edges during darkness and reduced visibility.

They are normally:

**white.**

On instrument runways:

the final portion may show **yellow** edge lights as a caution that the runway end is approaching.

### Threshold and Runway-End Lights

Threshold and runway-end fixtures are directional.

From the approach side:

**green** identifies the runway threshold.

From the runway looking toward the end:

**red** identifies the runway end.

### Runway Centerline Lights

Runway centerline lights are white over most of the runway.

Near the departure end:

- White begins alternating with red.
- The final portion becomes all red.

This gives the pilot a visual indication of remaining runway.

### Touchdown Zone Lights

Touchdown-zone lights are white lights arranged on both sides of the runway centerline near the touchdown area.

They improve:

- Runway definition.
- Depth perception.
- Touchdown-zone identification.

especially in reduced visibility.

### Approach Lighting

Approach lighting helps the pilot transition from:

- Instrument references.
- To the visual runway environment.

The installed system varies by runway.

This Task does not require memorizing every approach-light configuration.

The pilot should recognize the system shown in the applicable chart and airport information.

### VASI and PAPI

Visual glidepath systems provide descent guidance.

A typical **VASI** indication is:

- White over white — high.
- Red over white — on glidepath.
- Red over red — low.

A typical four-light **PAPI** indication is:

- Four white — high.
- Three white / one red — slightly high.
- Two white / two red — on glidepath.
- One white / three red — slightly low.
- Four red — low.

### Communication Risk Management

Communication failures often begin before the radio actually fails.

Risks include:

- Wrong frequency.
- Stepped-on transmission.
- Similar call signs.
- Expectation bias.
- Failure to hear a clearance change.
- Incorrect readback.
- Failure to challenge an unclear instruction.

Mitigation includes:

- Listen first.
- Use the aircraft call sign.
- Read back critical items.
- Ask for clarification.
- Write down complex clearances.
- Keep radio work secondary to aircraft control.

### Similar Call Signs

When aircraft on the same frequency have similar call signs:

- Listen closely.
- Use the full call sign when necessary.
- Do not act on a clearance unless you are certain it was intended for you.

If uncertain:

**verify with ATC.**

### Common Errors

Common errors include:

- Using the wrong frequency.
- Transmitting without listening first.
- Poor microphone technique.
- Omitting aircraft identification.
- Nonstandard or ambiguous phraseology.
- Failing to read back a hold-short instruction.
- Reading back an incorrect runway or altitude.
- Acting on a clearance intended for another aircraft.
- Failing to ask for clarification.
- Improper transponder use.
- Forgetting 7600 during communication failure.
- Not troubleshooting simple equipment issues.
- Misinterpreting light-gun signals.
- Crossing an illuminated REL.
- Beginning takeoff with illuminated THLs.
- Treating extinguished runway status lights as an ATC clearance.
- Delaying an emergency declaration.

## AI.VI.A.K1 — Obtaining the Appropriate Radio Frequencies

Know where to find the correct frequency. **On the ground**, use the **Chart Supplement**, the **airport diagram**, or the **VFR sectional** — the tower, ground, and ATIS frequencies are published. **In the air**, the important frequencies are printed on the **VFR sectional**. The common frequencies:

- **ATIS** — a recorded broadcast of the weather and airport information, coded by letter ("Information November").
- **Ground** — for taxi; **Tower** — for takeoff and landing.
- **Approach/Departure** — for radar service around the airport; **Center** — en route.
- **Guard (121.5)** — the emergency frequency, monitored.
- **FSS** — for flight service, briefings, and flight plans.
- **CTAF / UNICOM** — the common traffic advisory frequency at non-towered airports, and UNICOM for airport services.

## AI.VI.A.K2 — Radio Communication Procedures and Phraseology

Use the **standard phraseology** of the AIM and the Pilot/Controller Glossary. **Listen before transmitting — think, then speak.** Use the **phonetic alphabet** for call signs and alphanumeric identifiers (the tail number N12345 is "November One-Two-Three-Four-Five"; a runway 25 is "Two Five"). Read back what must be read back — hold-short instructions, runway assignments, clearances, and altitude/heading assignments.

**Radio-call examples (from the Flight Guide [Sample Radio Calls](/knowledge/SampleRadioCalls.pdf)):**

- **Towered — taxi:** "Hooks Ground, Cessna 12345, at the south ramp, with Information November, ready to taxi."
- **Towered — takeoff:** "Hooks Tower, Cessna 12345, holding short Runway 35, ready for departure."
- **Non-towered (CTAF):** "Apopka Traffic, Cessna 12345, five miles west at 1,500 feet, entering a left downwind for Runway 33, Apopka Traffic."

The CTAF/self-announce format: **[frequency name], [call sign], [position], [intentions], [frequency name again]**.

## AI.VI.A.K3 — ATC Light-Signal Recognition

When radio communication is lost at a towered airport, the tower can direct the aircraft with a **light gun**. Recognize the signals (§ 91.125) — for aircraft **in flight** and **on the ground**:

- **Steady green** — in flight: cleared to land; on the ground: cleared for takeoff.
- **Flashing green** — in flight: return for landing (to be followed by steady green); on the ground: cleared to taxi.
- **Steady red** — in flight: give way to other aircraft and continue circling; on the ground: stop.
- **Flashing red** — in flight: airport unsafe, do not land; on the ground: taxi clear of the runway in use.
- **Flashing white** — on the ground: return to the starting point.
- **Alternating red and green** — exercise extreme caution (both).

## AI.VI.A.K4 — Appropriate Use of the Transponder

The **transponder** makes the aircraft visible to ATC radar. Use it appropriately: **squawk the assigned code**, **Mode C (altitude reporting)** where required, **1200 for VFR** when not on an assigned code, **7700 for an emergency**, **7600 for lost communications**, and **7500 for hijack**. Respond to "squawk ident" when ATC requests it. The transponder and ADS-B are required in the controlled airspace covered in Task G.

## AI.VI.A.K5 — Lost Communication Procedures

If communications are lost, **fly the airplane first** and navigate; then troubleshoot the radio (volume, squelch, frequency, the intercom, the headset). If communications cannot be restored: **squawk 7600**, and there is always an ATC facility listening on **121.5 (Guard)** — call "in the blind." At a towered airport, approach the traffic pattern carefully and **watch for the light-gun signals** from the tower. Under IFR, the lost-communication procedures of § 91.185 apply.

## AI.VI.A.K6 — Equipment Issues That Could Cause Loss of Communication

Communications can be lost for many reasons: an **electrical-system failure**, **incorrect radio or intercom settings**, a **failed headset or stuck mic**, or simply **dialing in the wrong frequency**. Before assuming a failure, check the simple things — the volume, the squelch, the frequency, the audio selector, and the headset connection. Know the equipment and how to isolate the issue.

## AI.VI.A.K7 — Radar Assistance

ATC radar can assist a VFR aircraft — **flight following**, traffic advisories, and, when needed, **vectors and DF steers** to a position or an airport. If lost or in difficulty, **don't hesitate to declare an emergency** — radar assistance and 121.5 are there for exactly that. (Radar services are covered in depth in Task H.)

## AI.VI.A.K8 — Runway Status Lighting Systems

**Runway Status Lights (RWSL)** are a fully automated system that tells pilots and surface-vehicle operators when it is **unsafe to enter, cross, take off from, or land on a runway** — independent of the human controllers. The system processes surveillance and activates:

- **Runway Entrance Lights (REL)** — red lights at the taxiway/runway intersection when it is unsafe to enter or cross.
- **Takeoff Hold Lights (THL)** — red lights ahead when it is unsafe to take off.

## AI.VI.A.K9 — Common Errors Related to This Task

- **Using the improper frequency:** confirm the frequency on the sectional, diagram, or Chart Supplement before transmitting.
- **Improper procedure and phraseology:** at a non-towered airport, state the call sign, position, runway, and intentions; read back the required items at a towered airport.
- **Not acknowledging or complying with ATC instructions:** acknowledge and comply, or say "unable."
- **Not understanding or complying with the light signals:** know the in-flight and on-the-ground meanings.
- **Hesitating to declare an emergency:** if in doubt, declare it — radar assistance and Guard are there to help.

## Risk Management

### AI.VI.A.R1 — Communication

Poor communication — a wrong frequency, a clipped transmission, a misunderstood clearance, a missed readback — is a runway-incursion and collision risk. Mitigate by listening first, using standard phraseology, reading back what must be read back, confirming the frequency, and asking when uncertain. (The communication figures in this lesson illustrate the barriers to and the monitoring of clear communication.)

### AI.VI.A.R2 — Deciding If and When to Declare an Emergency

Pilots hesitate to declare an emergency out of pride or fear of consequence — and pay for the delay. Mitigate by treating a genuine doubt, a lost radio in IMC, or a deteriorating situation as a reason to declare: the system is there to help, and an early declaration preserves options.

## Skills — Teaching the Task

The ACS skill elements require the applicant to **select the frequencies, transmit with standard phraseology, acknowledge and comply, and analyze the common errors**:

- **AI.VI.A.S1 — Select and activate the appropriate frequencies:** the learner finds and sets the correct frequencies on the ground and in the air.
- **AI.VI.A.S2 — Transmit with standard phraseology:** the learner makes radio calls using the AIM/Pilot-Controller Glossary phraseology — the towered and CTAF formats, the phonetic alphabet, and the required readbacks.
- **AI.VI.A.S3 — Acknowledge and comply:** the learner acknowledges radio communications and complies with ATC instructions, or says "unable."
- **AI.VI.A.S4 — Analyze and correct the common errors:** the learner recognizes and corrects the common communication errors in a scenario.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

1. How do you determine the correct radio frequency, on the ground and in the air?

[[answer:
On the ground, use the Chart Supplement, the airport diagram, or the VFR sectional. In the air, the important frequencies are printed on the VFR sectional. Know the purpose of each: ATIS, ground, tower, approach/departure, center, Guard (121.5), FSS, and CTAF/UNICOM.
]]

2. What is the proper phraseology and format for a non-towered (CTAF) call?

[[answer:
Use the phonetic alphabet for call signs and read back what must be read back. The CTAF format is: [frequency name], [call sign], [position], [intentions], [frequency name again] — for example, "Apopka Traffic, Cessna 12345, five miles west at 1,500 feet, entering a left downwind for Runway 33, Apopka Traffic."
]]

3. What are the ATC light-gun signals and their meanings?

[[answer:
Steady green — in flight: cleared to land; on the ground: cleared for takeoff. Flashing green — in flight: return for landing; on the ground: cleared to taxi. Steady red — in flight: give way and continue circling; on the ground: stop. Flashing red — in flight: airport unsafe, do not land; on the ground: taxi clear of the runway in use. Flashing white — on the ground: return to the starting point. Alternating red and green — exercise extreme caution.
]]

4. What are the appropriate uses of the transponder?

[[answer:
Squawk the assigned code; use Mode C (altitude reporting) where required; squawk 1200 for VFR when not assigned; 7700 for an emergency; 7600 for lost communications; 7500 for hijack; and respond to "squawk ident" when requested. The transponder and ADS-B are required in the controlled airspace of Task G.
]]

5. What do you do if you lose radio communications?

[[answer:
Fly the airplane and navigate first, then troubleshoot the radio (volume, squelch, frequency, intercom, headset). If communications cannot be restored: squawk 7600, and call "in the blind" on 121.5 (Guard), which is always monitored. At a towered airport, approach the pattern carefully and watch for the tower's light-gun signals. Under IFR, follow § 91.185.
]]

6. What equipment issues can cause a loss of communication?

[[answer:
An electrical-system failure, incorrect radio or intercom settings, a failed headset or stuck mic, or a wrong frequency. Check the simple things first — volume, squelch, frequency, audio selector, and headset connection — before assuming a failure.
]]

7. What are the Runway Status Lights, and what do the REL and THL indicate?

[[answer:
Runway Status Lights are a fully automated system that warns when it is unsafe to enter, cross, take off from, or land on a runway, independent of the controllers. Runway Entrance Lights (REL) are red at a taxiway/runway intersection when it is unsafe to enter or cross; Takeoff Hold Lights (THL) are red ahead when it is unsafe to take off.
]]

8. What are the common errors in this Task, and how do you correct them?

[[answer:
Using the wrong frequency; improper procedure and phraseology (omitting the call sign, position, or intentions on a CTAF, or failing to read back at a towered airport); not acknowledging or complying with ATC; not understanding the light signals; and hesitating to declare an emergency. Correct them by confirming the frequency, using standard phraseology, reading back the required items, knowing the light signals, and declaring an emergency early.
]]

## Completion Standard

The learner explains how to obtain the frequencies, the proper phraseology and readbacks, the light-gun signals, the transponder use, the lost-communication procedures, the equipment issues, radar assistance, and the runway status lights; applies the recognize–assess–mitigate–verify process to the communication and emergency risks; and selects the frequencies, transmits with standard phraseology, acknowledges and complies with ATC, and analyzes and corrects the common errors.
