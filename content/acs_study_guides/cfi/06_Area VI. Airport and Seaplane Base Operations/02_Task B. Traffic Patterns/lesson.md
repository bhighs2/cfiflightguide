# Task B. Traffic Patterns

**Area VI - Airport and Seaplane Base Operations**

## Objective

To determine the applicant understands traffic patterns, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate appropriate skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Every VFR flight begins or ends in an airport traffic pattern. The pattern provides a predictable flow to the runway, but it only works when each pilot determines the published procedure, sees and avoids traffic, flies an accurate wind-corrected ground track, and manages spacing without rushing.

For the instructor applicant, this Task is more than drawing a rectangle. Teach the learner to arrive prepared, enter at the correct altitude, use the downwind leg as a deliberate planning point, make sound go-around decisions, and remain outside the airplane when traffic, radio, configuration, and wind compete for attention.

## Schedule / Equipment

- **Ground instruction:** 30 minutes.
- **Flight instruction:** 0.8-1.2 hour, adjusted for airport activity and conditions.
- Current airport diagram, Chart Supplement, NOTAMs, weather/ATIS/ASOS/AWOS, sectional or EFB, and airplane POH/AFM.
- Whiteboard or airport-pattern visual, aircraft checklist, and kneeboard.

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** Review the airport information before the flight; brief runway, pattern altitude, direction, entry, wind, and traffic; fly the assigned pattern while maintaining aircraft control and a continuous traffic scan; and explain GUMPS on downwind.

**Instructor:** Use the Binns-style sequence of pattern geometry, procedures, towered operations, nontowered operations, and common errors. Demonstrate an accurate pattern while explaining the outside picture, wind correction, spacing decisions, and an early go-around.

## Completion Standards

The learner explains and teaches towered and nontowered pattern operations, pattern selection, right-of-way, automated information, and common errors; identifies and manages the stated risks; and demonstrates and explains the ACS skills while maintaining the applicable Private/Commercial pilot pattern standards: pattern altitude **+/-100 feet** and appropriate airspeed **+/-10 knots**.

### Purpose

The traffic pattern creates a predictable flow of aircraft to and from the runway.

The pattern only works when pilots:

- Use the correct runway and traffic direction.
- Enter and depart predictably.
- Maintain the published traffic pattern altitude.
- Correct for wind to maintain the proper ground track.
- Maintain spacing and see-and-avoid.
- Follow ATC instructions or recommended nontowered procedures.
- Go around rather than force an unsafe sequence.

The instructor should teach the learner to understand the pattern as a **ground track around the runway**, not merely a set of headings.

### Pattern Preparation

Before arrival or departure, determine:

- Runway in use.
- Traffic pattern direction.
- Traffic pattern altitude.
- Wind.
- Airport elevation.
- Tower status.
- CTAF or tower frequency.
- NOTAMs.
- Special procedures.
- Other traffic.

Use:

- Chart Supplement.
- Airport diagram.
- Sectional or EFB.
- ATIS, ASOS, or AWOS.
- NOTAMs.
- Visual wind indicators.

Do not arrive at the airport and begin deciding these items while already entering the traffic flow.

### Traffic Pattern Altitude

Use the **published traffic pattern altitude** when one is available.

Traffic pattern altitude is normally expressed in:

**feet AGL**

but the altimeter displays:

**feet MSL.**

Convert the published pattern height to the indicated altitude by adding airport elevation.

A common FAA recommendation for light airplanes is approximately:

**1,000 feet AGL**

unless otherwise established.

Published airport information controls.

### Pattern Direction

Under **14 CFR §91.126(b)(1)**, when approaching to land at an airport without an operating control tower in Class G airspace, turns are made to the **left** unless visual markings indicate that turns should be made to the right.

Similar airport-vicinity operating rules apply in controlled airspace under the applicable Part 91 sections.

Pattern direction may also be established by:

- Chart Supplement.
- Segmented circle.
- Traffic-pattern indicators.
- ATC instructions.

Never assume every runway uses left traffic.

### Pattern Legs

A standard rectangular traffic pattern consists of:

- Departure.
- Crosswind.
- Downwind.
- Base.
- Final.

An **upwind leg** is a separate leg flown parallel to the landing runway in the direction of landing, generally on the opposite side of the runway from downwind.

The takeoff path immediately after liftoff is the **departure leg**.

### Departure Leg

The departure leg begins after takeoff and continues along the extended runway centerline.

Maintain:

- Runway alignment.
- Appropriate climb speed.
- Traffic awareness.
- Wind correction as needed.

If remaining in the traffic pattern:

- Continue beyond the departure end.
- Turn crosswind when clear and appropriate.
- Do not make an early turn that creates a conflict with traffic or the runway environment.

If departing the pattern:

- Follow the published or ATC-directed procedure.
- At a nontowered airport, normally continue straight out or depart with an appropriate 45-degree turn in the direction of the pattern after reaching a safe altitude.

### Crosswind Leg

The crosswind leg is approximately perpendicular to the runway.

Its purpose is to transition from the departure leg to downwind.

Correct for wind so the **ground track** remains approximately perpendicular to the runway.

The crosswind turn should be made:

- After the airplane is safely beyond the departure end.
- When traffic permits.
- Without rushing to reach downwind.

### Downwind Leg

The downwind leg is flown:

- Parallel to the runway.
- Opposite the direction of landing.
- At traffic pattern altitude until the planned descent point.

Downwind is the primary planning leg.

Use it to:

- Identify traffic.
- Establish spacing.
- Complete the before-landing checklist.
- Configure according to the POH and local training procedure.
- Decide where the base turn should occur.

Maintain a ground track parallel to the runway.

Wind may require a crab toward or away from the runway.

### Base Leg

The base leg is approximately perpendicular to the runway.

The base turn is adjusted for:

- Wind.
- Traffic.
- Spacing.
- Aircraft performance.

A strong tailwind on base can rapidly carry the airplane through final.

Recognize that setup early.

Do not attempt to save an overshoot with:

- Excessive bank.
- Inside rudder.
- A skidding turn.

If the turn to final is no longer working:

**go around.**

### Final Approach

Final is flown:

- Aligned with the runway.
- At the appropriate airspeed.
- On a stable descent path.
- With required landing configuration complete.

Correct for crosswind while maintaining the runway centerline.

The approach should be stabilized early enough that the pilot is not making large corrections close to the ground.

### Wind Correction

The goal is a rectangular **ground track**, not a rectangular series of headings.

Wind affects each leg differently.

On:

- **Crosswind:** crab to remain perpendicular to the runway.
- **Downwind:** crab to remain parallel.
- **Base:** compensate for drift toward or away from final.
- **Final:** maintain the extended centerline.

The stronger the wind:

the greater the difference between aircraft heading and ground track.

### Pattern Spacing

Maintain enough spacing that the aircraft ahead can:

- Complete its landing.
- Clear the runway.
- Go around if necessary.

Do not compress the pattern simply because another airplane is behind you.

Spacing may be increased by:

- Extending downwind.
- Reducing speed when appropriate.
- Going around.
- Leaving and reentering the pattern if necessary.

At a towered airport:

follow ATC instructions.

At a nontowered airport:

coordinate visually and by CTAF while maintaining see-and-avoid.

### Right-of-Way — 14 CFR §91.113

Right-of-way rules apply in the pattern.

Important principles include:

- Aircraft in distress have priority.
- When converging at approximately the same altitude, the aircraft to the other's right has right-of-way, subject to category priorities.
- Head-on aircraft alter course to the right.
- An overtaking aircraft keeps clear.
- Aircraft on final approach to land or while landing have right-of-way over other aircraft in flight or operating on the surface.

When two or more aircraft are approaching to land:

the aircraft at the lower altitude generally has right-of-way.

But:

**a pilot may not use the lower-aircraft rule to cut in front of another aircraft on final.**

Right-of-way never justifies creating a collision hazard.

### See and Avoid

Even when ATC is providing sequencing:

**the pilot remains responsible for seeing and avoiding other aircraft when operating VFR.**

Use:

- Continuous visual scanning.
- Radio calls.
- ADS-B traffic when available.
- ATC traffic advisories when available.

These aids support the visual scan.

They do not replace it.

### Towered Airport Operations

At a towered airport:

- Obtain ATIS when available.
- Establish the required communications.
- Follow the assigned runway and pattern entry.
- Comply with ATC sequencing and instructions.
- Read back required instructions.
- Ask when an instruction is unclear.

ATC may direct the pilot to:

- Enter a specific leg.
- Extend downwind.
- Make short approach.
- Follow traffic.
- Make a 360.
- Go around.

Do not accept or attempt an instruction that cannot be performed safely.

### Nontowered Airport Operations

At a nontowered airport:

- Review airport information before arrival.
- Monitor and transmit on CTAF.
- Observe wind and traffic.
- Determine the pattern in use.
- Enter predictably.
- Conform to traffic already established.

Radio communication improves awareness.

It does not create separation.

Aircraft without radios may also be operating.

### Recommended Nontowered Entry

FAA guidance recommends entering the pattern at:

**traffic pattern altitude**

and avoiding descent into established traffic.

When appropriate, the normal entry is:

**45 degrees to the midfield downwind.**

Before entering:

- Observe traffic.
- Determine the runway and pattern direction.
- Establish pattern altitude.
- Adjust speed to blend with traffic.

If arriving from the opposite side:

- Cross the airport only when safe and appropriate.
- Remain well above pattern traffic while observing the airport.
- Maneuver clear of the pattern.
- Descend outside the pattern.
- Join the normal 45-degree downwind entry at pattern altitude.

Do not descend through the traffic pattern.

### Straight-In Approaches at Nontowered Airports

A straight-in approach is not automatically prohibited.

But it should not disrupt aircraft already flying the established pattern.

When pattern traffic exists:

- Monitor the CTAF.
- Identify the established sequence.
- Do not force aircraft in the pattern to accommodate a conflicting straight-in.

Predictability and collision avoidance are more important than saving time.

### Automated Weather and Airport Information

Use available:

- ATIS.
- ASOS.
- AWOS.
- Wind information.
- Airport remarks.
- NOTAMs.

Use the information to determine:

- Runway.
- Wind direction and velocity.
- Gusts.
- Crosswind.
- Pattern direction.
- Airport conditions.

Then verify what is actually happening outside.

Automated information is advisory information.

The wind sock, traffic, runway condition, and actual weather still matter.

### Wind Indicators

Visual indicators may include:

- Windsock.
- Wind cone.
- Wind tee.
- Tetrahedron.
- Segmented circle.

A tetrahedron primarily indicates:

**landing direction**

and should not be relied upon as the sole wind indicator.

### Runway Orientation

Remain oriented to the runway throughout the pattern.

Useful checks include:

- Departure leg approximately runway heading.
- Downwind approximately reciprocal runway heading in no wind.
- Base approximately 90 degrees to the runway in no wind.
- Final aligned with runway heading.

These are references only.

Wind correction changes the actual heading required.

### Airport Signs, Markings, and Lighting

The ACS requires the applicant to identify and interpret the airport environment.

Before entering the pattern, identify:

- Correct runway.
- Parallel-runway configuration.
- Runway markings.
- Lighting.
- Taxiways.
- Hot Spots.

On final:

**verify the landing surface.**

Do not line up with:

- Taxiway.
- Wrong parallel runway.
- Road.
- Wrong airport.

### Collision Hazards

Collision risk is highest where aircraft converge.

Important locations include:

- Pattern entry.
- Crosswind-to-downwind.
- Base-to-final.
- Final approach.
- Go-around path.

Clear before every turn.

Do not assume traffic is where it last reported.

If traffic position is uncertain:

take the conservative action.

### Distractions and Task Prioritization

The traffic pattern combines:

- Aircraft control.
- Radio calls.
- Traffic scan.
- Checklists.
- Configuration changes.
- Wind correction.
- Spacing decisions.

At low altitude:

**aircraft control and traffic avoidance come first.**

If a radio call or checklist begins to degrade flying:

return attention to the airplane.

Complete landing checks early enough that base and final remain primarily:

- Outside.
- Stable.
- Predictable.

### Wake Turbulence

Wake vortices are produced whenever an airplane is generating lift.

The strongest vortices are generally produced by an aircraft that is:

- Heavy.
- Clean.
- Slow.

Vortices:

- Trail behind the aircraft.
- Sink below the generating aircraft.
- Drift with the wind.
- Dissipate over time.

### Wake Avoidance — Landing

When landing behind a larger aircraft on the same runway:

- Stay at or above its approach path.
- Land beyond its touchdown point.

When landing behind a departing larger aircraft:

- Land before its rotation point when safe and practical.

Consider wind direction because wake can drift:

- Across the runway.
- Onto a parallel runway.
- Into the final approach path.

### Wake Avoidance — Departure

When departing behind a larger aircraft:

- Rotate before its rotation point.
- Remain above its climb path.
- Turn clear of the wake when appropriate.

Do not accept a departure interval or intersection departure that creates an unacceptable wake hazard.

### Windshear

Windshear is a rapid change in:

- Wind direction.
- Wind speed.
- Or both.

Low-level windshear is especially hazardous because it can cause abrupt changes in:

- Airspeed.
- Lift.
- Flightpath.

Possible sources include:

- Thunderstorms.
- Microbursts.
- Fronts.
- Temperature inversions.
- Strong winds aloft.

### Microbursts

A microburst can produce:

- Strong downdrafts.
- Rapid wind-direction changes.
- Severe performance loss.

A typical encounter may begin with:

- Increasing headwind and performance.

Then transition to:

- Downdraft.

Then:

- Increasing tailwind and rapid performance loss.

At pattern altitude, there may be little room to recover.

Avoid known or suspected microburst activity.

### Windshear Response

If windshear makes the approach unstable:

**go around early.**

Use:

- Aircraft POH/AFM procedures.
- Maximum appropriate power.
- Appropriate pitch.
- Configuration specified by the manufacturer.

Do not continue an approach simply because the runway is close.

### Go-Around Decision

A go-around is appropriate when:

- Spacing becomes unsafe.
- Runway is occupied.
- Traffic position is uncertain.
- Base-to-final overshoot develops.
- Approach becomes unstable.
- Windshear is encountered or suspected.
- Wake turbulence cannot be avoided.
- ATC directs it.
- The pilot is uncomfortable with continuing.

The decision should be made early.

A go-around is a normal traffic-pattern maneuver.

### Common Errors

Common errors include:

- Entering at the wrong altitude.
- Entering against the established traffic flow.
- Descending into the pattern.
- Failing to comply with ATC instructions.
- Poor wind correction.
- Flying headings instead of maintaining the proper ground track.
- Inadequate spacing.
- Losing sight of traffic.
- Poor altitude control.
- Poor airspeed control.
- Turning base too early.
- Overshooting final.
- Skidding the base-to-final turn.
- Excessive bank close to the ground.
- Checklist fixation.
- Radio fixation.
- Continuing an unstable approach.
- Failing to account for wake turbulence.
- Continuing in windshear conditions.

## AI.VI.B.K1 - Towered and Nontowered Airport Operations

At a **towered airport**, obtain ATIS when available, establish the required communication, and comply with clearances and instructions. ATC may assign an entry, extend a leg, sequence traffic, approve a straight-in, or direct a go-around. Read back runway, hold-short, and other required instructions accurately; if uncertain, ask before acting.

At a **nontowered airport**, pilots determine the runway and conform to the traffic pattern in use. Review the Chart Supplement, NOTAMs, and local procedures before flight; monitor and transmit on CTAF; use the wind and traffic indicators; and scan continuously. A radio call improves shared awareness but does not provide separation or replace see-and-avoid.

Unless the Chart Supplement, visual indicators, Part 93 procedures, or ATC specifies otherwise, airplane pattern turns are **left**. The Chart Supplement and airport remarks control local direction, altitude, noise-abatement, and other procedures.

## AI.VI.B.K2 - Traffic Pattern Selection for Current Conditions

The traffic pattern is a **rectangular ground track** built around the landing runway: departure/upwind, crosswind, downwind, base, and final. Choose the runway and pattern by integrating published information, wind, traffic already established, runway condition, aircraft capability, and ATC direction when applicable.

**Traffic pattern altitude (TPA)** is published when applicable and must be used. A common convention is **1,000 feet AGL for light aircraft** and **1,500 feet AGL for multiengine, jet, or heavy aircraft**, unless the airport publishes a different altitude or ATC directs otherwise. Explain the distinction between **AGL** and indicated altitude: convert the published AGL height using field elevation, then verify with the airport's published information.

### Nontowered Pattern Entry - FAA Recommended Practice

AC 90-66C emphasizes entering at **traffic pattern altitude**, observing aircraft already in the pattern, and conforming to the established flow. The FAA regulates pattern flow, not a single required entry method, but recommends the standard pattern to reduce midair risk.

- Arrive with the airport information, CTAF, runway, direction, and TPA already identified.
- Remain clear of the traffic flow while descending or maneuvering to join it. **Do not descend into the pattern.**
- The normal entry is a **45-degree entry to downwind at midfield**, at TPA, when it fits the traffic picture.
- From the opposite side, cross over midfield **well above TPA**, observe wind and traffic, then maneuver outside the pattern to descend and join the 45-degree downwind entry at TPA.
- When other aircraft are established in the pattern, avoid a straight-in approach that conflicts with base-to-final traffic. A straight-in is appropriate only when it does not create a conflict or when ATC authorizes/directs it.
- Never force an entry. Extend, depart the area, or make another circuit to regain spacing.

After takeoff or a go-around, continue straight ahead beyond the departure end before turning. If remaining in the pattern, turn crosswind within 300 feet of TPA when clear and appropriate. If departing, continue straight out or make the applicable 45-degree exit after reaching TPA.

## AI.VI.B.K3 - Right-of-Way Rules

Right-of-way rules support safety; they never authorize a pilot to create a conflict. Under § 91.113, when weather permits, every pilot must maintain vigilance to **see and avoid** other aircraft.

- An aircraft in distress has priority over all other traffic.
- When aircraft converge at approximately the same altitude, give way to the aircraft on the right, subject to category priorities.
- In a head-on situation, both aircraft alter course to the right.
- The overtaking aircraft keeps clear and normally passes to the right.
- Aircraft on final approach to land or while landing have right-of-way over other aircraft in flight or on the surface. An aircraft at lower altitude has priority, but may not use that rule to cut in front of another aircraft on final or overtake it.

In the pattern, apply the rule early: extend downwind, slow only when safe and appropriate, make a 360 only when safely clear and suitable, or go around. Never steepen, skid, dive, or rush base-to-final to preserve a place in line.

## AI.VI.B.K4 - Use of Automated Weather and Airport Information

Before arrival, use all available information: ATIS at towered airports; AWOS/ASOS, CTAF/UNICOM, airport remarks, NOTAMs, and the Chart Supplement at nontowered airports. Automated information supports the decision; it does not replace looking at the windsock, runway, traffic, and actual conditions.

Use the information to brief:

- **Wind:** direction, velocity, gusts, crosswind/tailwind implications, and likely runway.
- **Runway and airport condition:** closures, NOTAMs, lighting, construction, obstacles, and hot spots.
- **Pattern:** TPA, left/right traffic, special procedures, and noise-abatement restrictions.
- **Traffic and services:** CTAF/UNICOM, tower status/hours, and known airport activity.

Wind indicators may include a windsock, wind cone, wind tee, or tetrahedron. A tetrahedron may indicate landing direction but should not be used as a wind indicator; at a towered airport, tower instructions supersede it.

## AI.VI.B.K5 - Common Errors Related to This Task

### Failure to Comply With Pattern Instructions, Procedures, or Rules

**Error:** Entering at the wrong altitude/direction, missing an ATC instruction, or relying on habit instead of current airport information.

**Correction:** Brief the current plan before arrival; write or repeat the critical clearance; verify with the diagram/Chart Supplement; and ask ATC or another facility when uncertain.

### Improper Correction for Wind Drift

**Error:** Flying headings that look rectangular on the panel while the ground track drifts wide, tight, or through final.

**Correction:** Look outside at runway relationship and ground references. Adjust crab on each leg; do not wait until the base-to-final turn to discover a strong crosswind.

### Inadequate Spacing From Other Traffic

**Error:** Continuing to close on the traffic ahead, turning base while another aircraft is on final, or compressing the pattern to avoid a go-around.

**Correction:** Plan spacing on downwind, extend before a conflict develops, maintain visual contact, and go around when the landing sequence no longer works.

### Poor Altitude or Airspeed Control

**Error:** Drifting from TPA, descending early, or allowing airspeed to vary while attention is consumed by radio, checklist, or traffic.

**Correction:** Use a disciplined outside/instrument cross-check. Establish and hold TPA until the planned descent point; complete GUMPS on downwind, not while rushing through base or final.

## Risk Management

### AI.VI.B.R1 - Collision Hazards

The highest collision exposure is around the runway, where aircraft converge from different directions and visual blind spots grow during turns. Radio calls are not separation. Teach a continuous scan, clearing before turns, visual acquisition of reported traffic, recognition of runway conflicts, and a conservative go-around when unsure.

### AI.VI.B.R2 - Distractions, Task Prioritization, Loss of Situational Awareness, or Disorientation

The pattern combines aircraft control, navigation, communication, traffic, configuration, and checklist work at low altitude. Prioritize **aviate, navigate, communicate**. If the radio call, checklist, or traffic search cannot be completed without degrading control, fly the airplane first and resolve the task at a safer time.

On downwind, use the calmer segment to identify traffic, establish spacing, complete **GUMPS**, set the planned configuration, and make the descent plan. Do not let GUMPS become head-down checklist fixation; verify one item at a time while maintaining the traffic scan and aircraft control.

### AI.VI.B.R3 - Windshear and Wake Turbulence

Reported or observed windshear, gusts, and rapidly changing winds can make the intended path or airspeed unreliable. Recognize sudden airspeed, pitch, or vertical-path changes and use an early go-around when the approach is no longer stable.

Wake vortices from larger/heavier aircraft can sink and drift with the wind. Increase spacing, remain above the preceding aircraft's approach path when feasible, and land beyond its touchdown point. Do not accept a close sequence merely because the runway appears open.

## Skills

### AI.VI.B.S1 - Identify and Interpret Airport/Seaplane Base Surfaces, Markings, Signs, and Lighting

Before flight, identify the runway in use, runway orientation, taxi route, hold-short points, lighting, and known hot spots from the airport diagram and current information. In the air, verify the landing surface before final; do not line up with a taxiway, road, parallel runway, or wrong airport.

### AI.VI.B.S2 - Comply With Recommended Traffic Pattern Procedures

Use the published direction, TPA, and local procedures. At nontowered airports, make the arrival and entry compatible with established traffic; at towered airports, comply with the assigned sequence and entry. Maintain the rectangular pattern unless traffic, ATC, or safety requires a change.

### AI.VI.B.S3 - Correct for Wind Drift to Maintain Proper Ground Track

Wind correction is continuous. Apply crab as needed on crosswind, downwind, base, and final so the ground track remains the intended rectangle. A runway-side crosswind on downwind can carry the airplane toward the runway and shorten base; recognize the setup early and avoid the temptation to overshoot or skid toward final.

### AI.VI.B.S4 - Maintain Orientation With the Runway/Landing Area in Use

Know the runway location, heading, and traffic relationship at every leg. Use the airport, runway, and ground references, not a vague sense of direction. If orientation is lost, climb or depart the pattern as appropriate, re-establish the airport picture, and reenter only when a safe plan exists.

### AI.VI.B.S5 - Maintain Pattern Altitude and Appropriate Airspeed

Hold the published TPA to **+/-100 feet** and the appropriate airspeed to **+/-10 knots**. Remain at TPA until at least abeam the approach end of the landing runway, then make the planned descent. Coordinate airspeed changes with configuration, wind, traffic spacing, and the POH/AFM.

### AI.VI.B.S6 - Maintain Situational Awareness and Proper Spacing

Build spacing early. On downwind, identify the preceding aircraft and judge whether its position, speed, and likely turn will permit a safe base and final. Extend downwind or go around rather than force a close sequence. Maintain visual contact with known traffic; if it is lost, do not turn toward its last known position.

### AI.VI.B.S7 - Analyze and Correct Common Errors

For every correction, first stabilize aircraft control and regain the outside picture. Then identify the source: wrong airport information, missed clearance, weak wind correction, late spacing decision, poor scan, or task saturation. State the correction aloud and use the next pattern to demonstrate it.

# Fly

## Setup

- Obtain airport weather, runway, NOTAMs, TPA, traffic direction, CTAF/ATIS, and current tower status.
- Brief runway, departure plan, entry/reentry plan, crosswind/tailwind implications, wake-turbulence exposure, and go-around plan.
- Complete taxi and before-takeoff procedures. Verify the runway and clear the final approach/departure path before entering.
- Select an altitude and area suitable for any needed reset. Do not use a crowded pattern as the place to troubleshoot confusion.

## Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate a complete pattern while explaining the outside picture:

1. **Upwind / crosswind:** Maintain runway alignment, climb to **300 feet below TPA**, clear, then turn crosswind. Crab for the intended ground track rather than accepting wind drift.
2. **Downwind / ready:** Establish a wind-corrected track parallel to the runway at TPA, normally 1/2 to 1 mile away unless traffic, aircraft performance, or local procedure calls for a different distance. When abeam midfield at a towered airport, report: **"Skyhawk 826SW, left downwind Runway [number]."** Listen for an instruction to extend downwind or make a 360; do not attempt a maneuver that is unclear.
3. **Abeam the landing point:** In this C172S/local training flow, reduce to **1,600 RPM**, select **10 degrees flaps**, pitch for **80 knots**, trim, and establish approximately a **500 FPM** descent toward roughly **1,000 feet AGL**. Complete GUMPS while continuing to scan and manage spacing.
4. **Base:** At approximately **45 degrees** from the landing point, turn base when spacing permits. Correct for drift, remain coordinated, select **20 degrees flaps**, hold the pitch picture, and allow airspeed to settle near **70-75 knots**. The base turn normally begins near **1,000 feet AGL** in this local flow.
5. **Final:** Turn final with adequate spacing and a coordinated turn, normally near **500 feet AGL** in this local flow. Select **30 degrees flaps**, pitch and trim for **65 knots**, and maintain the landing sight picture. On final, use **pitch for airspeed, power for altitude/path, ailerons for centerline tracking, and rudder for longitudinal alignment**. Crab as needed for ground track.

These configuration, power, airspeed, and approximate-altitude cues are a **Flight Guide C172S/local training flow**. The applicable POH/AFM, airport procedure, weather, traffic, and instructor direction control whenever they differ.

## Learner Practice

Have the learner brief each circuit before takeoff and narrate the changes observed from wind and traffic. Use a quiet pattern first, then introduce realistic ATC sequencing or nontowered traffic decisions as workload permits.

Require the learner to complete GUMPS **on downwind** on every landing pattern. Ask for the reason behind each item, especially in a fixed-gear/fixed-pitch airplane, so the flow remains a positive verification rather than an empty chant.

## Instructor Demonstration Technique

Use a progressive verbal demonstration:

- **First circuit:** instructor flies and narrates runway orientation, wind correction, scan, TPA, GUMPS, spacing, and go-around triggers.
- **Second circuit:** learner flies; instructor prompts only before decision points.
- **Third circuit:** learner briefs and teaches the pattern; instructor introduces a changing wind, an extended downwind, or traffic on final.

At a nontowered airport, stop the simulation if an entry will conflict with existing traffic. Demonstrate that extending outside the pattern or going around is sound judgment, not failed pattern flying.

## Analyze and Correct Common Errors

### Entering Below or While Descending Through TPA

**Recognize:** Traffic is at the same altitude with an uncertain relative position, or the learner descends while trying to find the entry.

**Correct:** Remain clear of the pattern, climb or maneuver outside as needed, establish TPA, observe the flow, and make a normal entry. Do not descend into established traffic.

### Rushed Base-to-Final Turn

**Recognize:** The runway is overshot, bank increases, inside rudder is added, or traffic spacing is closing.

**Correct:** Maintain coordination, abandon the approach, and go around. Discuss the wind and spacing cue that should have prompted an earlier decision.

### GUMPS Delayed Until Base or Final

**Recognize:** The learner is head-down or discovers an incomplete configuration as the workload peaks.

**Correct:** Complete the landing flow on downwind. If it is missed, preserve aircraft control and traffic scan first; do not force a checklist completion at the expense of a stable approach.

### Letting Radio Work Replace Looking

**Recognize:** The learner transmits accurately but fails to identify traffic or clears inadequately before turns.

**Correct:** Make the radio call, then return immediately to a deliberate outside scan. Require visual identification or a conservative spacing action when traffic position remains uncertain.

## Completion Standards

The applicant demonstrates and explains pattern operations that comply with the recommended/published procedure, maintain runway orientation and a wind-corrected ground track, hold TPA within +/-100 feet and appropriate airspeed within +/-10 knots, use GUMPS on downwind, maintain traffic awareness and spacing, and correct errors before they become hazards.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. How is a nontowered traffic-pattern direction determined?

[[answer:
Use the Chart Supplement, airport remarks, sectional/right-pattern notation, and segmented-circle/traffic indicators. Unless otherwise indicated, airplane pattern turns are left. Also observe and conform to traffic already established in the pattern.
]]

### 2. Describe a recommended nontowered arrival and entry.

[[answer:
Arrive with the runway, TPA, direction, CTAF, wind, and traffic picture identified. Remain clear of traffic while maneuvering to TPA, then normally join downwind at midfield on a 45-degree entry when it fits the flow. AC 90-66C recommends entry at TPA and avoiding a straight-in that conflicts with traffic already in the pattern.
]]

### 3. What is the typical TPA for light aircraft, and what may be different for larger aircraft?

[[answer:
The usual recommended TPA is 1,000 feet AGL for light aircraft unless otherwise established. Published airport information controls. Multiengine, jet, and heavy aircraft commonly use 1,500 feet AGL, helping separate operations with different speeds and performance.
]]

### 4. Why should GUMPS be emphasized on downwind?

[[answer:
Downwind provides a planned point with time and altitude to verify landing configuration before workload increases on base and final. The pilot must still fly the airplane and scan outside; GUMPS is a deliberate verification, not head-down checklist fixation.
]]

### 5. What right-of-way rules matter most in the traffic pattern?

[[answer:
Maintain see-and-avoid at all times. Aircraft on final or landing generally have priority, and the lower aircraft has priority when multiple aircraft approach to land, but no pilot may exploit those rules to cut in front of or overtake traffic on final. When a conflict is developing, give way, extend, or go around.
]]

### 6. How does wind correction change a pattern?

[[answer:
The pilot crabs as necessary on every leg to maintain the desired ground track. Wind can widen or tighten the pattern and strongly affect base-to-final geometry. A runway-side crosswind can carry the airplane inward and set up an overshoot if the pilot does not recognize it early.
]]

### 7. A learner loses sight of traffic reported ahead on final while turning base. What do you teach?

[[answer:
Do not turn toward an uncertain conflict. Maintain aircraft control, continue scanning, and use a conservative action such as extending downwind, going around, or departing/reentering as appropriate. Radio is useful, but visual separation and safe spacing remain the pilot's responsibility.
]]

### 8. How do wake turbulence and windshear change the pattern plan?

[[answer:
Increase spacing and adjust the flightpath to avoid wake vortices from larger/heavier aircraft, considering their sink and wind drift. For windshear, recognize changing performance or path early; discontinue an unstable approach with a go-around rather than trying to force it to landing.
]]

### 9. What are the primary common errors in this Task, and how do you correct them?

[[answer:
Key errors are noncompliance with instructions or published procedures, poor wind correction, inadequate traffic spacing, and poor altitude or airspeed control. Correct them through a thorough arrival brief, continuous outside scan, early spacing decisions, wind-corrected ground references, GUMPS on downwind, and an early go-around when the pattern no longer supports a stable final.
]]
