# Task C. Weather Information

**Area III — Preflight Preparation**

## Objective

Determine that the applicant understands weather information, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate the required skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Weather is one of the most hazardous aspects of flying, especially for the VFR-only pilot. The CFI must be able to obtain a thorough weather briefing, read the reports and forecasts, recognize the hazards, and correlate the weather to a sound go/no-go decision — and teach a learner to do the same against their own proficiency and the aircraft's capability.

This is a ground-focused Task. The ACS skill elements require the applicant to **obtain an adequate briefing, analyze at least three weather conditions, and correlate the weather to a go/no-go decision** for a scenario.

## Schedule / Equipment

- Ground: 30–45 minutes
- Student Q&A: 10 minutes
- Sample aviation weather products
- Tablet / EFB software (optional)
- Whiteboard / markers (optional)

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** explain the types of weather briefings and how to obtain one; decode METARs, TAFs, and winds aloft; describe the weather charts and the in-flight weather sources; recognize the common weather hazards; and state personal weather minimums — then obtain a briefing, analyze hazards, and make a go/no-go decision for a scenario.

**Instructor:** deliver the ground lesson, request a standard briefing from FSS, have the learner decode actual reports, and pose a weather and flight-planning scenario for the go/no-go decision.

## Completion Standards

The learner can explain and apply:

- The **types of weather briefings** and how to obtain one.
- **METARs, TAFs, and winds aloft** — and how to decode them.
- The **weather charts** available and the **in-flight weather sources**.
- The common **weather hazards** — wind shear, thunderstorms, and icing among them.
- **Personal weather minimums** and the factors in a weather go/no-go decision.
- For a scenario, the ability to **obtain an adequate briefing, analyze at least three hazards, and make a go/no-go decision**.

## ACS Elements

### Knowledge

- **AI.III.C.K1** — Sources of weather data (e.g., National Weather Service, Flight Service) for flight planning purposes.
- **AI.III.C.K2** — Acceptable weather products and resources required for preflight planning, current and forecast weather for departure, en route, and arrival, such as: **K2a** airport observations (METAR and SPECI) and pilot observations (PIREP); **K2b** Surface Analysis Chart, Ceiling and Visibility Chart (CVA); **K2c** Terminal Aerodrome Forecasts (TAF); **K2d** Graphical Forecasts for Aviation (GFA); **K2e** Wind and Temperature Aloft Forecast (FB); **K2f** Convective Outlook (AC); **K2g** Inflight Aviation Weather Advisories including AIRMET, SIGMET, and Convective SIGMET; **K2h** area and synoptic charts as appropriate.
- **AI.III.C.K3** — Meteorology applicable to departure, en route, alternate, and destination under VFR in VMC, including expected climate and hazardous conditions such as: **K3a** atmospheric composition and stability; **K3b** wind (windshear, mountain wave, etc.); **K3c** temperature and heat exchange; **K3d** moisture/precipitation; **K3e** weather system formation (air masses and fronts); **K3f** clouds; **K3g** turbulence; **K3h** thunderstorms and microbursts; **K3i** icing and freezing level information; **K3j** fog/mist; **K3k** frost; **K3l** obstructions to visibility (smoke, haze, volcanic ash).
- **AI.III.C.K4** — Flight deck instrument displays of digital weather and aeronautical information.

### Risk Management

- **AI.III.C.R1** — Making the go/no-go and continue/divert decisions, including: **R1a** circumstances that would make diversion prudent; **R1b** personal weather minimums; **R1c** hazardous weather conditions, including known or forecast icing or turbulence aloft.
- **AI.III.C.R2** — Use and limitations of: **R2a** installed onboard weather equipment; **R2b** aviation weather reports and forecasts; **R2c** inflight weather resources.

### Skills

- **AI.III.C.S1** — Use available aviation weather resources to obtain an adequate weather briefing.
- **AI.III.C.S2** — Analyze the implications of at least three of the conditions listed in K3a through K3l, using actual weather or weather conditions provided by the evaluator.
- **AI.III.C.S3** — Correlate weather information to make a go/no-go decision.

# Teach

Teach weather as a decision, not a report: gather the observation and the forecast, recognize the hazard, and correlate it against the pilot's proficiency and the aircraft's capability to a go/no-go decision. The Overview maps the ACS knowledge, risk, and skill elements; the sections below teach the material once, in the order an instructor would build a weather picture.

## AI.III.C.K1 — Sources and the Weather Briefing

Before every flight, gather all information vital to the flight — including a weather briefing from a **Flight Service Station (FSS)** specialist or the **National Weather Service**. To give an appropriate briefing, the specialist needs to know which of the **three types** is needed, plus the VFR/IFR status, aircraft identification and type, departure point, ETD, altitude, route, destination, and ETE.

- **Standard briefing** — the most complete picture, obtained before departure during flight planning. In sequence: **adverse conditions; VFR-flight-not-recommended advisory; synopsis; current conditions; en route forecast; destination forecast; forecast winds and temperatures aloft; NOTAMs; ATC delays; and other information** (frequencies for the flight plan and EFAS).
- **Abbreviated briefing** — a shortened update when departure is delayed or the previous briefing needs updating. Tell the briefer the time and source of the earlier briefing.
- **Outlook briefing** — for a departure **6 or more hours away**; limited forecast information for early planning. Follow up with a standard briefing near departure.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
"VFR flight not recommended" is an advisory, not a clearance. The decision is still the pilot's — but the briefer just said the weather is doubtful. Teach the learner to weigh that statement heavily, not to file around it.
]]

## AI.III.C.K2 — Weather Reports, Forecasts, Advisories, and Charts

### Observations (K2a)

- **METAR** — the **aviation routine weather report**, an observation of current surface weather in a standard format, issued hourly; a **SPECI** is issued between routine reports for rapidly changing weather. The METAR decodes in sequence: type of report, station identifier, date/time (UTC/Zulu), modifier (AUTO/COR), wind, visibility (SM), weather, sky condition, temperature/dew point, altimeter setting, and remarks.

**Decode example — METAR:** `METAR KGGG 161753Z AUTO 14021G26KT 3/4SM +TSRA BR BKN008 OVC012CB 18/17 A2970 RMK PRESFR` reads: routine METAR for Gregg County (KGGG) on the 16th at 1753Z, automated source; wind **140° at 21 knots gusting to 26**; visibility **¾ statute mile**; **thunderstorms with heavy rain and mist**; ceiling **broken at 800 feet, overcast at 1,200 feet with cumulonimbus**; **temperature 18 °C, dew point 17 °C**; altimeter **29.70 "Hg**; remarks — pressure falling rapidly. Note how much of a go/no-go picture one line carries.

- **ASOS / AWOS** — the **Automated Surface Observing System (ASOS)** and **Automated Weather Observing System (AWOS)** are automated stations that report the current conditions continuously and issue the METAR. They report the same elements — altimeter, wind, visibility, sky condition, temperature/dew point, density altitude, and precipitation — without a human observer. The decode card reads the same METAR format.

- **NEXRAD** — the weather radar mosaic shows precipitation intensity and coverage for the route. Know the coverage limits — terrain and distance create gaps where precipitation may not be depicted.

- **PIREP** — the pilot weather report: actual conditions in the air that no other source can provide — cloud bases and tops, wind shear, turbulence, and icing. Required items 1–5 (location, time, altitude, aircraft type) plus at least one weather phenomenon. When ceilings are below 5,000 feet or visibility at or below 5 miles, ATC solicits PIREPs — **file them**.

**Decode example — PIREP:** `UA/OV GGG 090025/TM 1450/FL 060/TP C182/SK 080 OVC/WX FV04SM RA/TA 05/WV 270030KT/TB LGT/RM HVY RAIN` reads: routine pilot report, 25 NM out on the Gregg County VOR 090° radial, at 1450Z, at **6,000 feet**, from a **Cessna 182**; sky **overcast at 8,000 feet**; flight visibility **4 SM in rain**; temperature **5 °C**; wind **270° at 30 knots**; **light turbulence**; remarks — heavy rain. A PIREP is the in-flight truth the forecasts cannot give.

### Charts (K2b) and Forecasts (K2c–K2f)

- **Surface Analysis Chart** — issued every 3 hours; shows fronts, pressure systems, temperatures, dew points, and wind. The **Ceiling and Visibility / weather-depiction** charts are derived from METARs and distinguish VFR/MVFR/IFR.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">

- **TAF (K2c)** — the **Terminal Aerodrome Forecast**, valid for 24 or 30 hours and issued four times daily. It uses the same format as the METAR for the expected conditions, with change groups: **FM** (a rapid, significant change), **TEMPO** (temporary fluctuation under 1 hour), and **PROB30** (a percentage probability of thunderstorms or precipitation).

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/TAF_Reading.png" alt="A decoded TAF example" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>A decoded TAF — the forecast window, with FM, TEMPO, and PROB30 change groups.</figcaption>
</figure>

**Decode example — TAF:** `TAF KPIR 111130Z 1112/1212 15012KT P6SM BKN090 TEMPO 1112/1114 5SM BR FM1500 16015G25KT P6SM SCT040 BKN250 FM120000 14012KT P6SM BKN080 OVC150 PROB30 1200/1204 3SM TSRA BKN030CB` reads: routine TAF for Pierre (KPIR) issued the 11th at 1130Z, **valid 1200Z on the 11th to 1200Z on the 12th**; wind 150° at 12 knots, visibility greater than 6 SM, broken at 9,000 feet; **TEMPO** 1200–1400Z, 5 SM in mist; **FM** 1500Z, wind 160° at 15 gusting 25 knots; **FM** 0000Z, wind 140° at 12 knots, broken 8,000, overcast 15,000; **PROB30** between 0000–0400Z, a 30 percent probability of 3 SM with thunderstorms and rain showers, broken cumulonimbus at 3,000 feet. Read the change groups as the windows that drive the go/no-go decision.

- **Graphical Forecasts for Aviation (GFA)** (K2d) — the graphical forecast tool for the route.
- **Wind and Temperature Aloft (FB)** (K2e) — forecast winds and temperatures at altitudes, issued twice daily, for altitude selection and performance. Temperatures above 24,000 feet are negative and reported without the minus sign.
- **Convective Outlook (AC)** (K2f) — the forecast for convective activity.

### Inflight advisories (K2g)

- **AIRMET (WA)** — issued every 6 hours with updates; potentially hazardous to **light aircraft**: moderate icing, moderate turbulence, sustained surface winds of 30 knots or more, widespread ceilings below 1,000 feet / visibility below 3 miles, and extensive mountain obscurement. Named **Sierra** (IFR/mountain obscuration), **Tango** (turbulence/wind/LLWS), and **Zulu** (icing/freezing levels).

**Decode example — AIRMET:** `BOSS WA 211945 — AIRMET SIERRA UPDT 3 FOR IFR AND MTN OBSCN VALID UNTIL 220200 — AIRMET IFR…ME NH VT MA CT RI NY NJ AND CSTL WTRS FROM CAR TO YSJ TO 150E ACK TO EWR TO YOW TO CAR — OCNL CIG BLW 010/VIS BLW 3SM PCPN/BR. CONDS CONT BYD 02Z THRU 08Z` reads: issued for the Boston area (BOSS) at 1945Z on the 21st; an **AIRMET Sierra**, the **third update**, for IFR conditions and mountain obscuration, valid until **0200Z on the 22nd**; covering the Boston-area states and coastal waters within a boundary defined by Caribou, Saint John, 150 NM east of Nantucket, Newark, and Ottawa; occasional **ceilings below 1,000 feet and visibility below 3 SM** in precipitation and mist, continuing beyond 02Z through 08Z. The named letter tells you the hazard class, the boundary tells you where, and the valid time tells you how long to plan around it.

- **SIGMET (WS)** — unscheduled, valid **4 hours** (6 for hurricanes); non-convective weather hazardous to **all aircraft**: severe icing, severe or extreme turbulence/CAT, dust or sand storms lowering visibility below 3 miles, and volcanic ash. Issued under an alphabetic identifier (November through Yankee); the first issuance is an Urgent SIGMET (UWS).

**Decode example — SIGMET:** `SIGMET ROME02 VALID UNTIL 100530 OR WA FROM SEA TO PDT TO EUG TO SEA OCNL SEV CAT BTN FL280 AND FL350` reads: SIGMET Romeo 2, the second issuance for this phenomenon, valid until 0530Z on the 10th, for Oregon and Washington in an area from Seattle to Portland to Eugene and back; occasional **severe clear-air turbulence between FL280 and FL350** due to the jet stream. A SIGMET is a no-go condition for most light aircraft in the affected area and altitude.

- **Convective SIGMET (WST)** — issued at 55 past the hour, valid **2 hours**, for the eastern (E), western (W), and central (C) United States; severe convective weather: surface winds over 50 knots, hail ¾ inch or larger, tornadoes, or embedded/lines of thunderstorms over 40 percent or more of a large region.

**Decode example — Convective SIGMET:** `CONVECTIVE SIGMET 20C VALID UNTIL 2055Z ND SD ... INTSFYG AREA SEV TS MOVG FROM 24045KT. TOPS ABV FL450. TORNADOES...HAIL TO 2 IN... WIND GUSTS TO 65KTS` reads: the 20th Convective SIGMET of the day for the **central** US, valid until 2055Z, for the Dakotas; an intensifying area of severe thunderstorms moving from 240° at 45 knots, tops above FL450, with tornadoes, 2-inch hail, and 65-knot gusts possible. This is an avoid-at-altitude, avoid-on-the-ground hazard.

## AI.III.C.K3 — Weather Concepts and Hazards

Teach the hazardous conditions the VFR pilot must recognize and respect (the S2 analysis draws on these):

- **Atmospheric stability** — a stable atmosphere resists vertical motion (smooth air, layered clouds, poor visibility); an unstable one produces turbulence, cumulus, and thunderstorms.
- **Wind, wind shear, and mountain wave** — sudden changes in wind speed or direction; dangerous at low altitude.
- **Thunderstorms and microbursts** — avoid by a wide margin; a microburst's downdraft can exceed the aircraft's climb capability.
- **Turbulence** — from convective activity, wind shear, and terrain.
- **Icing and freezing level** — structural icing degrades lift and adds weight; know the freezing level and escape it.
- **Fog, mist, frost, and visibility obstructions** (smoke, haze, volcanic ash) — reduced visibility and obscured references; frost on the wing destroys lift.
- **Temperature and heat exchange** — drives density altitude and the day's performance.

## AI.III.C.K4 — Flight Deck and In-Flight Weather

In-flight weather comes from several sources, each with limits:

- **ATC** — weather-avoidance assistance and AIRMET/SIGMET/Center Weather Advisories, but **ATC radar shows precipitation, not all hazardous weather** (light/moderate/heavy/extreme).
- **FSS** — communicate directly or via VOR.
- **Datalink weather (FIS-B over ADS-B / 978 MHz UAT)** and **flight-deck displays (EFD/MFD)** — continuous weather across the country, but with a **time delay** and an inability to show **developing** storms; the display is a strategic tool, not a way to thread between cells.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
Datalink and onboard weather are strategic, not tactical. The time delay means the cell on the screen has already moved. Use it to plan around weather, never to weave through it.
]]

## AI.III.C.R1, R2 — Risk Management

### R1 — The go/no-go and continue/divert decision

- **R1a — When diversion is prudent:** deteriorating weather, a closing destination, or conditions approaching the pilot's minimums.
- **R1b — Personal weather minimums:** a floor above the legal minimums, set by proficiency and experience — and honored.
- **R1c — Hazardous conditions:** known or forecast **icing or turbulence aloft** are go/no-go drivers for a VFR flight.

### R2 — Use and limitations of weather resources

- **R2a — Installed onboard weather equipment:** know what the equipment actually depicts and its delay.
- **R2b — Reports and forecasts:** observations are current but local; forecasts are broad but uncertain. Use both, and recognize their limits.
- **R2c — Inflight resources:** FSS, ATC, and datalink each have congestion, coverage, and delay limits.

### Common Errors and Corrections

- **Skipping the briefing or accepting an outlook as final:** get a standard briefing near departure.
- **Misreading the METAR or TAF:** decode each group in sequence; practice on real reports.
- **Treating a forecast as a promise:** verify with current observations and PIREPs, and update en route.
- **Using datalink weather tactically:** the delay makes it strategic only — plan around, never through.
- **No personal minimums:** set a floor above the legal minimums and honor it.

## Teaching the Task — AI.III.C.S1–S3

The ACS skill elements require the applicant to **obtain a briefing, analyze hazards, and decide**:

- **S1 — Obtain an adequate weather briefing:** have the learner request a standard briefing and gather the reports, forecasts, and charts for a route.
- **S2 — Analyze at least three conditions (K3a–K3l):** using actual or provided weather, the learner explains the implications of three hazards for the flight.
- **S3 — Correlate to a go/no-go decision:** the learner weighs the weather against proficiency and the aircraft and makes — and defends — a go/no-go decision.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

1. What are the three types of weather briefings, and when is each used?

[[answer:
Standard — the complete picture, obtained before departure during flight planning (adverse conditions, VFR-not-recommended, synopsis, current conditions, en route and destination forecasts, winds aloft, NOTAMs, ATC delays). Abbreviated — a shortened update when departure is delayed or to update a previous briefing. Outlook — for a departure 6 or more hours away, for early planning, followed by a standard briefing near departure.
]]

2. What is the difference between a METAR and a TAF?

[[answer:
A METAR is an observation of current surface weather, issued hourly (SPECI for rapidly changing conditions). A TAF is a forecast of expected conditions for a 24- or 30-hour window, issued four times daily. The METAR tells you what is; the TAF tells you what may be.
]]

3. What does a PIREP provide that no other source can?

[[answer:
Actual conditions in the air — cloud bases and tops, wind shear, turbulence, and icing — reported by a pilot. ATC solicits PIREPs when ceilings are below 5,000 feet or visibility is at or below 5 miles. Pilots should file them when encountering unexpected weather.
]]

4. What is the difference between an AIRMET, a SIGMET, and a Convective SIGMET?

[[answer:
AIRMET — issued every 6 hours, potentially hazardous to light aircraft (moderate icing/turbulence, sustained 30-knot surface winds, low ceilings/visibility, mountain obscuration); named Sierra (IFR/mountain), Tango (turbulence/wind/LLWS), and Zulu (icing/freezing). SIGMET — unscheduled, valid 4 hours, non-convective weather hazardous to all aircraft (severe icing, severe/extreme turbulence, dust/sand, volcanic ash). Convective SIGMET — valid 2 hours, severe convective weather (50-knot winds, ¾-inch hail, tornadoes, or widespread embedded thunderstorms).
]]

5. Name three hazardous weather conditions a VFR pilot must respect and the risk each poses.

[[answer:
Thunderstorms/microbursts — a downdraft can exceed climb capability; avoid by a wide margin. Icing — structural icing degrades lift and adds weight; know the freezing level. Wind shear — a sudden wind change, dangerous at low altitude. (Also: turbulence, fog and visibility obstructions, frost, and temperature effects on density altitude.)
]]

6. What is the limitation of datalink and onboard weather displays?

[[answer:
Datalink weather (FIS-B) and flight-deck displays have a time delay and cannot show developing storms — the cell on the screen has already moved. They are strategic planning tools, not tactical tools for threading between cells. ATC radar shows precipitation, not all hazardous weather.
]]

7. What makes diversion prudent, and what are personal weather minimums?

[[answer:
Diversion is prudent when weather deteriorates, the destination is closing, or conditions approach the pilot's minimums. Personal weather minimums are a floor above the legal minimums, set by proficiency and experience — and honored.
]]

8. How do you correlate weather to a go/no-go decision?

[[answer:
Gather the observation and the forecast, recognize the hazards (especially forecast icing or turbulence aloft for a VFR flight), weigh them against personal minimums and the aircraft's capability, and decide — go, delay, change the route or altitude, divert, or no-go. The decision belongs to the pilot and should favor safety over the schedule.
]]

## Completion Standard

The learner explains the types of weather briefings and how to obtain one; decodes METARs, TAFs, and winds aloft; describes the charts and in-flight weather sources and their limitations; recognizes the common weather hazards; states personal weather minimums; and for a scenario obtains an adequate briefing, analyzes at least three hazards, and correlates the weather to a sound go/no-go decision.
