# Task B. Airworthiness Requirements

**Area III — Preflight Preparation**

## Objective

Determine that the applicant understands airworthiness requirements, including aircraft certificates, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate the required skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Every pilot is responsible for ensuring the airplane is in an airworthy condition before flight — and an instructor must be able to determine it and teach it. This lesson covers the required instruments and equipment, the process for dealing with inoperative equipment, the required documents and inspections, airworthiness directives, special flight permits, and preventive maintenance.

This is a ground-focused Task. The ACS skill elements require the applicant to **locate airworthiness and registration information, determine the airplane is airworthy, and apply the procedures for inoperative equipment** in a scenario.

## Schedule / Equipment

- Ground: 15–30 minutes
- Student Q&A: 10 minutes
- FAR/AIM (current) — 14 CFR Parts 43 and 91
- Airplane logbooks (optional)
- Whiteboard / markers (optional)

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** explain the required day/night VFR equipment, the process for determining airworthiness with inoperative equipment, the required documents and inspections, the role of ADs, the special flight permit, and preventive maintenance — then demonstrate the airplane is airworthy for a scenario.

**Instructor:** deliver the ground lesson, have the learner locate each requirement in the regulations and the aircraft's paperwork, and require the learner to determine airworthiness and handle an inoperative-equipment scenario.

## Completion Standards

The learner can explain and apply:

- The **equipment and instruments required** for VFR day and night operations.
- The **process for determining airworthiness** when inoperative instruments or equipment are found.
- The purpose of a **special flight permit** and how to obtain one.
- The **required airworthiness documentation and inspections**.
- **Preventive and deferred maintenance**.
- The ability to **demonstrate the aircraft is airworthy** for a particular scenario.

# Teach

Teach airworthiness as a preflight decision the pilot owns: is the aircraft legal and safe for this flight? The answer combines the required equipment, the documents, the inspections, and — when something is inoperative — a defined process. The Overview maps the ACS knowledge, risk, and skill elements; the sections below teach the material once, in ACS knowledge-element order.

## AI.III.B.K1 — General Airworthiness Requirements and Compliance

### AI.III.B.K1a — Location and Expiration of Required Aircraft Certificates

The aircraft must carry its documents, and the airworthiness certificate must be displayed.

- **Airworthiness certificate — § 91.203(a)(1):** displayed at the cabin/cockpit entrance; **valid as long as the aircraft is maintained in an airworthy condition**.
- **Registration — § 91.203(a)(2):** valid for **7 years** from the month issued.
- **Operating limitations / POH — § 91.9(b):** the current weight and balance and the flight-manual supplements (for STCs or updated avionics) must be carried — this is the approved AFM data, not a prepared weight-and-balance for a specific flight.

### AI.III.B.K1b — Required Inspections and Aircraft Logbook Documentation

- **Annual inspection — § 91.409(a):** every **12 calendar months**.
- **100-hour inspection — § 91.409(b):** required when the aircraft **carries persons for hire** (or is used for instruction when a person is carried for hire); a **10-hour flyover allowance** applies to reach the inspection location.
- **Transponder test and inspection — § 91.413(a):** every **24 calendar months**.
- **ELT test and inspection — § 91.207(c)/(d):** every **12 months**, and the battery on **1 hour of cumulative use or 50 percent of useful life**.
- **Logbook documentation — § 91.417:** maintenance and inspection records are kept **1 year or until superseded**; **total time in service, AD compliance, and life-limited-item status transfer with the airplane**.

### AI.III.B.K1c — Airworthiness Directives and Special Airworthiness Information Bulletins

- **Airworthiness Directives — 14 CFR Part 39 / § 39.7:** compliance is **mandatory**. **One-time ADs** require a single inspection or action; **recurring ADs** repeat at a set interval — **with no flyover allowance**.
- **Special Airworthiness Information Bulletins (SAIBs)** are non-regulatory safety information that does not meet the AD threshold but should not be ignored.

### AI.III.B.K1d — Special Flight Permits — § 21.199

A **special flight permit** authorizes a flight that would otherwise not meet airworthiness requirements — for example, **ferrying an aircraft with an inoperative item** to a repair location. Apply to the **FSDO**, stating the **purpose, itinerary, crew, and the unairworthy items**; the permit carries the conditions under which the flight may be made.

## AI.III.B.K2 — Pilot-Performed Preventive Maintenance

**Preventive maintenance** (§ 91.407) consists of simple items the **owner may perform** that do not require complex disassembly — for example, **oil changes and tire changes** — listed in **14 CFR Part 43 Appendix A(c)**. Per the FAA's Coleal (2009) legal interpretation, **Appendix A is not exhaustive but is a good guide**. Preventive maintenance requires a **logbook entry** and sometimes a test flight. **The owner/operator is responsible for maintaining the aircraft in an airworthy condition (§ 91.405); the pilot verifies the paperwork.** Deferred maintenance follows the same § 91.213 process — placard and deactivate, with a logbook entry, when no safety-of-flight issue exists.

## AI.III.B.K3 — Equipment Requirements for Day and Night VFR

**§ 91.205** sets the minimum instruments and equipment for VFR flight.

**Day VFR** (the **ATOMATO FLAMES** set): **airspeed indicator, tachometer, oil pressure gauge, manifold pressure gauge (if applicable), altimeter, temperature gauge (liquid-cooled engines), oil temperature gauge (air-cooled), fuel gauge (each tank), landing gear position indicators (if retractable), anticollision lights (red or white, aircraft certificated after March 11, 1996), magnetic compass, ELT, and seat belts.** Flotation gear and a flare apply for certain for-hire and over-water operations.

**Night VFR** adds (the **FLAPS** set): **fuses (a spare set or three of each type, accessible to the pilot), a landing light (if the aircraft is for hire), anticollision lights, position lights, and an adequate source of electrical power (alternator/battery).**

### AI.III.B.K3a — Flying with Inoperative Equipment

When equipment is inoperative, the pilot determines airworthiness by a defined process under **§ 91.213**. Without a MEL, the inoperative item may be flown only if it is **not** required by any of:

- the **VFR-day type certification** requirements of the aircraft (e.g., 14 CFR Part 23 — for example, a stall-warning indicator),
- the **Kinds of Operation Equipment List (KOEL) / equipment list** (not all airplanes have one),
- **§ 91.205** (the day/night VFR equipment), or
- an applicable **Airworthiness Directive**.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
The inoperative-equipment decision is a checklist of "not required by." Walk the four gates — type certificate, KOEL/equipment list, § 91.205, and ADs. If any gate requires the item, the flight does not go without it. If none do, deactivate and placard it and confirm the flight is safe.
]]

### AI.III.B.K3b — Using an Approved Minimum Equipment List (MEL)

An approved **Minimum Equipment List** states which items may be inoperative and the conditions and procedures for the deferral. The MEL is the controlling document.

### AI.III.B.K3c — Kinds of Operation Equipment List (KOEL)

The **Kinds of Operation Equipment List (KOEL)** — or the aircraft's equipment list — identifies the equipment required for the kinds of operation the aircraft is approved for; not all airplanes have one. An item required by the KOEL cannot be inoperative unless a MEL provides the deferral.

### AI.III.B.K3d — Required Discrepancy Records or Placards

If the item is not required by any of the four gates, it must be **removed or deactivated and placarded "Inoperative,"** and a determination made that the aircraft is safe to fly. A **required discrepancy record or placard** documents the inoperative item.

## AI.III.B.K4 — Standard and Special Airworthiness Certificates

- **Standard (§ 21.175(a))** — issued for aircraft conforming to an approved type design in the normal, utility, acrobatic, commuter, or transport categories (and balloons/special classes). Most training aircraft hold a standard certificate, allowing broad operational flexibility.
- **Special (§ 21.175(b))** — issued for aircraft not meeting standard criteria: primary, restricted, provisional, or limited category; light-sport; experimental; or aircraft on a special flight permit. These carry **specific operational limitations**.

## Risk Management

### AI.III.B.R1 — Inoperative Equipment Discovered Prior to Flight

The named risk is **inoperative equipment discovered before flight**.

- **Recognize** — find it on preflight or on the run-up.
- **Assess** — is it required? Walk the four gates (type certificate, KOEL/equipment list, § 91.205, ADs).
- **Mitigate** — if required, do not fly; if not required, deactivate and placard it, confirm the flight is safe, and record the discrepancy.
- **Verify** — confirm the aircraft is still airworthy and the required documents and inspections are current.

### Common Errors and Corrections

- **Flying with a required item inoperative:** if type certification, the KOEL, § 91.205, or an AD requires it, the flight does not go.
- **Skipping the placard and logbook entry:** an allowable inoperative item still must be deactivated, placarded, and recorded.
- **Letting a recurring AD lapse:** recurring ADs have no flyover allowance.
- **Confusing the 100-hour with the annual:** the 100-hour applies when carrying for hire (or for-hire instruction); both have their own intervals.
- **Assuming the owner keeps the pilot legal:** the pilot verifies the paperwork before flight.

## Skills — Teaching the Task

The ACS skill elements require the applicant to **locate, determine, and apply**:

### AI.III.B.S1 — Locate and Describe the Airworthiness and Registration Information

The airworthiness certificate (displayed, valid while airworthy), the registration (7-year), and the operating limitations/POH with supplements.

### AI.III.B.S2 — Determine the Airplane Is Airworthy in a Scenario

Given an aircraft and its paperwork, the learner confirms the documents, inspections (annual/100-hour, transponder, ELT), and AD compliance, and determines the airplane is airworthy.

### AI.III.B.S3 — Apply the Inoperative-Equipment Procedure in a Scenario

Given an inoperative item, the learner walks § 91.213 — with or without a MEL, the four gates, deactivate-and-placard — and determines whether the flight may proceed.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

1. What equipment is required for day VFR flight?

[[answer:
Under § 91.205, day VFR requires (ATOMATO FLAMES): airspeed indicator, tachometer, oil pressure gauge, manifold pressure gauge (if applicable), altimeter, temperature gauge (liquid-cooled), oil temperature gauge (air-cooled), fuel gauge for each tank, landing-gear position indicators (if retractable), anticollision lights (aircraft certificated after March 11, 1996), magnetic compass, ELT, and seat belts. Night VFR adds fuses, a landing light (if for hire), anticollision and position lights, and an electrical power source (FLAPS).
]]

2. How do you determine airworthiness with an inoperative item and no MEL?

[[answer:
Under § 91.213, the item may be inoperative only if it is not required by (1) the aircraft's VFR-day type-certification basis (e.g., Part 23), (2) the KOEL/equipment list, (3) § 91.205, or (4) an applicable Airworthiness Directive. If none require it, the item must be removed or deactivated and placarded "Inoperative," with a discrepancy record, and the pilot must determine the aircraft is safe to fly.
]]

3. What is the difference between a MEL and a KOEL?

[[answer:
A MEL (Minimum Equipment List) is an approved document stating which items may be inoperative and the conditions for deferral — it is controlling where it applies. A KOEL (Kinds of Operation Equipment List) is in the POH and lists the equipment required for a kind of operation (VFR, IFR, day, night); not all airplanes have one. Both bear on whether an inoperative item is allowable.
]]

4. What documents must the aircraft carry, and what are their durations?

[[answer:
The airworthiness certificate (displayed; valid as long as the aircraft is maintained airworthy, § 91.203(a)(1)), the registration (valid 7 years from the month issued, § 91.203(a)(2)), and the operating limitations/POH with the current weight and balance and flight-manual supplements (§ 91.9(b)).
]]

5. What inspections are required, and at what intervals?

[[answer:
Annual inspection every 12 calendar months (§ 91.409(a)); a 100-hour inspection when carrying persons for hire (or for-hire instruction), with a 10-hour flyover allowance (§ 91.409(b)); transponder test every 24 calendar months (§ 91.413(a)); ELT test every 12 months and battery at 1 hour cumulative use or 50 percent of useful life (§ 91.207(c)/(d)). ADs are mandatory — recurring ADs have no flyover allowance.
]]

6. What is the difference between an AD and a SAIB?

[[answer:
An Airworthiness Directive (14 CFR Part 39) is a mandatory regulation to correct an unsafe condition — compliance is required, and recurring ADs have no flyover allowance. A SAIB is non-regulatory FAA safety information that does not meet the AD threshold — not mandatory, but not to be ignored.
]]

7. What is a special flight permit, and how is one obtained?

[[answer:
A special flight permit (§ 21.199) authorizes a flight that would otherwise not meet airworthiness requirements — for example, ferrying an aircraft with an inoperative item to a repair location. Apply to the FSDO, stating the purpose, itinerary, crew, and the unairworthy items; the permit carries the conditions for the flight.
]]

8. What preventive maintenance may a pilot perform, and what is required?

[[answer:
Simple items not requiring complex disassembly, listed in Part 43 Appendix A(c) — for example, oil changes and tire changes (Appendix A is a good guide but not exhaustive, per the Coleal 2009 interpretation). Preventive maintenance requires a logbook entry and sometimes a test flight. The owner/operator maintains airworthiness (§ 91.405); the pilot verifies the paperwork.
]]

9. How do you manage inoperative equipment discovered before flight?

[[answer:
Recognize it on preflight; assess whether it is required by walking the four gates (type certificate, KOEL, § 91.205, ADs); mitigate by not flying if required, or deactivating and placarding if not required and confirming the flight is safe; and verify the aircraft remains airworthy with current documents and inspections.
]]

## Completion Standard

The learner explains the day/night VFR equipment requirements, the inoperative-equipment process (with and without a MEL), the required documents and inspections, the role of ADs and SAIBs, the special flight permit, and pilot-performed preventive maintenance; applies the recognize–assess–mitigate–verify process to inoperative equipment; and locates the airworthiness/registration information, determines the airplane is airworthy, and applies the inoperative-equipment procedure for given scenarios.
