# Task A. Pilot Qualifications

**Area III — Preflight Preparation**

## Objective

Determine that the applicant understands pilot training and qualification requirements for the different levels of pilot certificate — student, sport, recreational, private, commercial, and flight instructor — can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate the required skills, and provide effective instruction.

## Purpose

Every pilot must know the rules that make them legal to fly. The CFI carries a special weight: an instructor who misreads a currency rule or an endorsement requirement can put a student in the air illegally. This lesson covers the classes of pilot and medical certificates and their privileges and limitations, the recency-of-experience requirements, the logbook rules, and the documents a pilot must carry.

This is a ground-only knowledge Task. The ACS skill element requires the applicant to **teach** at least two of the knowledge elements.

## Schedule / Equipment

- Ground: 15–30 minutes
- Student Q&A: 10 minutes
- FAR/AIM (current) — 14 CFR Parts 61 and 91
- Whiteboard / markers (optional)

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** explain the training requirements and privileges/limitations for the certificate sought, the classes and durations of medical certificates and BasicMed, the recent-flight-experience requirements as PIC, the logbook-entry requirements, and the documents a pilot must carry — then teach at least two elements aloud.

**Instructor:** deliver the ground lesson, have the learner locate each rule in Part 61, and require the learner to teach two elements back as if to a student.

## Completion Standards

The learner can explain:

- The **training requirements** and the **privileges and limitations** for each pilot certificate (student, sport, recreational, private, commercial, flight instructor).
- The **classes and durations of medical certificates** and operations under **BasicMed**.
- The **recent flight experience** required to act as PIC — flight review, passenger-carrying, and night currency.
- The **requirements for logging flight time** and the contents of a logbook entry.
- The **certificates and documents** a pilot must possess to exercise the privileges of a certificate.

# Teach

Teach pilot qualifications as the answer to one question: **is this pilot legal for this flight?** The answer combines the certificate, the medical, the currency, and the documents. The Overview maps the ACS knowledge, risk, and skill elements; the sections below teach the material once, in ACS knowledge-element order.

## AI.III.A.K1 — Certification, Currency, and Recordkeeping Requirements

### Training requirements (aeronautical experience)

- **Recreational pilot — § 61.99 (Subpart D):** 30 hours total, 15 hours dual, 3 hours solo, 2 hours dual cross-country (greater than 25 NM).
- **Private pilot — § 61.109 (Subpart E; student pilots in Subpart C):** 40 hours total, 20 hours dual, 10 hours solo, 3 hours night, 3 hours instrument, 5 hours solo cross-country, 3 hours dual cross-country.
- **Commercial pilot — § 61.129 (Subpart F):** hold at least a private certificate; 250 hours total, 100 hours PIC (50 in airplanes, 50 cross-country), 20 hours dual, 10 hours complex/TAA dual, 10 hours instrument dual, 10 hours solo (cross-country), 5 hours solo night VFR. (TAA is defined in § 61.129(j).)

### Recent flight experience — §§ 61.56, 61.57

- **Flight review — § 61.56:** within the preceding **24 calendar months**, **1 hour of flight and 1 hour of ground** training, with an endorsement.
- **Passenger-carrying currency — § 61.57(a)(1):** to carry passengers, the pilot must have made **3 takeoffs and 3 landings as sole manipulator of the controls** in the preceding **90 days**.
- **Night currency — § 61.57(b)(1):** to carry passengers at night, **3 takeoffs and 3 landings to a full stop** as sole manipulator, in the period **1 hour after sunset to 1 hour before sunrise**, in the preceding **90 days**.

### Logbook entries — § 61.51

Log the time needed to meet the recency-of-experience requirements or to earn a certificate or rating. Every entry (**§ 61.51(b)**) contains the **date, total flight time, points of departure and arrival, aircraft type and identification, the safety-pilot name if required, the type of experience (solo, PIC, dual, etc.), and the conditions of flight (day, night, simulated instrument, etc.)**. A **training entry (§ 61.51(h))** must be **endorsed legibly (signed)** by the authorized instructor with the **certificate number, certificate expiration, a description of the training, and the training time**.

## AI.III.A.K2 — Privileges and Limitations of Pilot Certificates and Ratings

Teach each certificate by what it allows, and have the learner read the controlling section.

### Privileges and limitations

- **Recreational pilot — § 61.101:** maximum **1 passenger**; generally remain within **50 NM** of the home airport unless additional training and an endorsement allow otherwise.
- **Private pilot — § 61.113:** may **not fly for compensation or hire** and must **pay at least a pro-rata share**; the narrow exception is flying **incidental to a business** (for example, a traveling professional). Requires at least a **third-class medical or BasicMed**.
- **Commercial pilot — § 61.133:** may fly for **compensation or hire**; certain operations (ferry flights, banner towing, cropdusting, parachute drop, and the like under § 119.1(e)) do not require an operating certificate. Requires at least a **second-class medical** to exercise the privileges. **Without an instrument rating** — § 61.133(b)(1) — **no carrying passengers for hire beyond 50 NM or at night**.
- **Flight instructor:** the privileges and limits of the instructor certificate — including the need for the underlying commercial or ATP certificate and the appropriate ratings — apply when giving instruction.

[[highlight:
Flight Guide Instructor Principle |
Privileges live in a specific section — § 61.101, § 61.113, § 61.133. Teach the learner to read the actual section rather than rely on "everyone knows." The compensation-and-hire line and the 50-NM/night limits are where pilots get into trouble.
]]

## AI.III.A.K3 — Medical Certificates: Class, Expiration, Privileges, Temporary Disqualifications, and BasicMed

The medical certificate's **class** sets which privileges it supports and how long it lasts (§ 61.23). A certificate can be used for the privileges of a **lower class** after the higher-class privileges expire, subject to the lower class's own duration.

- **First class** — for **ATP privileges**: valid 12 calendar months if under 40, 6 calendar months if 40 or over.
- **Second class** — for **commercial privileges (or ATP second-in-command)**: 12 calendar months.
- **Third class** — for **private-pilot privileges or as a required crewmember**: 60 calendar months if under 40, 24 calendar months if 40 or over.

**BasicMed (14 CFR Part 68):** an alternative that lets a qualifying private pilot fly **without holding a Part 67 medical certificate** under § 61.113(i). To qualify, the pilot must hold a valid **U.S. driver's license**, have held a Part 67 medical at some point after July 14, 2006 (not suspended, revoked, or denied), complete the **BasicMed medical education course** (§ 68.3) within the preceding **24 calendar months**, and receive a **comprehensive medical examination** from a state-licensed physician using the FAA checklist (§§ 68.5, 68.7) within the preceding **48 calendar months**. The completed checklist and the course-completion certificate must be in the pilot's logbook. **The operating limits:** the aircraft may carry **no more than 7 occupants**, weigh **no more than 12,500 pounds** (MTOW), have **no more than 6 passengers** on board, and the flight may not exceed **18,000 feet MSL**, **250 knots** indicated, or leave the United States without the host country's authorization — and not for compensation or hire. See AC 68-1A.

**Special issuance and SODA (§ 67.401):** at the discretion of the Federal Air Surgeon, an applicant not otherwise eligible may receive an **Authorization for special issuance** after additional evaluation, or a **Statement of Demonstrated Ability (SODA)** for a static, non-progressive condition where the person demonstrates the ability to perform airman duties without endangering public safety. The Authorization or SODA states the class and any operational limitations. **Temporary disqualifications** — illness, medication, surgery — ground a pilot until resolved, regardless of the medical's date.

## AI.III.A.K4 — Documents Pilots Must Possess to Exercise Privileges

To exercise the privileges of a certificate, the pilot must have in their **personal possession or readily accessible**:

- A **government-issued photo ID**.
- The **pilot certificate** (a student pilot on a solo carries the student pilot certificate).
- The **medical certificate** (or evidence of BasicMed qualification).
- On a **solo**, the **logbook with all solo endorsements**.
- For **international flight**, a restricted radiotelephone operator's license as applicable.

## Risk Management

### AI.III.A.R1 — Proficiency Versus Currency

Being **legally current is not the same as being proficient**. A pilot can meet the 90-day rule and still be unsafe. Mitigate by setting personal minimums above the legal floor, seeking instruction after a lapse, and honestly self-assessing before a demanding flight.

### AI.III.A.R2 — Flying Unfamiliar Aircraft or Operating with Unfamiliar Flight Display Systems and Avionics

A legal pilot in an unfamiliar airplane or behind an unfamiliar glass display is at risk. Mitigate with transition training, a thorough avionics checkout, and conservative first flights in the new equipment.

### Common Errors and Corrections

- **Confusing currency with proficiency:** read the rule for legality, then set a higher personal standard for safety.
- **Misreading the medical durations:** the class sets both the privilege and the calendar-month duration, with the under-40 / 40-and-over split.
- **Carrying passengers without the 90-day landings:** three takeoffs and landings as sole manipulator — and to a full stop at night.
- **Flying for compensation on a private certificate:** § 61.113 prohibits it except for the narrow pro-rata-share and incidental-business provisions.
- **Missing documents:** photo ID, pilot certificate, medical, and — on solo — the logbook with endorsements.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

1. What are the aeronautical-experience requirements for a private pilot certificate?

[[answer:
Per § 61.109: 40 hours total, 20 hours dual, 10 hours solo, 3 hours night, 3 hours instrument, 5 hours solo cross-country, and 3 hours dual cross-country. The recreational (§ 61.99, 30 hours) and commercial (§ 61.129, 250 hours with PIC, complex/TAA, instrument, and cross-country components) requirements differ — read the section for each.
]]

2. What are the privileges and limitations of a private pilot?

[[answer:
Per § 61.113: a private pilot may not fly for compensation or hire and must pay at least a pro-rata share; the narrow exception is flying incidental to a business. At least a third-class medical or BasicMed is required. A commercial pilot (§ 61.133) may fly for hire, but without an instrument rating may not carry passengers for hire beyond 50 NM or at night.
]]

3. What are the classes and durations of medical certificates?

[[answer:
First class — for ATP privileges: 12 calendar months under 40, 6 calendar months at 40 or over. Second class — for commercial privileges: 12 calendar months. Third class — for private privileges or as a required crewmember: 60 calendar months under 40, 24 calendar months at 40 or over. A certificate can be used for lower-class privileges after the higher-class privileges expire, subject to the lower class's duration.
]]

4. What is BasicMed, and what is a SODA?

[[answer:
BasicMed (14 CFR Part 68) lets a qualifying private pilot fly under § 61.113(i) without holding a Part 67 medical: the pilot holds a driver's license, held a Part 67 medical after July 14, 2006, completes the BasicMed medical-education course within 24 calendar months, and receives a comprehensive medical examination using the FAA checklist within 48 calendar months, with the checklist and course certificate in the logbook. Operating limits: no more than 7 occupants / 6 passengers, no more than 12,500 pounds MTOW, not above 18,000 feet MSL or 250 knots, and not for compensation or hire. A SODA (§ 67.401) is a Statement of Demonstrated Ability granted at the Federal Air Surgeon's discretion for a static condition when the person demonstrates they can perform airman duties safely; a special issuance is an Authorization granted after additional evaluation.
]]

5. What recent flight experience is required to carry passengers, and at night?

[[answer:
To carry passengers: 3 takeoffs and 3 landings as sole manipulator of the controls in the preceding 90 days (§ 61.57(a)(1)). To carry passengers at night: 3 takeoffs and 3 landings to a full stop as sole manipulator, in the period 1 hour after sunset to 1 hour before sunrise, in the preceding 90 days (§ 61.57(b)(1)). A flight review (1 hour flight, 1 hour ground) is also required every 24 calendar months (§ 61.56).
]]

6. What must every logbook entry contain, and what must a training entry add?

[[answer:
Every entry (§ 61.51(b)): date, total flight time, points of departure and arrival, aircraft type and identification, safety-pilot name if required, type of experience (solo/PIC/dual/etc.), and conditions of flight. A training entry (§ 61.51(h)) adds a legible instructor signature with certificate number and expiration, a description of the training, and the training time.
]]

7. What documents must a pilot carry to exercise the privileges of a certificate?

[[answer:
A government-issued photo ID, the pilot certificate, and the medical certificate (or BasicMed evidence). On a solo, the student also carries the logbook with all solo endorsements. For international flight, a restricted radiotelephone operator's license as applicable.
]]

8. What is the difference between proficiency and currency, and why does it matter?

[[answer:
Currency is legal recency — meeting the 90-day and flight-review rules. Proficiency is actual skill and safety. A pilot can be current but dangerously rusty; the rule is a floor, not a goal. Manage the risk with personal minimums above the legal floor, instruction after a lapse, and honest self-assessment.
]]

9. What risk does an unfamiliar aircraft or avionics suite pose, and how do you manage it?

[[answer:
A legal pilot in an unfamiliar airplane or behind unfamiliar displays is at risk even though current. Manage it with transition training, a thorough avionics checkout, and conservative first flights in the new equipment.
]]

## Completion Standard

The learner explains the training, privileges, and limitations of each pilot certificate; the classes, durations, and privileges of medical certificates and BasicMed; the recent-flight-experience requirements as PIC; the logbook-entry requirements; and the documents a pilot must carry; applies the proficiency-versus-currency and unfamiliar-aircraft risk concepts; and delivers instruction on at least two of the knowledge elements, anchoring each answer in the controlling regulation.
