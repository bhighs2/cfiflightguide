# Task A. Straight-and-Level Flight

**Area VIII — Fundamentals of Flight**

## Objective

To determine that the instructor applicant understands straight-and-level flight, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate the maneuver while simultaneously providing effective instruction, and analyze and correct common errors.

## Purpose

Straight-and-level flight is the most fundamental flight maneuver. Nearly every other maneuver begins from, returns to, or represents a deliberate departure from straight-and-level flight. It introduces the learner to aircraft attitude, primary flight controls, outside visual references, power, trim, coordination, and the integrated use of outside and instrument references.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested Schedule**

- Ground lesson: approximately 15–20 minutes
- Flight introduction and demonstration: approximately 20 minutes
- Continued proficiency: reinforced on subsequent flights
- Debrief: approximately 10 minutes

**Equipment**

- Airplane checklist
- Airplane Flying Handbook
- Pilot's Handbook of Aeronautical Knowledge
- CFI ACS
- Whiteboard or tablet and stylus
- Model airplane, if useful
- Flight Guide visual aids

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner Actions**

- Review assigned references and video
- Explain the purpose and basic aerodynamics of straight-and-level flight
- Identify pitch and bank references outside the airplane
- Practice establishing and maintaining straight-and-level flight
- Use trim to relieve control pressure
- Maintain an effective visual scan and integrated cross-check
- Analyze deviations and make timely, measured corrections

**Instructor Actions**

- Present the ground lesson in a logical sequence
- Demonstrate straight-and-level flight while simultaneously explaining the maneuver
- Emphasize outside visual references and light control pressures
- Demonstrate proper trim technique and integrated flight instruction
- Introduce realistic distractions only when appropriate
- Identify, demonstrate when useful, and correct common learner errors
- Debrief performance using specific, objective observations

## Completion Standards

The learner can explain the purpose, aerodynamics, controls, trim, visual references, integrated flight instruction, risks, and common errors associated with straight-and-level flight.

During flight, the learner establishes and maintains straight-and-level flight using appropriate outside references, coordinated control pressures, power, and trim; maintains an effective traffic scan and instrument cross-check; recognizes deviations; and makes timely, measured corrections.

For the CFI practical test, the instructor applicant should be able to **perform the Task at the level of a commercial pilot while providing effective instruction** and analyze and correct common errors.

# Teach

## The Four Fundamentals

The four fundamental flight maneuvers are:

1. Straight-and-level flight
2. Turns
3. Climbs
4. Descents

Straight-and-level flight is the starting point because it teaches the learner to establish an aircraft attitude, recognize the resulting performance, and make small corrections using coordinated control pressure, power, and trim.

[[highlight:
Key Point |
Nearly every maneuver is a deliberate departure from straight-and-level flight followed by a return to another stabilized condition.
]]

## Aircraft Attitude, Axes, and Flight Controls

**Attitude** is the airplane's orientation relative to the horizon.

An airplane rotates around three axes:

- **Longitudinal axis → Roll → Ailerons**
- **Lateral axis → Pitch → Elevator**
- **Vertical axis → Yaw → Rudder**

### Ailerons — Roll

Ailerons control rotation around the longitudinal axis. In straight-and-level flight, the pilot uses small coordinated aileron inputs to correct bank and maintain the desired direction.

### Elevator — Pitch

The elevator controls rotation around the lateral axis. Pitch attitude is the primary means of establishing the airplane's nose position relative to the horizon and is central to maintaining altitude while power determines the resulting energy state and airspeed.

### Rudder — Yaw and Coordination

The rudder controls rotation around the vertical axis and is used primarily to maintain coordinated flight. The airplane is not normally turned with rudder alone; turns are established by banking with coordinated aileron and rudder.

## Four Forces in Straight-and-Level Flight

The four forces acting on the airplane are:

- **Lift** — primarily upward
- **Weight** — downward toward the center of the earth
- **Thrust** — generally forward
- **Drag** — generally rearward

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img
        src="/knowledge/Four_Forces_Flight.png"
        alt="Four forces of flight showing lift, weight, thrust, and drag"
        loading="lazy"
    >
    <figcaption>Four Forces of Flight</figcaption>
</figure>

In **steady, unaccelerated straight-and-level flight**:

- Lift equals weight.
- Thrust equals drag.

The airplane is neither accelerating vertically nor horizontally. A change in one force disturbs that equilibrium and produces a change in performance until a new equilibrium is established.

### Power, Airspeed, and Altitude

Power should not be treated independently from pitch.

At a stabilized altitude:

- Increasing power without an appropriate pitch correction tends to increase airspeed and may cause the airplane to climb.
- Decreasing power without an appropriate pitch correction tends to decrease airspeed and may cause the airplane to descend.
- Significant airspeed changes require a new pitch attitude and new trim setting to maintain altitude.

[[highlight:
Control Concept |
Use **attitude + power** to establish the desired performance, allow the airplane to stabilize, then trim away the remaining control pressure.
]]

## Outside Visual References

Straight-and-level flight should be established primarily with **outside visual references**.

The learner should develop a repeatable sight picture for both pitch and bank rather than attempting to fly primarily by chasing the flight instruments.

### Nose Reference — Pitch

The nose or another consistent point on the airplane can be compared with the natural horizon to establish and maintain pitch attitude.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img
        src="/knowledge/Straight_Level_Nose_Reference.png"
        alt="Outside nose reference for straight-and-level flight"
        loading="lazy"
    >
    <figcaption>Straight-and-Level Nose Reference</figcaption>
</figure>

The exact sight picture changes with:

- Pilot seating position
- Aircraft loading
- Airspeed
- Power setting
- Configuration

The goal is not to memorize one universal windshield position. The learner should establish a repeatable reference for the airplane and flight condition being flown.

### Wing Reference — Bank

The wingtips provide a much longer horizontal reference than the airplane's nose and make small bank deviations easier to detect.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img
    src="/knowledge/Straight_Level_Wing_Reference.png"
        alt="Wing reference used to recognize level bank attitude"
        loading="lazy"
    >
    <figcaption>Straight-and-Level Wing Reference</figcaption>
</figure>

A common beginner error is attempting to judge wings-level attitude only from the nose. A slightly lowered wing may be difficult to detect from the short nose reference but is much easier to recognize by comparing the wingtips to the horizon.

### Instructor Technique — Lindbergh Reference

The **Lindbergh Reference** is a Flight Guide instructional technique, not an FAA-defined term.

Have the learner identify stable outside references relative to the airplane — forward for pitch and laterally for bank — and return attention to those references after each instrument cross-check. The objective is to build a repeatable outside sight picture rather than a habit of staring at one instrument or one arbitrary windshield point.

## Integrated Flight Instruction

Integrated flight instruction teaches the learner to use both:

- **Outside visual references** to establish and maintain attitude, traffic awareness, and orientation
- **Flight instruments** to verify the resulting performance

The outside reference remains primary during normal VFR maneuvering. Instruments provide supporting information and confirmation.

A useful scan is:

**Outside attitude and traffic → supporting instruments → outside**

### Pitch / Altitude Cross-Check

Use the outside pitch reference to establish the attitude, then verify performance with the altimeter, vertical speed indication, and airspeed as appropriate.

### Bank / Heading Cross-Check

Use the wingtips and horizon to maintain wings level, then verify heading with the heading display or directional reference.

### Coordination

Use aircraft feel, rudder pressure, and the slip/skid indication to verify coordinated flight.

[[highlight:
Instructor Emphasis |
Do not let the learner use the instruments to **create** a VFR attitude that should first be established outside. Use the instruments to verify the performance produced by the outside attitude.
]]

## Elevator Trim

Trim reduces the continuous control pressure required to maintain a desired attitude.

The pilot first establishes the desired attitude and power, holds that condition with normal elevator pressure, allows the airplane to stabilize, and then trims to relieve the remaining pressure.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img
        src="/knowledge/Elevator_Trim_Control.png"
        alt="Elevator trim control and trim system"
        loading="lazy"
    >
    <figcaption>Elevator Trim Control</figcaption>
</figure>

For the Cessna 172S trim wheel:

- Wheel forward → **nose-down trim**
- Wheel aft → **nose-up trim**

### Correct Trim Sequence

1. Establish attitude.
2. Set or confirm power.
3. Allow performance to stabilize.
4. Hold the desired attitude with normal control pressure.
5. Trim until the pressure is relieved.
6. Recheck attitude and performance.

Trim is **not** a primary flight control and should not be used to fly the airplane to the desired attitude.

[[highlight:
Trim Rule |
**Attitude → Power → Stabilize → Trim.**
Do not chase the airplane with the trim wheel.
]]

## Control Pressure, Grip, and Feel

Straight-and-level flight requires small, smooth corrections.

A tight two-handed palm grip can reduce tactile feedback and encourage overcontrol. During normal maneuvering, a relaxed one-handed grip often improves feel and leaves the other hand available for power, trim, radios, and other cockpit tasks.

The pilot should think in terms of **control pressure**, not large mechanical control movements.

### Developing Feel

Encourage the learner to notice:

- Control pressure
- Airplane response
- Engine sound
- Airflow noise
- Seat pressure
- Rudder pressure
- Visual movement of the horizon

These sensory inputs support the visual and instrument cross-check and help the learner recognize a deviation earlier.

## Risk Management

### Distraction and Task Prioritization — AI.VIII.A.R1

Straight-and-level flight often occurs while the pilot is also navigating, communicating, programming avionics, reviewing a checklist, or managing passengers.

The instructor should teach the learner to:

- Maintain aircraft control first
- Keep outside scan active
- Avoid prolonged head-down time
- Break nonessential tasks into short segments
- Delay or discontinue a secondary task when aircraft control or situational awareness begins to degrade

**Aviate → Navigate → Communicate** remains a useful prioritization model.

### Loss of Situational Awareness / Disorientation — AI.VIII.A.R1

A prolonged instrument fixation or poor outside scan can reduce orientation and traffic awareness. During VFR flight, continually reconnect the instrument indications with the actual outside attitude and environment.

### Collision Hazards — AI.VIII.A.R2

Straight-and-level flight is not a reason to stop looking outside.

Maintain an active visual scan for:

- Aircraft
- Converging traffic
- Weather
- Terrain and obstacles
- Changes in visibility

Clearing and collision avoidance remain continuous responsibilities, especially while teaching, demonstrating, or correcting a maneuver.

## Common Errors and Corrections

### 1. Improper Pitch or Bank Reference Points

**Error:** Selecting poor or inconsistent visual reference points on the airplane.

**Correction:** Help the learner establish a clear nose reference for pitch and wingtip/horizon relationship for bank.

### 2. Forgetting Preselected Reference Points

**Error:** Failing to remember the sight picture established on previous flights.

**Correction:** Deliberately identify the reference points each flight and compare the current sight picture to prior experience.

### 3. Establishing or Correcting Attitude With Instruments Instead of the Natural Horizon

**Error:** Going immediately inside to correct pitch or bank.

**Correction:** Re-establish the outside attitude first, then verify the result with the instruments.

### 4. Chasing the Flight Instruments

**Error:** Continuously reacting to small instrument movements instead of maintaining a stable attitude.

**Correction:** Hold a stable outside attitude, allow the airplane and instruments time to respond, then make a measured correction.

### 5. Mechanical Pushing or Pulling Instead of Smooth Pressure

**Error:** Moving the controls abruptly or by predetermined amounts.

**Correction:** Apply smooth pressure and observe the aircraft response; release unnecessary pressure as the desired attitude is reached.

### 6. Inadequate Outside Scan

**Error:** Failing to scan for traffic, weather, terrain, and other external influences.

**Correction:** Establish a deliberate outside-dominant scan and keep instrument looks brief.

### 7. Tight Palm Grip

**Error:** Gripping the controls tightly, reducing sensitivity and feel.

**Correction:** Relax the hand and use a light grip and fingertip pressure where practical.

### 8. Overcontrolling

**Error:** Making corrections larger or faster than necessary.

**Correction:** Recognize deviations early and use smaller, measured pressures.

### 9. Flying With One Wing Low or Using Rudder Alone for Directional Control

**Error:** Allowing a bank to persist while attempting to hold heading with rudder.

**Correction:** Level the wings with coordinated aileron and rudder; use the wingtip/horizon relationship as the primary bank reference.

### 10. Late or Unmeasured Corrections

**Error:** Waiting until a deviation becomes large, then making an excessive correction.

**Correction:** Detect small deviations early and make timely, proportional corrections.

### 11. Inadequate Attention to Sensory Inputs

**Error:** Ignoring control feel, pressure, sound, and other cues that indicate changing aircraft performance.

**Correction:** Encourage deliberate awareness of the airplane's visual, tactile, and auditory cues while maintaining the primary outside scan.

# Fly

## Setup

### Objective

Demonstrate how to establish and maintain straight-and-level flight while teaching the learner to use outside visual references, coordinated control pressure, power, trim, and an integrated cross-check.

### Before the Demonstration

- Select an appropriate practice area and altitude.
- Clear the area and establish traffic awareness.
- Establish a desired heading, altitude, and airspeed or cruise configuration.
- Verify the learner understands positive exchange of flight controls.
- Explain what the learner should watch during the demonstration:
  - Nose/horizon relationship
  - Wingtip/horizon relationship
  - Control pressures
  - Power setting
  - Trim technique
  - Outside-to-inside cross-check

## Instructor Demonstration

### 1. Establish the Outside Sight Picture

Select a prominent reference generally ahead of the airplane and establish the desired heading.

Use the natural horizon to establish:

- **Pitch** — nose reference relative to the horizon
- **Bank** — wingtip relationship to the horizon

Keep the wings level and the nose aligned with the desired direction of flight.

### 2. Establish Power and Performance

Set an appropriate power setting for the desired flight condition.

Allow the airplane to stabilize. Observe the resulting:

- Airspeed
- Altitude trend
- Heading
- Coordination

Make small pitch or power corrections as necessary rather than chasing individual indications.

### 3. Coordinate the Controls

Use coordinated aileron and rudder pressure for directional corrections.

If the airplane begins to turn because a wing is low:

1. Correct the bank with coordinated aileron and rudder.
2. Return the controls toward neutral as the wings become level.
3. Re-establish the desired heading with a small coordinated correction if needed.

Do not attempt to hold heading against a bank using rudder alone.

### 4. Trim

Once the desired attitude and power are established and the airplane is stable:

1. Hold the attitude with elevator pressure.
2. Adjust trim in the direction that relieves that pressure.
3. Reduce hand pressure as trim takes effect.
4. Stop trimming when the airplane can maintain the desired pitch with minimal pressure.
5. Recheck altitude and airspeed.

### 5. Integrated Cross-Check

Use an outside-dominant scan:

1. Outside attitude and traffic
2. Altimeter / altitude trend
3. Heading
4. Airspeed
5. Coordination
6. Back outside

Use the instruments to **confirm performance**, not to replace the natural horizon during normal VFR maneuvering.

### 6. Make a Small Correction

Demonstrate a minor altitude or heading deviation and a measured recovery.

Explain:

- Recognize the deviation early.
- Correct the attitude smoothly.
- Avoid overshooting with an excessive input.
- Return to a stable attitude.
- Re-trim only if a meaningful new control pressure remains.

## Learner Practice

Have the learner establish and maintain straight-and-level flight while progressively reducing instructor prompting.

Practice should include:

- Maintaining a selected heading
- Maintaining altitude
- Maintaining a selected or stabilized airspeed
- Maintaining coordinated flight
- Identifying nose and wing references
- Trimming correctly
- Correcting small deviations
- Maintaining an outside traffic scan
- Briefly referencing instruments without fixation
- Straight-and-level flight at more than one practical airspeed when appropriate

## Instructor Demonstration Technique

While demonstrating, simultaneously explain:

- **What** you are doing
- **Why** you are doing it
- **What outside cue** you are using
- **What aircraft response** you expect
- **What instrument indication** confirms the result
- **What common learner error** to watch for

Keep the explanation short enough that aircraft control, visual scanning, and collision avoidance remain primary.

## Analyze and Correct Common Errors

The CFI applicant should be prepared for the evaluator to simulate learner errors.

### Head Inside / Instrument Chasing

**Recognize:** Learner stares at altitude or heading and makes continuous corrections.

**Correct:** Redirect attention outside. Re-establish sight picture, then use a brief instrument check to verify performance.

### Tight Grip / Overcontrol

**Recognize:** Repeated oscillations, abrupt control movement, visible tension.

**Correct:** Demonstrate a relaxed grip and pressure-based control. Have the learner briefly release excess pressure and observe the airplane's stability.

### Wing Low / Rudder-Only Correction

**Recognize:** Heading appears acceptable but the airplane remains banked with opposite rudder pressure.

**Correct:** Use the wingtips to identify the bank, level the wings with coordinated controls, then re-establish heading.

### Poor Trim Technique

**Recognize:** Learner uses trim to move the nose or continually adjusts trim before stabilizing.

**Correct:** Re-teach the sequence: **Attitude → Power → Stabilize → Trim**.

### Late Corrections

**Recognize:** Learner permits large deviations, then responds aggressively.

**Correct:** Emphasize early recognition and small, proportional corrections.

### Inadequate Traffic Scan

**Recognize:** Learner spends excessive time inside or fixates on the maneuver.

**Correct:** Stop or simplify the secondary task, restore outside scan, and reinforce task prioritization.

## Completion Standards

The instructor applicant should be able to:

- Explain the purpose and procedure for straight-and-level flight.
- Explain flight control and trim use.
- Teach and demonstrate effective pitch and bank visual references.
- Apply integrated flight instruction.
- Identify and manage distraction, loss of situational awareness, disorientation, and collision hazards.
- Establish and maintain straight-and-level flight while simultaneously providing effective instruction.
- Analyze and correct common errors.
- Perform the Task at the level of a commercial pilot while teaching.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. What is straight-and-level flight?

[[answer:
Flight in which the airplane maintains a constant heading and altitude. In steady, unaccelerated straight-and-level flight, lift equals weight and thrust equals drag.
]]

### 2. Why is straight-and-level flight considered a fundamental maneuver?

[[answer:
It establishes the basic relationship among attitude, power, flight controls, trim, outside visual references, and performance. Other maneuvers are deliberate departures from or transitions between stabilized flight conditions.
]]

### 3. What are the primary outside references for straight-and-level flight?

[[answer:
Use a consistent nose-to-horizon relationship for pitch and the wingtip-to-horizon relationship for bank. The exact sight picture depends on the airplane, seating position, airspeed, power, and configuration.
]]

### 4. What is integrated flight instruction?

[[answer:
Integrated flight instruction teaches the learner to use outside visual references and supporting flight instruments together. During normal VFR maneuvering, outside references establish attitude and maintain traffic awareness while the instruments verify performance.
]]

### 5. What is the proper sequence for using elevator trim?

[[answer:
Establish the desired attitude, set or confirm power, allow the airplane to stabilize, hold the required control pressure, and then trim to relieve that pressure. Trim should not be used as a primary flight control.
]]

### 6. Why are the wingtips important for recognizing bank?

[[answer:
The wingtips provide a longer horizontal reference against the natural horizon than the nose. Small bank deviations are often easier to detect using the wingtips, reducing the tendency to fly with one wing low and compensate with rudder.
]]

### 7. How should a CFI correct an instrument-chasing learner?

[[answer:
Redirect the learner outside, re-establish the desired sight picture using the natural horizon, hold the attitude long enough for the airplane to stabilize, and then use a brief instrument check to verify the resulting performance.
]]

### 8. What risks are specifically identified by the CFI ACS for this Task?

[[answer:
Distractions, task prioritization, loss of situational awareness or disorientation, and collision hazards.
]]

### 9. What should the evaluator see from the CFI applicant during the flight demonstration?

[[answer:
The applicant should establish and maintain straight-and-level flight while simultaneously providing effective instruction, manage the associated risks, and recognize, analyze, and correct common errors while performing to the level of a commercial pilot.
]]
