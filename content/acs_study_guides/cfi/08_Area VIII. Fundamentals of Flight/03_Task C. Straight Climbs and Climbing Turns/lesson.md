# Task C. Straight Climbs and Climbing Turns

**Area VIII - Fundamentals of Flight**

## Objective

To determine that the instructor applicant understands straight climbs and climbing turns, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate the maneuvers while simultaneously providing effective instruction, and analyze and correct common errors.

## Purpose

A climb is sustained by **excess power**, not elevator alone. This Task teaches the applicant to establish a climb attitude, set power, trim for airspeed, compensate for left-turning tendencies, maintain coordinated flight, and level off at a target altitude. Climbing turns combine climb control with bank, coordination, and increased workload.

## Schedule / Equipment

- **Ground instruction:** 20 minutes.
- **Flight instruction:** 0.6-1.0 hour.
- Checklist, AFH/ACS, performance information, and kneeboard.
- Whiteboard/model airplane and approved visuals.

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** Establish a straight climb, trim for the target climb speed, perform climbing turns in both directions, maintain outside scan and coordination, and level off at a target altitude.

**Instructor:** Use Binns' sequence of four fundamentals, controls/trim, climb aerodynamics, climb speeds, left-turning tendencies, integrated references, and climbing turns. Demonstrate while teaching without losing traffic awareness.

## Completion Standards

The applicant maintains outside scan, trims appropriately, establishes the normal climb attitude and airspeed, turns to assigned headings, holds heading within **+/-10 degrees**, airspeed within **+/-10 knots**, and levels off within **+/-100 feet** while providing effective instruction.

### Objective

**Teach the learner to establish, maintain, and level off from straight climbs and climbing turns using outside visual references, maximum or appropriate climb power, coordinated flight controls, proper trim, and a brief instrument cross-check while maintaining the desired airspeed, heading, bank, traffic awareness, and situational awareness.**

### Purpose

A climb is not created by simply pulling back on the elevator.

A sustained climb requires **excess power**.

Pitch establishes the climb attitude and therefore the airspeed. Power provides the energy necessary to gain altitude.

So the teaching story is:

**clear → power → pitch → right rudder → verify airspeed → trim → hold the outside picture → coordinate the turn if needed → lead the level-off → accelerate → set cruise power → retrim.**

---

### Start With the Big Idea — A Climb Requires Excess Power

In straight-and-level flight, power available is being used to overcome the drag required for that flight condition.

To climb, the airplane needs enough additional power to overcome drag **and** gain potential energy.

So:

**elevator changes pitch; power makes the climb sustainable.**

If you simply raise the nose without adding power:

- airspeed decreases,
- the initial climb may be temporary,
- and eventually the airplane settles at a slower condition or stalls.

The practical teaching point is:

**pitch determines climb airspeed; power determines how much climb performance is available.**

---

### V<sub>X</sub>, V<sub>Y</sub>, and Cruise Climb

There are three climb concepts the learner needs to separate.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Vx_Vy_Diagram.png" alt="Comparison of best-angle climb Vx and best-rate climb Vy" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>V<sub>X</sub> gives the greatest altitude gain over the shortest horizontal distance. V<sub>Y</sub> gives the greatest altitude gain in the shortest time.</figcaption>
</figure>

#### V<sub>X</sub> — Best Angle

V<sub>X</sub> gives:

**the most altitude gained for the least horizontal distance.**

Use it when an obstacle controls the climb.

For the C172S:

- **V<sub>X</sub> = 62 KIAS at sea level**
- **V<sub>X</sub> = 67 KIAS at 10,000 ft**

As altitude increases, V<sub>X</sub> increases.

#### V<sub>Y</sub> — Best Rate

V<sub>Y</sub> gives:

**the most altitude gained in the least time.**

For the C172S:

- **V<sub>Y</sub> = 74 KIAS at sea level**
- **V<sub>Y</sub> = 72 KIAS at 10,000 ft**

As altitude increases, V<sub>Y</sub> decreases.

#### Cruise Climb

The C172S POH says normal enroute climbs are performed:

- **flaps UP**
- **FULL THROTTLE**
- at speeds **5–10 knots above best-rate climb speed**

for a better combination of:

- performance,
- visibility,
- and engine cooling.

For your C172S kneeboard/teaching flow:

**Cruise Climb: 70–85 KIAS, FULL THROTTLE.**

Mixture:

- **RICH below 3,000 ft**
- above **3,000 ft**, lean as appropriate for smoother operation or maximum RPM

Climbs below V<sub>Y</sub> should be kept relatively short because of reduced engine cooling.

---

### C172S Maximum Rate-of-Climb Performance

At **2550 lb**, the C172S maximum-rate chart uses:

- flaps UP,
- full throttle,
- V<sub>Y</sub> approximately:
    - **74 KIAS at sea level**
    - **73 KIAS through much of the middle altitudes**
    - **72 KIAS by the higher altitudes**

The important teaching point is not memorizing every chart cell.

It is:

**climb performance decreases with altitude and temperature.**

Hotter air and higher altitude reduce excess power and therefore reduce rate of climb.

Trying to pitch higher does not create performance that the airplane does not have.

---

### Establish the Straight Climb — Outside First

Before climbing:

**clear the area, especially above and ahead.**

A climb raises the nose and reduces forward visibility, so traffic and obstacle awareness matter before the maneuver begins.

Under **14 CFR §91.113**, the pilot remains responsible for seeing and avoiding other aircraft when weather conditions permit.

Then:

1. smoothly apply climb power,
2. establish the known climb attitude using the natural horizon,
3. anticipate right rudder,
4. allow the airspeed to stabilize,
5. verify the desired climb speed,
6. trim away sustained pressure.

The order matters.

Do not stare at the airspeed indicator and pull until it shows the number.

That is **airspeed chasing**.

Instead:

**set the attitude outside → wait → verify the airspeed → make a small pitch correction if needed.**

---

### What the Climb Looks Like

The nose is higher than in straight-and-level flight.

Use that outside sight picture as the primary pitch reference.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Climb_Indications.png" alt="Outside and instrument indications during a climb" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>Establish the climb attitude with the natural horizon, then verify the expected pitch, airspeed, altitude trend, heading, and coordination on the instruments.</figcaption>
</figure>

The instrument indications should support the outside picture:

- attitude indicator — climb attitude,
- airspeed — target climb speed,
- altimeter — altitude increasing,
- VSI — positive climb trend,
- heading — desired direction,
- slip/skid indicator — coordinated flight.

The integrated scan is:

**outside attitude and traffic → quick instrument check → back outside.**

---

### Right Rudder — Anticipate the Left-Turning Tendencies

Climbs combine:

- high power,
- lower airspeed,
- and higher angle of attack.

That makes the left-turning tendencies more noticeable.

Expect:

- torque,
- spiraling slipstream,
- P-factor,
- and gyroscopic effects where applicable.

So:

**add right rudder as power and pitch increase.**

Do not wait for the nose to visibly yaw left.

Anticipate it.

The amount of rudder is not a memorized pedal position.

Use enough to maintain:

- heading,
- coordination,
- and a centered ball.

---

### Do Not Fixate on the Nose

A common error is staring at the nose during the climb and allowing one wing to remain low.

Use:

- the nose/horizon relationship for pitch,
- **both wings** for bank,
- and outside references ahead for heading.

A straight climb is still a **wings-level maneuver**.

If heading starts drifting:

**fix the bank first.**

Do not hold heading with rudder alone.

---

### Pitch Controls Climb Airspeed

Once climb power is set, pitch is the primary airspeed control.

If too fast:

**raise the nose slightly.**

If too slow:

**lower the nose slightly.**

Then:

**wait for the airplane to stabilize before making another correction.**

Do not make a series of rapid pitch changes while staring at the airspeed indicator.

The correct sequence is:

**outside attitude → small correction → wait → verify → retrim if needed.**

---

### Trim

Once:

- power is set,
- climb attitude is established,
- and airspeed is stable,

trim away the sustained elevator pressure.

The sequence remains:

**Attitude → Power → Stabilize → Trim**

Do not fly the airplane with trim.

Use the elevator to establish the attitude first.

Then trim to relieve pressure.

---

### Climbing Turns — Add Bank Without Giving Away the Climb

A climbing turn combines the straight-climb problem with the level-turn problem.

Now some of the lift is used horizontally to turn.

That means less vertical lift is available for the climb.

So compared with a straight climb:

- climb rate is lower,
- slightly more back pressure may be required,
- coordination becomes more demanding,
- and bank should normally remain shallow.

A shallow bank preserves climb performance.

Do not try to use a medium or steep bank unless the situation requires it.

The steeper the bank, the more lift is diverted to turning and the less efficiently the airplane climbs.

---

### Enter the Climbing Turn

A climbing turn can be entered by:

- establishing the climb first, then banking,
- or combining the climb and turn entry.

For early training, it is often easier to:

**establish the climb first → stabilize → then add the turn.**

To enter:

1. maintain climb power,
2. hold the climb pitch picture,
3. apply coordinated aileron and rudder,
4. establish the selected shallow bank,
5. add the pitch compensation needed,
6. verify airspeed and coordination.

The key is:

**do not sacrifice climb attitude while banking.**

---

### Rudder in Climbing Turns

Rudder demand changes depending on turn direction because adverse yaw interacts with the airplane's normal left-turning tendencies.

In a **right climbing turn**:

- right rudder is needed to counter the normal left-turning tendencies,
- and adverse yaw from right aileron also initially yaws the nose left.

So right-rudder demand may be greater.

In a **left climbing turn**:

- the airplane's natural left-turning tendencies already favor the turn,
- while adverse yaw from left aileron initially yaws right.

So less right rudder may be needed than in a straight climb.

Do not memorize pedal pressure.

Use the outside yaw picture and coordination indicator.

---

### Why a Slip Hurts Climb Performance

A slip creates extra drag.

Extra drag consumes some of the airplane's excess power.

That means:

**less excess power remains available for climbing.**

So a slipping climbing turn may produce:

- poor climb rate,
- little altitude gain,
- or no useful climb at all.

If climb performance is poor:

**check coordination before simply pulling harder.**

---

### Maintain the Climbing Turn

Once established:

- hold the pitch picture,
- hold the bank,
- maintain coordination,
- monitor airspeed,
- continue the traffic scan.

Use:

**outside first → instruments verify.**

If bank increases:

- more vertical lift is lost,
- climb performance worsens,
- and more pitch may be required.

If pitch changes:

- airspeed changes,
- which may then require another small correction.

So pitch and bank interact.

The goal is not to make them perfect instantly.

The goal is to make **small, measured corrections**.

---

### Do Not Exceed the Airplane's Climb Capability

If the airplane is not climbing as expected:

do not simply keep raising the nose.

That causes:

- airspeed loss,
- greater angle of attack,
- poorer cooling,
- and eventually a stall.

Instead:

**lower the nose enough to preserve the correct airspeed and accept the climb performance available.**

Then consider:

- density altitude,
- weight,
- temperature,
- bank,
- configuration,
- mixture,
- and engine performance.

The airplane decides how much excess power is available.

The pilot cannot create more climb by demanding a steeper pitch attitude.

---

### Leveling Off — Lead It

Do not wait until the target altitude to start lowering the nose.

A useful starting technique is to begin the level-off approximately:

**10% of the current rate of climb before the target altitude.**

Example:

500 FPM climb → begin about **50 ft early**.

This is a technique, not an ACS tolerance.

The sequence is:

1. lead the target altitude,
2. smoothly lower the nose toward the level-flight sight picture,
3. keep climb power temporarily while the airplane accelerates,
4. maintain heading and coordination,
5. at the desired cruise airspeed, set cruise power,
6. retrim.

Do not push abruptly.

Excessive forward elevator can:

- unload the airplane,
- create a low-G sensation,
- and cause altitude loss.

The teaching cue is:

**lower the nose smoothly and let the airspeed build.**

---

### Leveling Off From a Climbing Turn

Now two things must happen together:

- pitch returns toward level,
- bank returns toward wings level.

The learner may initially perform them sequentially.

With proficiency, teach:

**roll out and lower the nose smoothly together.**

As:

- pitch decreases,
- bank is removed,
- and power later decreases,

rudder pressure also changes.

Continue coordinating through the entire transition.

---

### Risk Management — Distraction and Task Prioritization

Climbs often occur during high workload:

- departure,
- radio changes,
- checklists,
- navigation setup,
- traffic avoidance.

The priority is still:

**Aviate → Navigate → Communicate.**

If the airplane begins to drift in:

- airspeed,
- heading,
- coordination,
- or bank,

stop the secondary task and fix the airplane first.

---

### Risk Management — Collision Hazards

The nose-high attitude can hide traffic.

Before a climbing turn:

**clear in the direction of turn and above the flightpath.**

During the climb:

- move the scan around the nose,
- check both sides,
- monitor traffic systems as supplemental information,
- and keep looking outside.

Do not let the climb attitude become a blind spot.

---

### Common Errors

#### 1. Chasing Airspeed

**Error:** Establishing pitch from the airspeed indicator.

**Correction:** Set the known outside climb attitude first, then verify airspeed.

#### 2. Excessive Elevator Pressure

**Error:** Pulling too aggressively and creating an excessive climb angle.

**Correction:** Use smooth pressure and allow airspeed to stabilize.

#### 3. Inappropriate Rudder in Climbing Turns

**Error:** Poor coordination as bank and climb are combined.

**Correction:** Coordinate aileron and rudder while accounting for left-turning tendencies.

#### 4. Yaw During the Climb

**Error:** Insufficient right rudder.

**Correction:** Anticipate yaw as power and angle of attack increase.

#### 5. Nose Fixation / One Wing Low

**Error:** Looking only over the nose and missing bank.

**Correction:** Use both wingtips/horizon and maintain the outside scan.

#### 6. Poor Climbing-Turn Entry

**Error:** Banking without coordinated controls, producing little turn and one wing low.

**Correction:** Establish climb, then bank with coordinated aileron/rudder.

#### 7. Slip Reducing Climb

**Error:** Uncoordinated flight creates drag and poor climb performance.

**Correction:** Restore coordination before demanding more pitch.

#### 8. Unstable Pitch and Bank

**Error:** Constantly chasing both parameters.

**Correction:** Re-establish known outside references and make small corrections.

#### 9. Exceeding Climb Capability

**Error:** Pulling harder when performance is inadequate.

**Correction:** Lower pitch to preserve airspeed and accept available climb performance.

#### 10. Poor Level-Off

**Error:** Excessive forward elevator causes altitude loss or low-G.

**Correction:** Lead the level-off and lower pitch smoothly while allowing acceleration.

---

### Instructor Demonstration

Teach it in the order the learner will actually fly it:

1. Clear the area and select heading, altitude, and climb type.
2. Establish straight-and-level flight.
3. **Apply full/appropriate climb power.**
4. **Establish the climb pitch attitude outside.**
5. Add **right rudder** as required.
6. Verify the target airspeed.
7. Trim after stabilization.
8. Maintain wings level, heading, coordination, and outside scan.
9. Demonstrate:
    - Cruise Climb,
    - V<sub>Y</sub>,
    - V<sub>X</sub> conceptually or as conditions permit.
10. For a climbing turn, establish the climb first.
11. Add shallow bank with coordinated aileron/rudder.
12. Add the pitch compensation needed to preserve climb airspeed.
13. Hold bank, pitch, and coordination using outside references.
14. Briefly cross-check airspeed, heading, altitude trend, and ball.
15. Lead rollout from the climbing turn.
16. Lead the target altitude by approximately **10% of rate of climb** as a starting technique.
17. Smoothly lower the nose toward level flight.
18. Leave climb power in temporarily and let the airplane accelerate.
19. At cruise airspeed, set cruise power.
20. Retrim for straight-and-level.

A concise teaching flow is:

**“Clear. Power. Pitch. Right rudder. Let it stabilize. Verify the airspeed. Trim. For the turn, bank and coordinate without giving away the climb. Lead the level-off, lower the nose, accelerate, set power, trim.”**

## AI.VIII.C.K1 - Purpose of and Procedures for Straight Climbs and Climbing Turns

A straight climb is established by setting the climb attitude, applying the required power, maintaining coordination, and trimming for the target airspeed. A climbing turn combines the climb with bank and requires additional attention to pitch, power, rudder, heading, and traffic.

Procedure:

1. Clear the area and establish the desired direction.
2. Apply the required power smoothly and anticipate left-turning tendencies.
3. Establish the climb pitch attitude using the natural horizon.
4. Verify the target climb airspeed and trim.
5. For a climbing turn, bank smoothly with coordinated rudder and add the pitch/power compensation needed to maintain airspeed and climb performance.
6. Level off before the target altitude, reduce pitch, adjust power, and trim.

## AI.VIII.C.K2 - Flight Control and Trim Use

**Elevator** establishes pitch attitude and climb airspeed. **Throttle** supplies the excess power required for climb. **Rudder** counters left-turning tendencies and coordinates climbing turns. **Ailerons** establish bank. **Trim** relieves control pressure after attitude, power, and performance stabilize.

Do not fly the climb by mechanically pulling until the airspeed indicator shows a number. Establish the outside attitude, verify performance, and use smooth pressure. In a climbing turn, maintain the desired bank with small aileron inputs and use elevator/power to prevent altitude or airspeed deviations.

## AI.VIII.C.K3 - Pilot Visual References When Performing the Maneuver

Use the natural horizon and climb sight picture as the primary reference. The nose will appear higher than in level flight; in a climbing turn, judge bank from the panel/horizon relationship and use the attitude indicator as a brief cross-check.

Use the altimeter and VSI to verify climb performance, the airspeed indicator to verify the pitch/speed relationship, and the heading indicator to confirm direction. Return attention outside after each check.

## AI.VIII.C.K4 - Integrated Flight Instruction

Teach the learner to use outside references to establish attitude and instruments to verify performance. While demonstrating, explain the expected response before the input, then keep the explanation short enough to preserve control, scan, and collision avoidance.

Demonstrate climbs and climbing turns in both directions. Ask the learner to identify pitch, bank, rudder, airspeed, altitude trend, and heading without fixating on a single instrument.

## AI.VIII.C.K5 - Common Errors Related to This Task

### 1. Airspeed Chasing

**Error:** Establishing climb pitch primarily by the airspeed indicator and chasing the airspeed.

**Correction:** Set pitch by the natural horizon, allow response, verify airspeed, and trim.

### 2. Excessive Elevator Pressure

**Error:** Applying elevator too aggressively and producing an excessive climb angle.

**Correction:** Use smooth pressure and the target attitude; respect the airplane's climb capability.

### 3. Rudder Errors in Climbing Turns

**Error:** Inadequate or inappropriate rudder during climbing turns.

**Correction:** Coordinate rudder with bank and use the slip/skid indication and aircraft feel.

### 4. Yaw During Climb

**Error:** Allowing yaw from inadequate right rudder pressure.

**Correction:** Anticipate left-turning tendencies and maintain coordinated rudder pressure.

### 5. Nose Fixation and One-Wing-Low Climb

**Error:** Fixating on the nose and climbing with one wing low.

**Correction:** Use both wingtips/horizon and maintain an outside scan.

### 6. Uncoordinated Climbing Turn

**Error:** Initiating a climbing turn without coordinated controls, resulting in little turn and one wing low.

**Correction:** Bank with coordinated rudder and maintain the climb attitude.

### 7. Slip Counteracting Climb

**Error:** A slip reduces climb performance and produces little or no altitude gain.

**Correction:** Center coordination, reduce unnecessary bank, and re-establish the climb attitude.

### 8. Unstable Pitch or Bank

**Error:** Inability to keep pitch and bank constant during climbing turns.

**Correction:** Stabilize pitch/bank separately, use small pressures, and trim after the condition is stable.

### 9. Exceeding Climb Capability

**Error:** Attempting to climb beyond the airplane's available performance.

**Correction:** Reduce pitch to preserve airspeed, accept the available climb performance, and reassess weight, density altitude, and power.

### 10. Poor Level-Off

**Error:** Excessive forward elevator during level-off causes altitude loss or low-G flight.

**Correction:** Begin level-off early, reduce pitch smoothly, adjust power, and trim without unloading the airplane.

## Risk Management

### AI.VIII.C.R1 - Distractions and Situational Awareness

Prioritize aircraft control, climb performance, traffic, and collision avoidance. Pause instruction or secondary tasks if workload increases.

### AI.VIII.C.R2 - Collision Hazards

Clear before climbing turns and continue scanning above, below, and in the turn direction. The nose-high climb attitude can hide traffic and obstacles.

## Skills

### AI.VIII.C.S1 - Establish, Maintain, and Level Off From Climbs and Climbing Turns

Clear, set power, establish climb attitude, trim for airspeed, coordinate turns, hold heading/airspeed, and level off smoothly at the target altitude.

### AI.VIII.C.S2 - Analyze and Correct Common Errors

Identify whether the deviation is pitch, power, bank, rudder, trim, scan, or workload related; apply a small correction and verify the result outside and inside.

# Fly

## Setup

- Select a safe practice area, altitude, heading, and climb speed.
- Clear the area and brief traffic, terrain, weather, and target altitude.
- Establish straight-and-level flight before each climb; verify exchange of controls.

## Instructor Demonstration

1. Clear and establish the desired heading.
2. Apply climb power, set climb pitch, anticipate right rudder, and verify airspeed.
3. Trim after the climb stabilizes.
4. For a climbing turn, bank with coordinated rudder and add required pitch/power compensation.
5. Lead the level-off, lower pitch smoothly, adjust power, and trim.

## Learner Practice

Practice straight climbs and climbing turns in both directions, with target headings and altitudes. Require the learner to narrate outside attitude references, power, airspeed, rudder, and altitude trend.

## Analyze and Correct Common Errors

### Pitch/Power and Airspeed

**Recognize:** Airspeed chasing, overpitch, or failure to climb.

**Correct:** Re-establish attitude outside, verify performance, and adjust power/pitch smoothly.

### Coordination and Track

**Recognize:** Yaw, slip/skid, one wing low, or turn not developing.

**Correct:** Coordinate rudder with bank, reduce overcontrol, and use both wingtips/horizon.

### Level-Off

**Recognize:** Overshoot, altitude loss, low-G sensation, or excessive forward pressure.

**Correct:** Lead the level-off, lower pitch gradually, adjust power, and trim.

## Completion Standards

The applicant demonstrates while teaching straight climbs and climbing turns: outside scan, climb attitude, power, airspeed, trim, coordination, heading control, level-off, risk management, and common-error correction.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. Why does a climb require power?

[[answer:
A sustained climb requires excess power: power available must exceed power required for level flight.
]]

### 2. What controls climb airspeed?

[[answer:
Pitch attitude establishes the climb airspeed; power supports the climb, and trim relieves control pressure after stabilization.
]]

### 3. What changes in a climbing turn?

[[answer:
The airplane needs coordinated bank and additional pitch/power compensation for the loss of vertical lift, while maintaining airspeed and heading control.
]]

### 4. Name the supplied common errors.

[[answer:
Airspeed chasing, excessive elevator, rudder errors, yaw from inadequate right rudder, nose fixation/one wing low, uncoordinated climbing turns, slip reducing climb, unstable pitch/bank, exceeding capability, and poor level-off.
]]
