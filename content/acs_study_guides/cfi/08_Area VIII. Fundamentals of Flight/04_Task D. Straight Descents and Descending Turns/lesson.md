# Task D. Straight Descents and Descending Turns

**Area VIII - Fundamentals of Flight**

## Objective

To determine that the instructor applicant understands straight descents and descending turns, can apply that knowledge, manage associated risks, demonstrate the maneuvers while simultaneously providing effective instruction, and analyze and correct common errors.

## Purpose

Descents introduce energy management, reduced-power engine operation, glide awareness, and the relationship among pitch, power, airspeed, and rate of descent. Descending turns combine those skills with bank, coordination, and collision avoidance.

## Schedule / Equipment

- **Ground instruction:** 20 minutes.
- **Flight instruction:** 0.6-1.0 hour.
- Checklist, AFH/ACS, POH/AFM engine-operation guidance, and kneeboard.
- Whiteboard/model airplane and approved visuals.

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** Establish straight descents and descending turns, maintain target airspeed/heading/rate, use trim and appropriate power, apply carburetor heat or clear the engine as applicable, and recover smoothly to level flight.

**Instructor:** Use Binns' sequence of controls/trim, outside references, reduced power, descent forces, airspeed, downwash, engine considerations, glides, descending turns, and error correction.

## Completion Standards

The applicant maintains outside scan and coordinated flight, establishes and trims an appropriate descent, turns to assigned headings, maintains heading within **+/-10 degrees**, airspeed within **+/-10 knots**, and levels off within **+/-100 feet** while providing effective instruction.

### Objective

**Teach the learner to establish, maintain, and level off from straight descents and descending turns using outside visual references, appropriate power, pitch, trim, coordinated controls, engine management, and brief instrument cross-checks while maintaining airspeed, heading, traffic awareness, and situational awareness.**

### Purpose

Descents are an energy-management exercise.

A good descent is not simply “push the nose down.” We are deliberately changing the balance between **power, pitch, airspeed, and flightpath**.

The teaching story is:

**clear → reduce power → slow as needed → lower the nose to the descent attitude → trim → hold airspeed and heading → coordinate the turn if needed → manage the engine → lead the level-off → add power → raise the nose → retrim.**

---

### Start With the Big Idea — Why Does the Airplane Descend?

In straight-and-level flight, thrust and drag are balanced for the current airspeed.

When we reduce power, the airplane no longer has enough thrust to maintain the same altitude and airspeed combination.

In a descent, part of the airplane's weight acts along the flightpath and helps keep the airplane moving forward.

That is why an airplane can glide with little or no power.

The practical teaching point is:

**less power + the proper pitch attitude = a controlled descent.**

If we reduce power and do nothing else, the airplane will initially slow and the nose may tend to drop.

If we lower the nose too much, airspeed builds.

If we hold the nose too high, airspeed decays.

So the pilot must establish the correct **descent attitude** and then let the airplane stabilize.

---

### Lift-to-Drag and Best Glide

A glide is a power-off or reduced-power descent.

The airplane trades altitude for distance.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Lift_Drag_Max.png" alt="Lift-to-drag ratio curve showing maximum lift-to-drag ratio" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>Maximum L/D is the airplane's most aerodynamically efficient glide condition. Best-glide speed corresponds closely to this condition and gives the greatest distance for the altitude available in still air.</figcaption>
</figure>

At **L/D max**, the airplane gets its best aerodynamic efficiency.

That is the basis for **best glide**.

The important lesson is what happens if the pilot tries to “stretch” the glide:

**raising the nose does not create energy.**

It reduces airspeed, increases angle of attack, increases drag, and can actually shorten the glide.

So:

**never stretch a glide. Protect the target airspeed.**

---

### Three Useful Descent Types

The learner should understand that not every descent is flown the same way.

#### Partial-Power Descent

This is the normal way to lose altitude in routine flight.

Select:

- power,
- pitch,
- airspeed,
- and desired descent rate.

Then hold that combination.

#### Glide

A glide uses idle or little power.

Use the POH/AFM best-glide speed when maximum distance matters.

Pitch controls the airspeed.

Do not try to extend the glide with back pressure.

#### Minimum-Airspeed / Steep Approach Type Descent

A low-speed, power-assisted descent may be used when a steeper path is required, such as some short-field approaches.

That is a different energy condition and should not be confused with a normal glide.

---

### Straight Descent — Slow Before Lowering the Nose

For a normal training descent, I want the sequence to be deliberate.

First:

**reduce power smoothly while maintaining altitude.**

As power comes back, the airplane slows.

Use enough elevator back pressure to hold altitude while approaching the desired descent speed.

Then, just before reaching the target airspeed:

**relax the back pressure and lower the nose to the known descent attitude.**

That avoids one of the classic errors:

**dropping the nose first and accelerating through the desired descent speed.**

The sequence is:

**Power → Slow → Pitch → Trim.**

---

### Descent Sight Picture — Outside First

The descent should be established with the natural horizon as the primary reference.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Descent_Indications.png" alt="Outside and instrument indications during a stabilized descent" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>Use the lower nose-to-horizon sight picture to establish the descent, then briefly verify airspeed, altitude trend, VSI, heading, and coordination.</figcaption>
</figure>

The outside picture should show:

- nose lower than straight-and-level,
- wings level for a straight descent,
- stable visual attitude.

Then briefly verify:

- airspeed,
- altimeter decreasing,
- VSI showing descent,
- heading,
- coordination.

The scan remains:

**outside → quick instrument check → outside.**

Do not fly the descent by staring at the VSI or airspeed indicator.

---

### Pitch Controls Airspeed

Once power is fixed in the descent:

**pitch is the primary control for airspeed.**

If too fast:

**raise the nose slightly.**

If too slow:

**lower the nose slightly.**

Then wait.

Do not chase the airspeed indicator.

The correct flow is:

**change the attitude → let the airplane respond → verify → trim if needed.**

---

### Sound and Feel Matter

The learner should begin developing a feel for:

- airflow sound,
- engine sound,
- control pressure,
- seat pressure,
- and airplane response.

In a descent, reduced power changes the sound and feel of the airplane.

The goal is not to replace instruments.

The goal is to avoid becoming dependent on them.

A proficient pilot should often notice that airspeed is changing before looking inside.

---

### Trim

Once the airplane is stabilized at the desired descent airspeed:

**trim away the sustained elevator pressure.**

The sequence remains:

**Attitude → Power → Stabilize → Trim.**

Do not use trim to force the airplane into the descent.

Use the elevator first.

Then trim.

---

### Engine Management

Reduced-power descents also introduce engine-management considerations.

Follow the POH/AFM for the airplane being flown.

As applicable:

- use carburetor heat,
- clear the engine during prolonged low-power operation,
- monitor engine indications,
- avoid unnecessarily long idle-power descents when manufacturer guidance cautions against them.

For the fuel-injected C172S, carburetor heat is not applicable, but the larger teaching point remains:

**know the airplane-specific engine precautions for prolonged low-power descents.**

---

### Straight Descent — Maintain Heading and Coordination

A straight descent still requires:

- wings level,
- heading maintained,
- coordinated flight.

At reduced power, left-turning tendencies are much weaker.

That means the rudder pressure required in the descent may be significantly different from the climb.

Do not hold the same right-rudder pressure out of habit.

Use only the amount required to remain coordinated.

---

### Descending Turns — Same Descent, Now Add Bank

A descending turn combines the descent with coordinated bank.

There are two ways to enter:

1. establish the descent first, then turn, or
2. enter the descent and turn together.

For initial training, it is often easier to:

**establish the descent first → stabilize → then add the turn.**

To enter the turn:

- maintain the descent pitch picture,
- bank with coordinated aileron and rudder,
- lower the nose slightly if needed to preserve the target airspeed,
- establish the desired bank,
- trim as needed.

The key relationship is:

**bank changes the turn; pitch protects airspeed; rudder coordinates.**

---

### Why the Nose May Need to Lower in a Gliding Turn

When we bank, some lift is directed horizontally to turn the airplane.

That leaves less vertical lift available.

If the pilot simply rolls into a gliding turn and holds the same pitch attitude, airspeed may decrease.

So during entry:

**lower the nose slightly as needed to preserve the target airspeed.**

That is one of the common errors in this maneuver:

**banking into the turn but failing to lower pitch, then watching the airspeed decay.**

---

### Coordination Is Different With Low Power

In a climb, high power creates strong left-turning tendencies.

In a glide, those power-related tendencies are greatly reduced.

But **adverse yaw still exists**.

So coordinated rudder is still required during roll-in and rollout.

The learner needs to recognize that the amount and direction of rudder pressure required changes with:

- power,
- bank,
- airspeed,
- and turn direction.

Do not memorize a pedal position.

Use:

- outside yaw cues,
- seat pressure,
- and the slip/skid indicator.

---

### Do Not Skid or Cross-Control Near the Ground

A descending turn is exactly where poor coordination becomes dangerous.

A student who is low or overshooting may try to:

- pull,
- bank,
- and add excessive rudder.

That creates a skid and can lead to an accelerated stall/spin.

The rule is:

**bank the airplane to turn; rudder coordinates.**

If the geometry is no longer safe:

**reduce bank, correct the path, or go around when available.**

Do not cross-control to save a bad turn.

---

### Maintaining the Descending Turn

Once established:

- hold the pitch picture,
- hold the bank,
- maintain target airspeed,
- stay coordinated,
- keep scanning outside.

If the bank changes, airspeed and descent rate may also change.

Make small corrections.

Do not try to fix pitch and bank with large simultaneous inputs.

The outside references should remain primary.

---

### Level-Off — Lead the Altitude

Because the airplane has downward inertia, begin the level-off before the target altitude.

A useful starting technique is:

**lead by about 10% of the descent rate.**

Examples:

- 500 FPM descent → begin about **50 ft early**
- 800 FPM → about **80 ft early**
- 1,000 FPM → about **100 ft early**

This is a technique, not an ACS tolerance.

At the lead point:

1. smoothly add cruise power,
2. raise the nose toward the straight-and-level sight picture,
3. maintain wings level or roll out as appropriate,
4. allow the airplane to accelerate,
5. set the final cruise power,
6. trim.

Do not abruptly pull the nose up.

Do not wait until the target altitude to begin recovery.

---

### Level-Off From a Descending Turn

From a descending turn, two changes happen together:

- the nose comes up,
- the wings come level.

Initially, a learner may perform them in two steps.

With proficiency, the goal is:

**raise the nose and roll wings level smoothly so both arrive at the straight-and-level attitude at the target altitude.**

As power is added, rudder requirement changes again.

Coordinate through the whole transition.

---

### Risk Management — Distractions and Task Prioritization

Descents often occur while the pilot is also:

- changing frequencies,
- briefing an approach,
- configuring,
- navigating,
- looking for traffic,
- and managing checklists.

The priority remains:

**Aviate → Navigate → Communicate.**

If the airplane starts drifting in airspeed, heading, bank, or coordination:

**stop the secondary task and fly the airplane.**

---

### Risk Management — Collision Hazards

Under **14 CFR §91.113**, see-and-avoid remains a continuous responsibility.

Before a descending turn:

**clear in the direction of the turn and below the flightpath.**

During the descent:

- continue scanning,
- look for traffic below,
- consider terrain and obstacles,
- and do not let the nose-low attitude become an excuse to stare inside.

Descending turns can move the airplane through another aircraft's altitude quickly.

---

### FAR Considerations

**14 CFR §91.113** applies directly to collision avoidance and right-of-way responsibilities.

**14 CFR §91.119** is also relevant when practicing descents: maintain required minimum safe altitudes except where the regulation permits otherwise.

The maneuver should always be planned with enough altitude to recover safely.

---

### Common Errors

#### 1. Inadequate Clearing

**Error:** Failure to clear for traffic in the direction of turn or descent.

**Correction:** Clear before entry and maintain the outside scan throughout.

#### 2. Overly Steep Glide

**Error:** Inadequate back pressure during glide entry lets the nose drop too far.

**Correction:** Reduce power smoothly, control the deceleration, and lower the nose only to the required descent attitude.

#### 3. Lowering Pitch Before Slowing

**Error:** Nose goes down before reaching the approximate glide speed.

**Correction:** Reduce power and hold altitude first; then establish the descent attitude.

#### 4. Instrument-Only Glide

**Error:** Establishing or maintaining the glide from instruments.

**Correction:** Use the natural horizon and outside sight picture first, then verify inside.

#### 5. Poor Sensory Awareness

**Error:** Unable to sense changes in airspeed through sound and feel.

**Correction:** Teach engine sound, airflow noise, control pressure, and seat-of-the-pants cues.

#### 6. Chasing Airspeed

**Error:** Repeated pitch changes while staring at the ASI.

**Correction:** Set attitude, wait, verify, then make one small correction.

#### 7. Stretching the Glide

**Error:** Pulling back to reach farther.

**Correction:** Maintain target/best-glide airspeed and choose a reachable path.

#### 8. Slip or Skid in Gliding Turn

**Error:** Poor coordination or failure to recognize changed rudder forces at low power.

**Correction:** Coordinate with rudder and use outside yaw/seat cues plus the slip/skid indicator.

#### 9. Failure to Lower Pitch in Turn Entry

**Error:** Airspeed decreases during the gliding turn.

**Correction:** Lower pitch slightly as bank is established to preserve target speed.

#### 10. Excessive Rudder on Recovery

**Error:** Too much rudder while rolling wings level.

**Correction:** Relax rudder smoothly as aileron input is removed.

#### 11. Poor Straight-Glide Recovery

**Error:** Abrupt or inadequate pitch control during level-off.

**Correction:** Lead the altitude, add power, raise the nose smoothly, and trim.

#### 12. Cross-Control Near the Ground

**Error:** Using opposite controls to force a descending turn.

**Correction:** Stay coordinated; reduce bank or change the plan.

#### 13. Bank Drift

**Error:** Failing to maintain constant bank in the descending turn.

**Correction:** Use the outside bank reference and small aileron corrections.

---

### Instructor Demonstration

Teach it in this sequence:

1. Select a safe practice altitude, heading, target descent speed, rate, and level-off altitude.
2. Clear ahead, below, and in the direction of any turn.
3. From straight-and-level, **reduce power smoothly**.
4. Hold altitude while the airplane slows toward the desired descent speed.
5. Just before the target speed, **lower the nose to the descent attitude**.
6. Maintain heading and coordination.
7. Let the airspeed stabilize.
8. **Trim**.
9. Verify airspeed, altitude trend, VSI, heading, and coordination with quick instrument checks.
10. Demonstrate small pitch corrections for airspeed.
11. Demonstrate a glide and explain **best glide / L/D max**.
12. For a descending turn, establish the descent first.
13. Roll into the selected bank with coordinated aileron/rudder.
14. Lower pitch slightly as needed to preserve airspeed.
15. Hold bank, pitch, and coordination.
16. Repeat in both directions.
17. Lead the level-off by about **10% of the descent rate** as a starting technique.
18. Add cruise power smoothly.
19. Raise the nose toward straight-and-level while rolling wings level if turning.
20. Allow acceleration, set final cruise power, and retrim.

A concise teaching flow is:

**“Clear. Power back. Hold altitude and slow. Lower the nose to the descent picture. Let it stabilize. Trim. Pitch for airspeed. For the turn, bank and coordinate and lower the nose enough to hold the speed. Lead the level-off, add power, raise the nose, wings level, accelerate, trim.”**

## AI.VIII.D.K1 - Purpose of and Procedures for Straight Descents and Descending Turns

A straight descent is established by reducing power, allowing the nose to lower, setting the desired descent attitude/airspeed, trimming, and maintaining the desired heading and rate. A descending turn combines the descent with coordinated bank and requires appropriate pitch/ power compensation.

Procedure:

1. Clear the area and establish the target heading/altitude/airspeed.
2. Reduce power smoothly; anticipate the nose-down tendency.
3. Establish the descent pitch attitude and trim for airspeed.
4. Use the VSI/altimeter to verify the rate and altitude trend.
5. For a descending turn, bank with coordinated rudder and maintain airspeed/heading control.
6. Lead the level-off, add power, raise pitch smoothly, and trim.

### Glides

A **glide** is a power-off or reduced-power descent. Use the POH/AFM best-glide speed and configuration. Pitch controls airspeed; the selected landing area and geometry determine the desired path. Never raise the nose to stretch a glide; lower the nose to preserve airspeed and select the safest reachable area.

## AI.VIII.D.K2 - Flight Control and Trim Use

**Elevator** establishes descent attitude and airspeed. **Throttle** controls energy and descent rate when power is available. **Rudder** coordinates descending turns. **Ailerons** establish bank. **Trim** relieves pressure after the descent stabilizes.

Power reduction can cause the nose to drop because propeller downwash over the horizontal stabilizer decreases. Anticipate the change, establish the desired attitude, then trim. In a descending turn, maintain coordination and compensate for reduced vertical lift.

## AI.VIII.D.K3 - Pilot Visual References When Performing the Maneuver

Use the natural horizon and descending sight picture as primary references. The nose is lower than in level flight; in a descending turn, judge bank from the panel/horizon and wing relationship. Briefly verify airspeed, altitude, VSI, heading, and coordination with instruments.

For a glide, use outside terrain/landing-area references and the airspeed attitude. Maintain a broad scan rather than fixating on the desired landing point.

## AI.VIII.D.K4 - Integrated Flight Instruction

Teach the learner to establish attitude outside, verify performance inside, and return outside. Explain the expected effects of power reduction, downwash change, trim, bank, rudder, and engine precautions while preserving aircraft control and collision avoidance.

Practice both left and right descending turns, straight powered descents, and glides so the learner understands the difference between reduced-power descent and power-off energy management.

## AI.VIII.D.K5 - Common Errors Related to This Task

### 1. Inadequate Clearing

**Error:** Failure to clear for traffic in the turn direction or descent.

**Correction:** Clear before entry and keep scanning during the descent/turn.

### 2. Excessive Glide Angle

**Error:** Inadequate elevator back pressure during glide entry produces an overly steep glide.

**Correction:** Lower the nose only enough to establish the target airspeed; trim and stabilize.

### 3. Speed Before Pitch

**Error:** Lowering pitch before slowing to approximate glide speed.

**Correction:** Establish the target speed/attitude before settling into the glide.

### 4. Instrument-Only Glide

**Error:** Establishing/maintaining the glide solely by instruments.

**Correction:** Use the outside horizon and landing-area references, then briefly verify instruments.

### 5. Poor Sensory Awareness

**Error:** Inability to sense airspeed through sound and feel.

**Correction:** Develop awareness of control pressure, airflow sound, vibration, and aircraft response.

### 6. Chasing Airspeed

**Error:** Inability to stabilize the glide while chasing the airspeed indicator.

**Correction:** Set attitude, allow stabilization, then make small proportional corrections.

### 7. Stretching the Glide

**Error:** Applying back pressure to reach a distant landing point.

**Correction:** Lower pitch to preserve airspeed and select the safest reachable area.

### 8. Uncoordinated Gliding Turns

**Error:** Skidding/slipping and failing to recognize different rudder forces without power.

**Correction:** Coordinate with rudder and use the slip/skid indicator, sound, and seat-of-the-pants cues.

### 9. Failure to Lower Pitch in Turn Entry

**Error:** Airspeed decreases when entering a gliding turn.

**Correction:** Lower pitch as bank is established and maintain the target airspeed.

### 10. Excessive Rudder During Recovery

**Error:** Excessive rudder pressure during recovery from a gliding turn.

**Correction:** Relax rudder smoothly as the wings level; avoid an abrupt yaw.

### 11. Poor Straight-Glide Recovery

**Error:** Inadequate pitch control during recovery from a straight glide.

**Correction:** Raise pitch smoothly, add power as appropriate, prevent airspeed loss, and trim.

### 12. Low-Altitude Cross-Control

**Error:** Cross-controlling during gliding turns near the ground.

**Correction:** Maintain coordination and avoid steep/low turns; select a safer path or go around when available.

### 13. Bank Drift

**Error:** Failure to maintain constant bank during gliding turns.

**Correction:** Hold the outside bank reference and make small aileron corrections.

## Risk Management

### AI.VIII.D.R1 - Distractions and Situational Awareness

Prioritize aircraft control, airspeed, terrain/traffic, and the descent path. Simplify the task if workload rises.

### AI.VIII.D.R2 - Collision Hazards

Clear before turns and maintain an outside scan throughout descent and glide. Descending turns can move the airplane through traffic layers quickly.

## Skills

### AI.VIII.D.S1 - Establish, Maintain, and Level Off

Clear, reduce power, establish attitude/airspeed, trim, coordinate turns, maintain heading/rate, and level off smoothly with appropriate power and pitch.

# Fly

## Setup

- Select a safe practice area, altitude, heading, target airspeed, descent rate, and level-off altitude.
- Brief engine precautions, carburetor heat/engine clearing as applicable, traffic, terrain, and recovery.
- Establish straight-and-level flight before each descent or glide.

## Instructor Demonstration

1. Clear the area and reduce power smoothly.
2. Allow the nose to lower, establish the descent attitude/airspeed, and trim.
3. Verify heading, airspeed, altitude trend, and rate with brief instrument checks.
4. For a descending turn, bank with coordinated rudder and maintain the target airspeed/rate.
5. For a glide, establish best glide/POH speed, maintain outside landing-area scan, and avoid stretching.
6. Lead the level-off, add power, raise pitch smoothly, and trim.

## Learner Practice

Practice straight descents, descending turns in both directions, and glides. Require the learner to verbalize power, attitude, airspeed, trim, coordination, engine precautions, and level-off lead point.

## Analyze and Correct Common Errors

### Speed and Glide Stabilization

**Recognize:** Airspeed chasing, overly steep glide, or pitch changes that destabilize the descent.

**Correct:** Establish attitude, verify speed, trim, and use power/path control appropriately; never stretch the glide.

### Coordination and Bank

**Recognize:** Slip/skid, excessive rudder, cross-control, or changing bank.

**Correct:** Use coordinated aileron/rudder, maintain the outside bank reference, and reduce bank or recover when near the ground.

### Engine and Level-Off Management

**Recognize:** Forgotten carburetor heat/engine clearing, abrupt pitch, altitude loss, or low-G level-off.

**Correct:** Follow POH/AFM precautions, lead the level-off, add power smoothly, and maintain safe airspeed.

## Completion Standards

The applicant demonstrates while teaching straight descents, descending turns, and glides: outside scan, pitch/power/trim, airspeed/rate control, coordination, engine management, heading control, smooth level-off, risk management, and common-error correction.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. How is a descent initiated?

[[answer:
Reduce power, establish the nose-low descent attitude, adjust pitch for airspeed, and trim. Anticipate the nose-down effect of reduced power/downwash.
]]

### 2. How is a glide different from a powered descent?

[[answer:
A glide uses idle or no power and relies on pitch/airspeed and geometry for energy management. Use best-glide/POH speed and never stretch the glide by pulling.
]]

### 3. What changes in a descending turn?

[[answer:
Add coordinated bank and maintain airspeed while compensating for the changed lift components and turn forces. Lower pitch as needed when entering the gliding turn.
]]

### 4. Name the supplied AFH common errors.

[[answer:
Inadequate clearing, excessive glide angle, lowering pitch before slowing, instrument-only glide, poor sensory awareness, airspeed chasing, stretching the glide, uncoordinated gliding turns, failure to lower pitch in turn entry, excessive rudder on recovery, poor straight-glide recovery, low-altitude cross-control, and bank drift.
]]
