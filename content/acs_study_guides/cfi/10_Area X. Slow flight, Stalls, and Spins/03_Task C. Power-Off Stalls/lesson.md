# Task C. Power-Off Stalls

**Area X — Slow Flight, Stalls, and Spins**

## Objective

Determine that the instructor applicant understands power-off stalls, applies that knowledge, manages associated risks, demonstrates the required skills while providing effective instruction, and analyzes and corrects common errors.

## Purpose

A power-off stall develops recognition, prevention, and recovery skills for an inadvertent stall during an **approach to landing**, a **turn from base to final**, or an attempt to **stretch a glide**. The maneuver reinforces that a stall results from exceeding **critical angle of attack (AOA)** and that recovery begins by reducing AOA, regardless of airspeed or attitude.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested Schedule:** **25–35 minutes ground**, **25–35 minutes flight**, and **10 minutes debrief**.

**Equipment:** C172S POH/checklist, CFI ACS, AFH, AC 61-67C, model airplane or whiteboard, stall-recovery diagrams, and maneuver card.

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** Explain stall aerodynamics, approach-to-landing risks, warning cues, recovery priorities, and C172S limitations; demonstrate straight and turning stalls while teaching; recognize and correct errors.

**Instructor:** Brief impending versus full stall, altitude floor, positive control transfer, automation status, and recovery standard; demonstrate sight/sound/feel cues and AOA-first recovery; prevent unsafe yaw, bank, or secondary stall.

## Completion Standards

The applicant clears, configures as specified, establishes a coordinated stabilized descent, transitions smoothly to the stall attitude, maintains heading **±10°** or bank **not exceeding 20°, ±5°**, acknowledges the first cue, recovers at the evaluator-specified point, configures as recommended, accelerates to $V_X$ or $V_Y$, and returns to the assigned altitude, heading, and airspeed while providing effective instruction.

### Objective

**Teach the learner to recognize, avoid, and recover from a power-off stall by understanding that the stall is caused by exceeding the wing’s critical angle of attack, by identifying the sight, sound, and feel cues of an impending or full stall, and by performing a coordinated recovery that first reduces angle of attack, then restores controlled flight, configures the airplane as recommended, accelerates to** **V<sub>X</sub>** **or** **V<sub>Y</sub>**, **and returns to the assigned flightpath while providing effective instruction.**

### Applicable FARs

There is no single FAR that “describes” the maneuver, but several regulations directly apply to how we teach and perform it:

- **14 CFR § 91.9** — comply with AFM/POH operating limitations and procedures.
- **14 CFR § 91.103** — know the airplane, weather, and performance before flight.
- **14 CFR § 91.113** — see and avoid; collision avoidance remains continuous during the maneuver.
- **14 CFR § 91.119** — select an altitude and area that allow the maneuver to be conducted safely.

For ACS purposes, the task must be started at an altitude that allows completion **no lower than 1,500 feet AGL** in ASEL.

---

### Teaching Story

The teaching story for a power-off stall is straightforward:

**This maneuver simulates an approach-to-landing stall.**  
We configure the airplane like we are approaching to land, we let the wing reach critical angle of attack, and then we recover the right way.

The real lesson is not “how to make the airplane stall.”  
The real lesson is:

- how stalls happen on approach,
- how to recognize them early,
- how to avoid the base-to-final trap,
- and how to recover correctly if we do get there.

The key teaching line is:

**A stall is caused by excessive angle of attack.**  
**Recovery begins by reducing angle of attack.**

That has to be the anchor point for the whole lesson.

---

### What This Maneuver Represents

A power-off stall is intended to simulate the kind of stall most likely to happen when the pilot is trying to land.

That could be:

- a normal approach where the pilot lets the airplane get too slow,
- a base-to-final overshoot where the pilot increases bank and back pressure,
- a glide that gets low and the pilot tries to stretch it,
- or a go/no-go indecision where the pilot keeps pulling instead of unloading or going around.

So when I teach this maneuver, I tell the student:

**“Think landing. Think low power. Think high drag. Think increasing pitch. Think the temptation to hold the airplane off or rescue the glide. That is where this stall lives.”**

---

### C172S POH References and Limits

The C172S gives us several useful references for this lesson.

#### Maneuver limits

The airplane is certificated in the **normal category** for stalls, except whip stalls.  
The POH also notes:

- **Stalls (except whip stalls): entry by slow deceleration**
- **Abrupt use of the controls is prohibited above 105 KIAS**

So the entry is not aggressive.  
We do not jerk it into a stall.  
We decelerate and smoothly increase pitch.

#### Flap operating limits

For the C172S:

- **Flaps 10°: below 110 KIAS**
- **Flaps 20° and 30°: below 85 KIAS**

That matters because the local training procedure uses full flaps.

#### Stall warning system

The C172S uses a **pneumatic stall warning horn**.  
It normally activates **5 to 10 knots above the stall** in all configurations.

That means the horn is an important cue, but not the only cue.

#### Stall speeds

Relevant C172S power-off stall speed references at maximum weight include approximately:

- **Flaps up, power off: 53 KCAS**
- **Flaps down, power off: 48 KCAS**

The full POH table also shows stall speed rising with bank angle and changing slightly with CG.

#### Recovery altitude loss

The POH notes that **altitude loss during a stall recovery may be as much as 230 feet**.

That matters for risk management and for why we do this maneuver with plenty of altitude.

### The Aerodynamics We Have to Teach

A stall happens because the wing exceeds **critical angle of attack**.

It is not caused by one magic airspeed.

That means a stall can happen:

- at low airspeed,
- at a higher-than-expected airspeed,
- in a climb,
- in a descent,
- in a turn,
- with power on,
- or with power off.

Airspeed is only a clue.  
AOA is the cause.

As angle of attack increases:

- lift increases up to a point,
- induced drag rises,
- control feel becomes softer,
- buffet may begin,
- the stall horn may sound,
- and then at critical AOA, the wing can no longer sustain attached airflow.

After that:

- lift drops,
- drag increases sharply,
- and the airplane stalls.

That is why the first recovery step must always be:

**Reduce AOA.**

---

### Factors That Affect Stall Behavior

#### Configuration

Flaps change camber, lift, drag, and pitch attitude.  
In the landing configuration, the airplane will stall at a lower 1G indicated airspeed than in the clean configuration, but it will also carry more drag and feel more like an actual landing situation.

#### Weight

A heavier airplane requires more lift, which means it reaches the necessary AOA at a higher stall speed.

#### Center of gravity

A forward CG generally increases stability and recovery tendency, but it also tends to increase stall speed and control force.  
An aft CG generally lowers stall speed somewhat, but it reduces longitudinal stability and can make recovery worse.

#### Load factor and bank

This is critical for the power-off stall lesson because of the base-to-final scenario.

As bank increases in level flight or in a tightening turn:

- load factor increases,
- stall speed increases,
- and the margin to the stall decreases.

That is why an overshooting base-to-final turn becomes dangerous so quickly.

#### Yaw and coordination

An uncoordinated stall is far more dangerous than a coordinated stall.

If one wing is more stalled than the other, especially with yaw present, the airplane may begin autorotation.

That is spin entry.

So a major teaching point is:

**High AOA plus yaw is the combination we fear most.**

---

### Recognizing the Stall

We teach stall recognition using **sight, sound, and feel**.

#### Impending stall cues

- Stall horn
- Buffet
- Mushy controls
- Decreasing control effectiveness
- High pitch / high-AOA sight picture
- Increasing back pressure
- Sink developing even as pitch increases

#### Full stall cues

- Stall break
- Nose drop
- Distinct loss of lift
- Wing drop if uncoordinated
- More obvious buffet or sink

I teach the student to say:

- **“Imminent stall”** at the stall warning horn or other first valid cue.
- **“Stall”** when the actual stall occurs, if the evaluator has required a full stall.

That verbalization helps the student tie the cues to the recovery decision.

---

### Stall Warning Limitations

The horn is useful, but it has limitations.

The ACS specifically expects the applicant to understand the **range and limitations of stall warning indicators**.

So the student must know:

- the horn may come on earlier or later depending on conditions,
- turbulence can change the margin quickly,
- the horn is not the only cue,
- and some normal operations, especially near landing, may briefly flirt with a warning margin if mishandled.

The right response to an unexpected stall warning in normal flight is not denial.

The right response is:

**Reduce AOA and fix the energy state.**

---

### The Risk Story

The main risk story for power-off stalls is **approach-to-landing loss of control**.

The classic accident chain is:

1. The airplane gets low, slow, or wide.
2. The pilot tries to save the approach.
3. Bank increases.
4. Back pressure increases.
5. Rudder is misused.
6. The airplane stalls.
7. The airplane rolls or yaws.
8. There is not enough altitude to recover.

That is why this lesson must connect directly to:

- **go-around judgment,**
- **stabilized approach discipline,**
- **not stretching a glide,**
- **not forcing the turn,**
- and **coordination.**

---

### Generic Stall Recovery Template

The generic recovery template is useful because it teaches priorities in the right order.

1. **Disconnect wing leveler or autopilot**, if installed.
2. **Pitch nose-down** until impending stall indications are eliminated.
3. **Trim nose-down as needed.**
4. **Bank — wings level.**
5. **Thrust/power — as needed.**
6. **Retract speed brakes/spoilers**, if installed.
7. **Return to the desired flight path.**

For the C172S, that translates to:

- reduce AOA,
- maintain coordination,
- level the wings,
- apply full power,
- retract flaps incrementally,
- establish climb,
- accelerate to **V<sub>X</sub>** or **V<sub>Y</sub>** as appropriate,
- and return to the assigned altitude, heading, and airspeed.

The order matters.

**Power does not unstall the wing.**  
**Reducing AOA does.**

---

### Local C172S Training Procedure

This is the local procedure you provided, and it works well as a teaching profile.

#### Entry

1. **Clearing turn at or above 1,500 feet AGL**.
2. **Reduce power to 1800 RPM.**
3. **Extend full flaps**:
   - below **110 KIAS** for **10°**
   - below **85 KIAS** for **20° and 30°**
4. Establish a **1500 RPM / 70 KIAS descent**.
5. Set **power to idle**.
6. Apply increasing back pressure to maintain altitude as the airplane decelerates.
7. Announce **“imminent stall”** at the stall warning horn.
8. Announce **“stall”** when the stall occurs.

This should feel and look like an approach or landing picture, not a random nose-high pitch-up.

#### Recovery

1. **Reduce pitch / unload the wing**, apply **full power**, and level the wings with coordinated rudder and aileron.
2. **Retract flaps to 20°** and establish the climb pitch attitude.
3. **Retract flaps to 10° accelerating through 55 KIAS** and trim.
4. **Retract flaps to 0° accelerating through 60 KIAS** and trim.
5. Stabilize climb-out at **V<sub>Y</sub> = 74 KIAS**.
6. Level off as briefed.

If obstacle clearance is a factor, accelerate to **V<sub>X</sub>** as appropriate.  
If not, **V<sub>Y</sub>** is the normal target after recovery.

### How I Would Teach It in Real Time

I would talk through it like this:

**“Clear the area first. Pick an altitude that gives us plenty of room and keeps us above the ACS floor. We’re simulating an approach-to-landing stall, so I want a landing configuration and a stabilized approach picture. Reduce power, extend flaps within their limits, and establish the descent. Now power comes to idle, and as airspeed bleeds off, smoothly increase pitch. Stay coordinated. Keep the heading if we’re straight, or the assigned bank if we’re turning. The first cue is important—horn, buffet, mushiness—call it out. If we’re doing a full stall, continue until the wing actually stalls. Recovery starts with reducing AOA. Unload first. Then full power, wings level, coordinated rudder, flap cleanup in stages, accelerate, establish the climb, and return to the assigned flight path.”**

That is a speakable version of the maneuver and it keeps the priorities correct.

---

### Turning Power-Off Stalls

The turning version is where the maneuver becomes more operationally relevant.

For the ACS, if the evaluator wants a turning stall:

- maintain the specified bank,
- **do not exceed 20°**, and
- hold bank within **±5°**.

The point of the turning version is not to show off.  
The point is to reinforce how little extra load factor and yaw margin we have when the airplane is already slow and configured for landing.

I teach the student to be disciplined here:

- coordinated rudder,
- no skidding,
- no tightening the turn with rudder,
- and no chasing the nose with excessive bank.

---

### Recovery Priorities

These are the priorities I want the student to say back to me:

1. **Reduce AOA.**
2. **Maintain coordination.**
3. **Level the wings.**
4. **Apply maximum allowable power.**
5. **Configure as recommended.**
6. **Establish climb and accelerate to V<sub>X</sub> or V<sub>Y</sub>.**
7. **Return to the assigned altitude, heading, and airspeed.**

And I emphasize what not to do:

- do not try to power out of the stall without unloading,
- do not level a wing before reducing AOA,
- do not yank the airplane back into a climb and create a **secondary stall**,
- and do not rush flap cleanup.

---

### Common Errors

These are the common errors I would emphasize during instruction:

#### Before the stall

- Failure to clear the area
- Failure to use the specified configuration
- Poor airspeed control during setup
- Uncoordinated flight
- Fixation inside the cockpit
- Failure to establish the stabilized descent

#### During stall entry

- Abrupt pitch increase instead of smooth deceleration and pitch change
- Letting heading drift in the straight-ahead version
- Exceeding the specified bank in the turning version
- Skidding with inside rudder
- Failing to recognize the first cue

#### During recovery

- Delayed recovery
- Adding power before reducing AOA
- Trying to level the wings before unloading
- Inadequate right rudder with power application
- Retracting flaps too quickly
- Pulling too hard after the break and causing a secondary stall
- Excessive altitude loss
- Failure to return to the assigned altitude, heading, and airspeed

---

### Environmental Factors

The ACS expects us to teach environmental effects on power-off stalls.

#### Turbulence

Turbulence can momentarily increase angle of attack and reduce stall margin.  
A wing can be pushed closer to critical AOA without much warning.

#### Microbursts / wind shear

A sudden loss of airspeed or performance on approach can tempt the pilot to pull.  
That is exactly the wrong response if it drives AOA higher.

#### High density altitude

High density altitude reduces climb performance.  
The wing still stalls at critical AOA, but the airplane may not recover with the same climb energy or margin.

So I tell the student:

**“Recovery still begins the same way—reduce AOA—but don’t expect sea-level climb performance at high density altitude.”**

---

### Collision Hazards and Distractions

This maneuver creates workload.  
That means the student must still:

- clear before entry,
- keep scanning,
- maintain orientation,
- and prioritize aircraft control.

If traffic, terrain, or confusion becomes a factor:

**stop teaching and aviate first.**

That directly addresses the ACS risk items on:

- collision hazards,
- distractions,
- task prioritization,
- situational awareness,
- and disorientation.

---

### Instructor Emphasis / Bottom Line

At the end of the lesson, I want the student to leave with these points:

1. A power-off stall simulates an **approach-to-landing stall**.
2. A stall happens because the wing exceeds **critical AOA**.
3. A stall can happen at **any airspeed**.
4. Bank, load factor, yaw, weight, CG, and configuration change the conditions under which the stall occurs.
5. The most dangerous real-world version is often the **uncoordinated base-to-final stall/spin**.
6. The first recovery step is always **reduce AOA**.
7. Then: **coordinate, wings level, power, configure, climb, and return to the flight path.**

My simple summary is:

**“Approach picture. Critical AOA. Recognize it early. Reduce AOA first. Then power, cleanup, climb, and reset.”**

## K1 — Purpose and Procedures

The power-off stall simulates an inadvertent stall during an approach, descending turn, or glide. Configure as assigned, stabilize at a normal approach airspeed and descent, then smoothly increase pitch to induce an impending or full stall. Maintain coordination and the assigned heading or bank until the evaluator-directed recovery point.

	<figcaption>Stabilized approach → increasing AOA → warning or full stall → AOA reduction → coordinated recovery.</figcaption>
</figure>

The first indication is always **called out**, even when the evaluator directs continuation to a full stall.

## K2 — Stall Aerodynamics in Various Configurations

A stall occurs when the wing exceeds **critical AOA**. It may occur at any airspeed, attitude, or power setting. Published stall speeds apply only to a specified **weight, CG, configuration, load factor, and coordinated condition**.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
	<img src="/knowledge/Critical_AOA.png" alt="Airfoil airflow separating as critical angle of attack is exceeded" loading="lazy">
	<figcaption>Reducing AOA below critical restores attached airflow; airspeed alone does not unstall the wing.</figcaption>
</figure>

- **Flaps** change camber, lift, drag, pitch, and critical AOA.
- **Higher weight** and **increased load factor** raise the indicated stall speed.
- **Forward CG** generally raises stall speed and control force; **aft CG** reduces stability and recovery margin.
- **Bank** in a level/turning entry increases load factor and stall speed.
- **Yaw or a skid** can cause one wing to stall more deeply and initiate autorotation.
- **Low power** reduces propeller airflow and changes pitch/control effectiveness.

## K3 — Stall Characteristics and Recognition

The C172S has conventional stall characteristics and an aural horn that normally sounds **5–10 knots above the stall** in all configurations. The horn is only one cue and is not infallible.

**Impending-stall cues:**

- Stall horn or other installed warning
- Buffet or vibration
- Increasing elevator displacement and changing pressure
- Reduced control effectiveness and slower response
- Changing airflow sound
- High AOA sight picture

**Full-stall cues:**

- Critical AOA exceeded
- Uncommanded nose-down pitch that cannot be readily arrested
- Possible uncommanded roll
- High descent rate, which may be especially prominent in a power-off 1G stall

Training-airplane wings commonly stall root-first, preserving some aileron effectiveness. Even so, do not attempt to level a dropped wing before reducing AOA; downward aileron can deepen the stall on that wing.

## K4 — Causes and Prevention

Power-off stalls commonly develop from:

- **Slow, unstable approach** or failure to monitor energy
- **Pitching up to stretch a glide**
- **Excessive bank or load factor** near the ground
- **Skidding base-to-final correction** after overshooting
- **Abrupt control input**
- **Distraction, fixation, or delayed go-around**
- **Wind shear, turbulence, microburst, or high density altitude**
- **Configuration or trim mismanagement**

Prevent the stall with a stabilized approach, coordinated turns, correct airspeed/configuration, early go-around, and immediate response to warning. Never use inside rudder and opposite aileron to force an overshot base-to-final turn.

## K5 — Fundamentals of Stall Recovery

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
	<img src="/knowledge/Stall_Recovery_Template.png" alt="FAA stall recovery template beginning with automation disconnect and angle-of-attack reduction" loading="lazy">
	<figcaption>Recovery priority: manual control, reduce AOA, restore roll control, add power, configure, and return to the flightpath.</figcaption>
</figure>

1. **Disconnect autopilot or wing leveler**, if equipped.
2. **Reduce AOA** with sufficient nose-down elevator and maintain that input until the warning is eliminated.
3. **Level the wings** after AOA reduction while maintaining coordination.
4. **Add power as needed**, promptly and smoothly, controlling yaw and pitch.
5. **Configure as recommended**; in the C172S, retract flaps incrementally after positive control/climb is established.
6. **Return to the desired flightpath** without a secondary stall or excessive airspeed.

[[highlight:
Recovery Priority |
**AOA first.** Power cannot unstall a wing, and aggressive roll control before AOA reduction can aggravate the stall.
]]

## K6 — Common Errors

The supplied FAA common-error set is taught as four diagnostic groups:

- **Setup/recognition:** inadequate clearing, instrument overreliance, abrupt entry, missed cues, delayed impending-stall recovery, and wrong bank.
- **Coordination/development:** poor rudder coordination or recovering before critical AOA during a required full stall.
- **Recovery order:** automation connected, AOA not prioritized, nose-down input released too soon, wings or power used before AOA reduction, or wings not leveled afterward.
- **Energy/orientation:** secondary stall, excessive forward pressure, excessive speed, or failure to return to the assigned flightpath/ATC instruction.

## Risk Management

### Inadvertent Stall, Spin, and Loss of Control

**Hazard:** Low-energy approach combined with excessive AOA, load factor, or yaw.  
**Recognition:** Unstable speed/path, overshoot, skid, warning, buffet, or wing drop.  
**Consequence:** Stall/spin at an altitude too low for recovery.  
**Mitigation:** Stabilized-approach criteria, coordinated turns, conservative bank, early go-around, and no attempt to stretch a glide.

### Warning Indicators and Normal Operations

**Hazard:** Ignoring or normalizing a horn/buffet, or relying on one device.  
**Recognition:** Warning during approach, flare, configuration change, or training.  
**Consequence:** Critical AOA exceedance.  
**Mitigation:** Respond to the earliest cue with AOA reduction; integrate sight, sound, feel, control response, and installed indications.

### Recovery Errors and Related Stalls

**Hazard:** Power/roll before AOA reduction, abrupt pullout, or uncoordinated recovery.  
**Recognition:** Warning persists, wing remains dropped, yaw increases, or warning returns.  
**Consequence:** Spin, accelerated stall, cross-control stall, or secondary stall.  
**Mitigation:** Follow the recovery order, maintain nose-down input until warning ends, then recover smoothly.

### Environment, Collision, and Distraction

**Hazard:** Turbulence/wind shear changes AOA; high density altitude reduces performance; training fixation interrupts scan.  
**Recognition:** Uncommanded speed/AOA changes, poor climb, traffic conflict, or lost orientation.  
**Consequence:** Stall, collision, or insufficient recovery altitude.  
**Mitigation:** Use suitable conditions and area, clear continuously, maintain an altitude gate, and pause teaching whenever aircraft control requires it.

## Scenario Application

**Situation:** The airplane overshoots final. The learner applies inside rudder and opposite aileron while pulling to keep the nose from dropping.

**Discuss / Decide:** What aerodynamic condition is developing and what is the safe operational response?

**Desired Learner Outcome:** Recognize a skidding, cross-controlled, high-AOA turn with spin potential. **Unload, coordinate, and go around** rather than tightening or stretching the approach.

# Fly

## Setup

- **Clear:** Complete clearing turns and continue traffic scan.
- **Altitude:** Select enough altitude to complete recovery above **1,500 feet AGL**; allow for the POH-noted potential recovery loss of up to **230 feet** under its test conditions plus a substantial training margin.
- **Automation:** Autopilot/wing leveler **OFF**; positive exchange of controls briefed.
- **Configuration:** Approach or landing configuration as specified; C172S gear is fixed.
- **Limits / references:** $V_{FE}$ **110 KIAS at 10°**, **85 KIAS beyond 10°**; normal approach flaps 30° **60–70 KIAS**; short-field reference **61 KIAS**.
- **Stall references:** Approximately **48 KIAS flaps up** and **40 KIAS flaps 30°** at 2,550 lb, 1G; actual cue and AOA control.
- **Recovery:** Evaluator specifies **impending or full stall** and **straight or turning** entry.

## Instructor Demonstration

1. Complete the before-landing/configuration flow and establish the assigned **approach or landing configuration** within $V_{FE}$.
2. Stabilize a coordinated descent at the selected normal approach speed.
3. For a turning stall, establish and hold the specified bank **not exceeding 20°**.
4. Smoothly transition from the approach attitude to a pitch attitude that induces the stall.
5. Maintain heading **±10°** or bank **±5°** and coordination.
6. Call the first cue: **“stall warning”** or **“buffet.”**
7. Recover at the first indication or continue to the full stall, as directed.
8. **AOA:** Disconnect automation if needed; apply/maintain nose-down elevator until warning is eliminated.
9. **Wings:** Level after AOA reduction; maintain coordination.
10. **Power:** Apply full/maximum allowable power smoothly; control yaw and pitch.
11. **Configure:** Retract flaps incrementally per the airplane procedure; establish positive climb.
12. **Climb:** Accelerate to **$V_X$ 62 KIAS at sea level (67 KIAS at 10,000 feet)** or **$V_Y$ 74 KIAS at sea level (72 KIAS at 10,000 feet)**, as appropriate.
13. Return to the assigned altitude, heading, and airspeed.

## Learner Practice

Practice impending stalls before full stalls, then straight before turning entries. Require the learner to verbalize **configuration, stabilized descent, first cue, AOA reduction, warning eliminated, wings level, power, configuration, climb speed, and return path**.

## Analyze and Correct Common Errors

1. **Failure to adequately clear the area** — **Recognize:** entry begins without complete traffic/terrain scan. **Correct:** discontinue, clear, and restart.
2. **Over-reliance on the airspeed indicator and slip-skid indicator while excluding other cues after recovery** — **Recognize:** prolonged head-down fixation. **Correct:** restore outside attitude/traffic scan and integrate sight, sound, feel, and brief instrument checks.
3. **Inadvertent accelerated stall by pulling too fast on the controls during a power-off or power-on stall entry** — **Recognize:** abrupt G/load rise or early warning. **Correct:** unload and reenter with a smooth AOA increase.
4. **Inability to recognize an impending stall condition** — **Recognize:** horn, buffet, mushy controls, or airflow change goes uncalled. **Correct:** terminate/recover and review cues before repeating.
5. **Failure to take timely action to prevent a full stall during the conduct of impending stalls** — **Recognize:** warning progresses to break. **Correct:** reduce AOA immediately at the first cue.
6. **Failure to maintain a constant bank angle during turning stalls** — **Recognize:** bank wanders or exceeds 20°. **Correct:** smooth coordinated control; discontinue an unstable entry.
7. **Failure to maintain proper coordination with the rudder throughout the stall and recovery** — **Recognize:** ball displaced, yaw, or wing drop. **Correct:** center yaw with rudder; do not force heading.
8. **Recovering before reaching the critical AOA when practicing the full stall maneuver** — **Recognize:** recovery starts at warning despite full-stall direction. **Correct:** acknowledge the cue, remain coordinated, and continue only as directed and safe.
9. **Not disconnecting the wing leveler or autopilot, if equipped, prior to reducing AOA** — **Recognize:** automation remains engaged or resists control. **Correct:** disconnect immediately and establish manual control.
10. **Recovery is attempted without recognizing the importance of pitch control and AOA** — **Recognize:** pilot prioritizes altitude, power, or heading. **Correct:** make the first call and action **reduce AOA**.
11. **Not maintaining a nose-down control input until the stall warning is eliminated** — **Recognize:** warning persists or returns. **Correct:** maintain sufficient forward pressure until airflow is restored.
12. **Pilot attempts to level the wings before reducing AOA** — **Recognize:** aggressive aileron with stalled wing. **Correct:** reduce AOA first, then roll level.
13. **Pilot attempts to recover with power before reducing AOA** — **Recognize:** power added while AOA remains critical. **Correct:** reduce AOA first; add power as needed afterward.
14. **Failure to roll wings level after AOA reduction and stall warning is eliminated** — **Recognize:** bank remains and altitude loss grows. **Correct:** level with coordinated aileron/rudder after the wing is flying.
15. **Inadvertent secondary stall during recovery** — **Recognize:** warning/buffet returns during pullout. **Correct:** reduce AOA again; accelerate before increasing load.
16. **Excessive forward-elevator pressure during recovery resulting in low or negative G load** — **Recognize:** abrupt nose drop or light/negative loading. **Correct:** use only enough input to eliminate warning.
17. **Excessive airspeed buildup during recovery** — **Recognize:** steep nose-low attitude or speed trend. **Correct:** use measured AOA reduction and smoothly return to the flightpath after warning ends.
18. **Losing situational awareness and failing to return to desired flightpath or follow ATC instructions** — **Recognize:** altitude, heading, traffic, or clearance forgotten. **Correct:** aviate, orient, comply, and resume instruction only when stable.

## Completion Standards

Configure and stabilize; maintain coordination and assigned heading/bank; acknowledge the first cue; recover at the evaluator-directed point using **AOA first**; configure and accelerate to $V_X$ or $V_Y$; complete above **1,500 feet AGL**; return to assigned conditions; and teach/correct effectively.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. What real-world conditions does a power-off stall represent?

[[answer:
An inadvertent stall during an approach, descending base-to-final turn, or attempt to stretch a glide, usually at low power and in an approach or landing configuration.
]]

### 2. What causes the stall?

[[answer:
Exceeding critical AOA. Airspeed, bank, weight, CG, configuration, power, and coordination affect when and how that AOA is reached.
]]

### 3. What is the required first recovery action?

[[answer:
Disconnect automation if equipped and reduce AOA with enough nose-down control to eliminate the warning. Roll and power do not replace AOA reduction.
]]

### 4. What are the straight and turning entry standards?

[[answer:
Maintain heading ±10° in straight flight, or a specified bank not exceeding 20° and within ±5° in turning flight, while remaining coordinated.
]]

### 5. What C172S approach and flap speeds apply?

[[answer:
Normal approach with flaps 30° is 60–70 KIAS; short-field approach is 61 KIAS. VFE is 110 KIAS at 10° and 85 KIAS beyond 10°.
]]

### 6. How is an impending-stall demonstration different from a full stall?

[[answer:
For an impending stall, recover immediately at the first warning. For a full stall, call the first warning but continue in coordinated flight until critical AOA is exceeded and the full-stall cue occurs, then recover.
]]

### 7. Why must AOA be reduced before leveling a dropped wing?

[[answer:
Roll control improves after attached airflow is restored. Aileron applied while stalled can increase AOA and drag on the downward-deflected wing and aggravate the roll.
]]

### 8. What causes a secondary stall?

[[answer:
Increasing AOA too rapidly during recovery before sufficient airspeed and control effectiveness return. Reduce AOA again and recover smoothly.
]]
