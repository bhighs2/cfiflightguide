# Task G. Elevator Trim Stall Demonstration

**Area X — Slow Flight, Stalls, and Spins**

## Objective

Determine that the applicant understands elevator trim stalls, applies that knowledge, manages associated risks, demonstrates the required skills, and provides effective instruction.

## Purpose

This demonstration shows how **nose-up landing trim**, sudden go-around power, propeller slipstream, thrust-line effects, and high workload can produce an uncommanded pitch-up toward a power-on stall. The operational lesson is to anticipate control force, establish go-around pitch immediately, coordinate, configure, and trim only after control is secure.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested:** **20–30 minutes ground**, **15–20 minutes demonstration**, **10 minutes debrief**.  
**Equipment:** C172S POH/checklist, CFI ACS, AFH, AC 61-67C, elevator-trim diagram, and maneuver card.

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** Explain trim-stall aerodynamics and go-around risk; demonstrate recognition/recovery while teaching avoidance.  
**Instructor:** Brief altitude, control force, recovery point, and positive control transfer; guard pitch, yaw, and flap retraction.

## Completion Standards

Clear; complete above **3,000 feet AGL**; establish landing configuration, normal glide, and trim; apply maximum allowable power as in a go-around; acknowledge the first cue; recover as directed; adjust trim; return to the desired flightpath; and teach avoidance and errors.

### Objective

**Teach the learner to recognize, avoid, and recover from an elevator trim stall by understanding how nose-up landing trim combined with go-around power can produce a rapid pitch-up, strong left-turning tendency, increasing angle of attack, and potential stall/spin if positive control is not maintained.**

### Purpose

The elevator trim stall is really a **go-around control lesson**.

The airplane is established on approach:

- slow,
- configured for landing,
- trimmed nose-up,
- and stabilized at normal glide speed.

Then maximum allowable power is applied for a go-around.

That power application changes the pitching forces on the airplane and increases the left-turning tendencies. If the existing nose-up trim is not immediately opposed with forward elevator pressure, the nose can rise rapidly, airspeed can decay, angle of attack can increase toward critical, and the airplane can stall.

The sequence is:

**Nose-up landing trim + go-around power → rapid pitch-up + left yaw → increasing AOA → stall/spin risk.**

The operational lesson is:

**control pitch first, maintain coordination, establish the go-around attitude, then trim away the pressure.**

---

### Applicable FARs

There is no regulation that prescribes an elevator trim stall demonstration step by step, but the maneuver is conducted within normal operating rules:

- **14 CFR §91.9** — comply with the airplane’s operating limitations and POH/AFM procedures.
- **14 CFR §91.103** — know the airplane, performance, limitations, weather, and practice area before flight.
- **14 CFR §91.113** — maintain continuous see-and-avoid vigilance.
- **14 CFR §91.119** — use an altitude that provides adequate recovery margin.

For the CFI ACS, the entry altitude must allow the maneuver to be completed **no lower than 3,000 feet AGL**.

---

### Start With the Real-World Scenario

Picture a normal landing approach.

The airplane is slowed and configured for landing. The pilot has trimmed nose-up so the airplane holds the approach attitude without excessive control pressure.

Now something changes:

- the runway becomes occupied,
- spacing collapses,
- the approach becomes unstable,
- or the pilot simply decides to go around.

Maximum allowable power is applied.

That is when the trim condition that was comfortable on final suddenly becomes a problem.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Elevator_Trim_Stall_Go_Around.png" alt="Go-around without retrimming causing excessive pitch-up" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>Go-around power applied while the airplane remains trimmed nose-up for approach can produce a rapid pitch-up unless positive elevator control is maintained.</figcaption>
</figure>

The nose wants to rise sharply.

At the same time, the airplane wants to yaw left.

If positive control is not maintained, the pitch attitude can quickly increase beyond the normal climb attitude, the airspeed can decay, and the wing can approach the critical angle of attack.

That is the entire hazard this lesson is built around.

---

### Why the Nose Wants to Rise

During the landing approach, nose-up trim reduces the elevator force needed to hold the slower, higher-angle-of-attack condition.

When go-around power is added:

- propeller airflow over the tail changes,
- the pitching moment changes,
- the existing nose-up trim remains,
- and the airplane tends to pitch up.

The important point is that trim does not fly the airplane.

**Elevator controls pitch. Trim only relieves control pressure.**

So when power is added, the correct response is not to reach immediately for the trim wheel.

The correct response is:

**hold the proper pitch attitude with elevator first.**

Then trim away the pressure after the airplane is under control.

---

### The Stall Still Happens Because of Critical AOA

The elevator trim stall follows the same aerodynamic rule as every other stall:

**the wing stalls when critical angle of attack is exceeded.**

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Critical_AOA.png" alt="Critical angle of attack illustration" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>The trim and power combination creates the pitch-up tendency, but the stall still occurs only when the wing exceeds critical AOA.</figcaption>
</figure>

So the correct explanation is not:

“Trim causes the stall.”

The correct explanation is:

**nose-up trim and go-around power create a strong pitch-up tendency; if that pitch is not controlled, AOA continues increasing until the wing stalls.**

That distinction matters because it tells the learner what must be corrected:

**the pitch attitude and AOA.**

---

### Why the Airplane Also Wants to Go Left

The go-around happens at high power and relatively low airspeed, so left-turning tendencies become much more noticeable.

- **Torque reaction** tends to roll the airplane left.
- **P-factor** tends to yaw the nose left.
- **Spiraling slipstream** tends to yaw the airplane left.
- **Gyroscopic effects** can contribute when pitch changes rapidly.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Left_Turning_Tendencies.png" alt="Left-turning tendencies at high power and high angle of attack" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>High power and increasing AOA increase the need for right rudder during the go-around.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is why the maneuver is not only about pitch.

It is also about coordination.

The correct control picture is:

**forward elevator to control pitch + right rudder to control yaw.**

If the nose is allowed to rise while the airplane also yaws left, the stall can become a spin.

---

### Situations Where an Elevator Trim Stall Can Develop

The classic scenario is a go-around from a normal landing approach, but the same hazard can appear whenever substantial nose-up trim is combined with a rapid power increase.

Examples include:

- go-around from a normal landing approach,
- recovery from a simulated forced landing,
- power application after a slow approach with substantial nose-up trim,
- takeoff with an inappropriate nose-up trim setting,
- or abnormal autopilot / trim operation.

The common thread is always the same:

**nose-up trim + power + delayed pitch control.**

---

### Recognition

The learner should recognize the developing condition before a full stall occurs.

The cues are:

- rapid nose rise beyond the normal climb attitude,
- increasing forward-elevator force,
- strong left yaw,
- increasing right-rudder requirement,
- rapid airspeed decay,
- stall warning horn,
- buffet,
- difficulty holding the intended go-around pitch.

The most useful recognition cue is:

**the airplane is pitching up more than the desired go-around attitude.**

Do not wait for the horn to decide that control is being lost.

The horn is a stall cue.

The abnormal pitch-up is the earlier operational cue.

---

### Prevention

A real go-around should never be allowed to progress to an elevator trim stall.

The correct sequence is:

1. Apply power while maintaining **positive elevator control**.
2. Anticipate and apply **right rudder**.
3. Establish the proper **go-around pitch attitude**.
4. Retract flaps incrementally according to the POH.
5. Allow the airplane to accelerate.
6. **Trim only after positive control is established.**

The short teaching line is:

**Fly first. Trim second.**

---

### Setup and Entry

Now the maneuver can be demonstrated.

1. **Clear the area.**
2. Select an entry altitude that allows the maneuver to be completed **no lower than 3,000 feet AGL**.
3. Complete the pre-maneuver checklist.
4. Slowly retard the throttle while maintaining altitude.
5. Configure the airplane for landing.
6. Close the throttle and maintain altitude until the airplane approaches normal glide speed.
7. Establish the normal glide.
8. Trim the airplane nose-up as it would be trimmed during a normal landing approach.
9. Stabilize the airplane before adding power.

At this point the airplane is deliberately placed in the condition that can create the problem:

**landing configuration + slow glide + nose-up trim.**

---

### Demonstrating the Pitch-Up

Once the trimmed glide is established, smoothly advance the throttle to **maximum allowable power** as though beginning a go-around.

The airplane will tend to:

- pitch up rapidly,
- yaw left,
- require significant forward elevator pressure,
- and lose airspeed quickly if the pitch is not controlled.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Elevator_Trim_Stall.png" alt="Elevator trim stall demonstration sequence" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>The maneuver demonstrates the rapid pitch-up tendency that can develop when go-around power is applied from a nose-up trimmed landing approach.</figcaption>
</figure>

The purpose of the demonstration is to allow the learner to see and feel that tendency without allowing the airplane to become uncontrolled.

The first stall cue must be acknowledged:

**“Imminent stall.”**

Recovery occurs:

- at the first indication of a stall, or
- after a full stall,

**as specified by the evaluator.**

---

### Recovery

The recovery is straightforward when the priorities are correct.

#### 1. Control Pitch

**Apply sufficient forward elevator pressure to reduce AOA and establish a normal climb attitude.**

If a full stall has occurred, reduce AOA enough to break the stall first.

Do not attempt to recover primarily with trim.

#### 2. Maintain Coordination

Apply the required **right rudder** to stop the left yaw and keep the airplane coordinated.

#### 3. Stabilize the Climb

Hold the proper go-around pitch attitude and maintain positive control.

#### 4. Configure

Retract flaps incrementally according to the normal go-around procedure.

#### 5. Re-Trim

Once the airplane is under control, adjust trim to relieve the heavy forward-elevator pressure.

#### 6. Return to the Desired Flightpath

Accelerate to the appropriate climb speed and return to the desired altitude, heading, and flightpath.

The concise recovery cue is:

**Forward elevator → right rudder → go-around pitch → configure → trim → climb.**

---

### Why Trim Comes After Control

A common mistake is reaching for the trim wheel as soon as the control pressure becomes heavy.

That reverses the priority.

Trim changes control force slowly.

Elevator changes pitch immediately.

So if the airplane is pitching toward a stall:

**elevator fixes the attitude; trim fixes the workload.**

That is why the sequence must remain:

**Elevator first → trim second.**

---

### What Happens if a Full Stall Occurs

If the demonstration is taken to a full stall, recovery may require a larger nose-down pitch change and greater altitude loss.

That is why this maneuver is practiced with substantial altitude margin.

The real-world lesson is even more important:

**an actual go-around close to the ground should never be allowed to progress to a full stall.**

The warning signs must be corrected early.

---

### Risk Management

#### Stall / Spin Risk

The main hazard is:

**maximum power + nose-up landing trim + increasing AOA + left yaw.**

If the airplane stalls while yawed, spin risk increases immediately.

Mitigation:

- maintain positive pitch control,
- maintain right rudder,
- reduce AOA promptly,
- keep the wings controlled.

#### Environmental Effects

Turbulence can change AOA rapidly and increase workload.

High density altitude reduces climb performance.

That means the pilot must not keep increasing pitch simply because the expected climb is not developing.

#### Collision Hazards

The maneuver is flown at altitude, but the real scenario happens during a high-workload go-around.

Traffic, runway environment, radio calls, flap retraction, trim, pitch, and rudder all compete for attention.

The priority remains:

**Aviate first.**

#### Aircraft Limitations

Stay within the POH limitations for:

- flap extension,
- airspeed,
- configuration,
- and maneuvering.

Do not create an abnormal configuration just to make the maneuver more dramatic.

#### Distraction and Task Prioritization

A go-around is already busy.

The correct priority is:

**pitch and coordination first → configuration second → trim third → communication after the airplane is stable.**

---

### Common Errors

Common errors include:

- failure to adequately clear the area,
- selecting inadequate entry altitude,
- failure to establish the landing configuration before entry,
- failure to trim the airplane for the glide,
- failure to anticipate the nose-up pitching tendency,
- insufficient forward elevator pressure,
- insufficient right rudder,
- poor coordination,
- allowing excessive pitch before correction,
- failure to acknowledge the first stall cue,
- attempting to recover primarily with trim,
- abrupt flap retraction,
- secondary stall during recovery,
- incomplete return to the desired flightpath,
- and poor explanation of the relationship among trim, power, yaw, and critical AOA.

The correction theme is always:

**control the airplane first, then relieve the workload.**

### Teaching Flow

The entire lesson should sound like one connected story:

**The airplane is stabilized on final and trimmed nose-up. A go-around is initiated. Maximum allowable power produces a strong pitch-up and left-yaw tendency. If positive control is not maintained, airspeed decays and AOA increases toward critical. The first stall cue is recognized. Forward elevator reduces AOA, right rudder maintains coordination, the normal go-around attitude is established, flaps are retracted incrementally, trim is adjusted only after control is secure, and the airplane returns to the desired flightpath.**

That is the maneuver.

That is the hazard.

And that is the lesson the learner should remember.



## K1 — Aerodynamics

In a low-power approach, the airplane is trimmed nose-up for slow flight. Applying maximum power changes pitching moment and increases propeller airflow/downwash over the tail. The existing trim force, power effect, and high AOA can drive the nose sharply upward unless the pilot applies forward elevator.

## K2 — Flight Situations

- Go-around from a trimmed approach
- Power-off stall recovery with landing trim
- Abrupt power application during slow flight
- Distraction during configuration change
- Delayed forward pressure or failure to manage trim

## K3 — Recognition

Recognize increasing forward-control force, rapid nose rise beyond normal climb attitude, increasing right-rudder demand, speed decay, horn/buffet, and inability to hold the intended go-around pitch.

## K4 — Entry and Minimum Altitude

Complete above **3,000 feet AGL**; plan at least **3,500 feet AGL**. Retard throttle, configure for landing within $V_{FE}$, establish **60–70 KIAS** normal approach with flaps 30° or assigned glide speed, and trim for the glide. Apply maximum allowable power as in a go-around.

## K5 — Recovery

Apply sufficient **forward elevator** to reduce AOA and establish a safe climb attitude, maintain right rudder/coordination, retract flaps incrementally, and adjust trim nose-down only while maintaining positive control. Recover at the evaluator-specified first indication or full stall, then accelerate to $V_X$/$V_Y$.

## K6 — Common Errors

Key errors are inadequate instruction about torque/trim hazards, insufficient altitude, incorrect configuration, and poor recognition/recovery. Related errors include power before AOA control, overreliance on trim, abrupt flap retraction, loss of coordination, and secondary stall.

## Risk Management

### Go-Around Pitch-Up / Stall
**Hazard:** Maximum power with nose-up landing trim. **Recognition:** rapid pitch-up, heavy forward force, speed decay, horn. **Consequence:** power-on stall/spin. **Mitigation:** anticipate forward pressure and right rudder; set climb attitude before trimming.

### Trim Misuse / Aircraft Limitations
**Hazard:** Attempting to recover primarily with trim or retracting flaps abruptly. **Consequence:** delayed AOA reduction, excessive force, sink, or secondary stall. **Mitigation:** fly with elevator first, then trim; configure incrementally within $V_{FE}$.

### Environment / Collision / Distraction
**Hazard:** Turbulence, high density altitude, traffic, or checklist fixation during high workload. **Mitigation:** adequate altitude, suitable conditions, continuous scan, and aviate-first prioritization.

## Scenario Application

**Situation:** A balked landing occurs with full flaps and substantial nose-up trim. The pilot applies full power but reaches for trim while the nose rises rapidly.  
**Desired outcome:** Apply forward elevator and right rudder first, set go-around attitude, retract flaps incrementally, then trim while accelerating.

# Fly

## Setup

- Clear; plan entry ≥**3,500 feet AGL** and complete >**3,000 feet AGL**.
- Autopilot OFF; positive control transfer and recovery point briefed.
- Retard throttle; configure for landing within **$V_{FE}$ 110 KIAS at 10° / 85 KIAS beyond 10°**.
- Establish and trim a **60–70 KIAS**, flaps-30° approach/glide or evaluator-assigned condition.

## Instructor Demonstration

1. Stabilize the trimmed landing glide and identify the go-around scenario.
2. Advance throttle smoothly to **maximum allowable power**.
3. Demonstrate the developing nose-up force while guarding the controls; do not allow unsafe pitch/yaw.
4. Call the first horn, buffet, or excessive-pitch cue.
5. Recover at the evaluator-directed point: **forward elevator / reduce AOA**, right rudder, wings level.
6. Establish go-around pitch; retract flaps incrementally per procedure.
7. Adjust nose-down trim while maintaining elevator control.
8. Accelerate to **$V_X$ 62 KIAS SL / 67 KIAS at 10,000 ft** or **$V_Y$ 74 KIAS SL / 72 KIAS at 10,000 ft**.
9. Return to desired flightpath and explain prevention.

## Learner Practice

Use instructor demonstration followed by closely guarded applicant practice. Require the applicant to call **power—forward pressure—right rudder—pitch—configure—trim—climb**.

## Analyze and Correct Common Errors

1. **Failure to emphasize torque and up-trim hazards** — Connect high power, nose-up trim, pitch-up, yaw, and spin risk.
2. **Performing below minimum safe altitude** — Discontinue; preserve the 3,000-foot completion floor.
3. **Failure to establish landing configuration and trimmed glide** — Stabilize before applying power.
4. **Inadequate recognition/recovery demonstration** — Guard the pitch-up, call the cue, and reduce AOA promptly.
5. **Trying to recover with trim instead of elevator** — Apply forward elevator first; trim only relieves controlled pressure.
6. **Insufficient right rudder** — Anticipate left yaw with maximum power.
7. **Abrupt flap retraction** — Retract incrementally after control and climb are established.
8. **Secondary stall or failure to regain flightpath** — Accelerate before increasing AOA; complete the go-around sequence.

## Completion Standards

Demonstrate the landing-trim go-around pitch-up, identify the first cue, recover as directed above **3,000 feet AGL**, control yaw/AOA, configure and trim correctly, return to the desired flightpath, and teach avoidance.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. What creates the pitch-up tendency?
[[answer: Nose-up landing trim combined with changed thrust-line pitching moment and increased propeller airflow/downwash over the tail when go-around power is applied.]]

### 2. What is the first corrective control?
[[answer: Forward elevator to control pitch and reduce AOA; trim follows after the airplane is under control.]]

### 3. Why is right rudder important?
[[answer: Maximum power and high AOA increase left-turning tendencies; uncorrected yaw can turn the pitch-up into an uncoordinated stall/spin.]]

### 4. What is the minimum completion altitude?
[[answer: 3,000 feet AGL.]]

### 5. What C172S speed limits apply?
[[answer: VFE is 110 KIAS at 10° and 85 KIAS beyond 10°; the normal flaps-30° approach range is 60–70 KIAS.]]

### 6. What is the prevention sequence during a real go-around?
[[answer: Apply power while controlling pitch and yaw, establish the go-around attitude, retract flaps incrementally, accelerate, then trim.]]
