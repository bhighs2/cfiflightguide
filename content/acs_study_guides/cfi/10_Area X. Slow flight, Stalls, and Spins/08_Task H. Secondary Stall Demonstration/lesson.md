# Task H. Secondary Stall Demonstration

**Area X — Slow Flight, Stalls, and Spins**

## Objective

Determine that the applicant understands secondary stalls, applies that knowledge, manages associated risks, demonstrates the required skills, and provides effective instruction.

## Purpose

A secondary stall occurs when a pilot incompletely recovers from an initial stall or raises the nose too aggressively before sufficient airspeed and control effectiveness return. This demonstration teaches why recovery must include a complete AOA reduction and a patient, smooth return to the flightpath.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested:** **20–30 minutes ground**, **15–20 minutes demonstration**, **10 minutes debrief**.  
**Equipment:** C172S POH/checklist, ACS, AFH, AC 61-67C, stall-recovery diagram, and maneuver card.

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** Explain the incomplete-recovery error chain; demonstrate the primary stall, deliberate second critical-AOA exceedance, and final recovery while teaching avoidance.  
**Instructor:** Brief the complete sequence and altitude gates; guard yaw, pitch rate, and repeated stall; take control immediately if limits are approached.

## Completion Standards

Clear; complete above **3,000 feet AGL**; enter a stall in the specified configuration; exceed critical AOA a second time during recovery; recognize and recover promptly; and explain the conditions and errors that produce a secondary stall.

**The main idea in a secondary stall is that the airplane stalls a second time because the first recovery was incomplete or the pilot tried to return to the flightpath too aggressively.**

The wing still stalls for the same reason:

**Critical angle of attack is exceeded.**

The difference is the timing.

The pilot has just begun recovering from one stall, but before enough airspeed and control effectiveness return, the pilot increases pitch and load factor too quickly.

That gives us the key relationship:

**Incomplete recovery → premature pull → AOA increases again → second stall**

### Real-World Application — Rushing the Recovery

The most realistic setup is a stall close to the ground.

The pilot recovers from the initial stall, sees the airplane losing altitude, and immediately tries to save the altitude by pulling hard.

But the airplane may still be:

- Slow.
- At reduced control effectiveness.
- Close to critical AOA.
- Building load factor rapidly.

That aggressive pull can exceed critical AOA a second time.

Another possibility is trying to recover from the first stall with **power only**, without adequately reducing AOA.

Power may improve performance, but:

**power does not unstall the wing.**

If AOA remains excessive, the airplane can remain stalled or stall again.

### What Is a Secondary Stall?

A secondary stall is a stall that occurs during recovery from a preceding stall.

Typically:

1. The airplane stalls.
2. The pilot begins the recovery.
3. AOA is reduced only briefly or incompletely.
4. The pilot raises the nose too soon.
5. Load factor and AOA increase.
6. Critical AOA is exceeded again.

The second stall may be:

- More abrupt.
- More pronounced.
- Associated with a sharper wing drop.
- Associated with greater altitude loss.

### Why the Second Stall Can Be Worse

The airplane may still have very little energy after the first stall.

Then the pilot adds a rapid pitch input.

That can create:

- Increased load factor.
- A rapid return to critical AOA.
- A deeper stall.
- More altitude loss.
- Greater yaw or roll.
- Greater spin potential.

The second stall may also surprise the pilot.

That startle can delay the second recovery.

### Airspeed and Control Effectiveness

After the first stall breaks, the airplane still needs time to:

- Accelerate.
- Restore airflow.
- Restore control effectiveness.
- Reestablish a safe flightpath.

This is why we do not immediately pull the nose back to the desired attitude.

**The airplane has to be allowed to recover before we ask it to carry load again.**

### Load Factor

If the pilot pulls too aggressively during recovery:

- Load factor increases.
- Required lift increases.
- AOA increases.
- Stall speed increases.

So a secondary stall can also become an **accelerated stall**.

That relationship is:

**Premature pull → load factor increases → stall speed increases → critical AOA reached again**

### Where Secondary Stalls Occur

Typical situations include:

- Abrupt pullout after a power-off stall.
- Abrupt pullout after a power-on stall.
- Trying to minimize altitude loss during recovery.
- Recovery from a spin or steep dive.
- Power-only stall recovery.
- Startle response close to the ground.
- Pulling before airspeed and control effectiveness return.

### Recognizing a Secondary Stall

The cues are similar to any stall, but they occur again during the recovery.

Look for:

- Stall horn returning.
- Buffet returning.
- Mushy controls.
- Loss of control effectiveness.
- Nose drop.
- Wing drop.
- Increased sink rate.
- Yaw.
- Rapidly increasing back pressure.

The key recognition point is:

**The airplane was recovering, then the stall cues came back.**

That is a secondary stall.

### Entry

This maneuver is a **demonstration of an improper recovery**.

- Clear the area.
- Select an entry altitude that allows the maneuver to be completed no lower than **3,000 feet AGL**.
- Establish the configuration specified by the evaluator.
- Enter a normal primary stall.
- Begin the stall recovery.
- Reduce AOA only enough to start the recovery.
- Before adequate airspeed and control effectiveness return, deliberately increase pitch again.
- Exceed critical AOA a second time.
- Call the secondary stall.
- Recover promptly and correctly.

The distinction needs to be obvious:

**First recovery = intentionally incomplete.**  
**Second recovery = complete and correct.**

### Recovery

Once the secondary stall occurs:

1. **Reduce AOA sufficiently.**
2. Maintain the reduced AOA until the stall warning is eliminated.
3. **Maintain coordination.**
4. Level the wings after airflow is restored.
5. Add or confirm appropriate power.
6. Configure as recommended by the manufacturer.
7. Allow the airplane to accelerate.
8. Establish the appropriate **VX or VY** climb if required.
9. Return smoothly to the desired flightpath.

The lesson is:

**Do not rush the pullout.**

Recover the wing first.

Then rebuild energy.

Then increase load.

### ACS Standards — Secondary Stall Demonstration

- Clear the area.
- Select an entry altitude that allows the maneuver to be completed no lower than **3,000 feet AGL**.
- Enter a stall in the configuration specified by the evaluator.
- During the recovery, exceed the **critical AOA a second time**.
- Recognize the secondary stall.
- Recover promptly and appropriately.
- Describe and demonstrate the conditions that lead to a secondary stall so the learner can avoid it.
- Analyze and correct common errors.

### Common Errors — Secondary Stall Demonstration

- Failure to adequately clear the area.
- Failure to establish the selected configuration before entry.
- Performing the maneuver without adequate altitude.
- Improper or inadequate demonstration of the primary stall.
- Improper or inadequate demonstration of the secondary stall.
- Failure to make the first recovery clearly incomplete.
- Pulling so aggressively that the maneuver becomes uncontrolled.
- Uncoordinated flight during either stall.
- Failure to recognize the second stall.
- Insufficient AOA reduction during the final recovery.
- Attempting to level the wings before reducing AOA.
- Using power before adequately reducing AOA.
- Excessive forward elevator during the final recovery.
- Creating a third stall through another premature pull.
- Excessive airspeed or G during recovery.
- Failure to regain situational awareness after the maneuver.
- Failure to explain why poor primary-stall recovery creates the secondary stall.

### Risk Management

#### Repeated Stall and Spin

The airplane is deliberately taken through two critical-AOA exceedances.

The second one occurs while the airplane is still low-energy.

If yaw is present, the secondary stall can develop into a spin.

Maintain coordination throughout.

#### Stall Recovery Procedure

The demonstration intentionally shows an improper first recovery.

The learner must understand that this is **not** the correct recovery technique.

The correct recovery is:

**Reduce AOA sufficiently and keep it reduced until the wing is flying again.**

#### Environmental Effects

Turbulence can increase AOA and load factor unexpectedly.

High density altitude can increase the time required to regain energy and climb performance.

Use suitable conditions and adequate altitude.

#### Collision Hazards

Clear before beginning and continue the traffic scan.

The maneuver has a high workload and large vertical component.

#### Aircraft Limitations

Respect:

- Configuration limits.
- Airspeed limits.
- VA as applicable.
- Flap limits.
- POH/AFM procedures.

Do not allow the demonstration to become an aggressive or structurally excessive maneuver.

#### Distraction and Task Prioritization

The pilot may become fixated on minimizing altitude loss.

That instinct is exactly what can produce the second stall.

The priority is:

**Recover from the stall first.**

Altitude preservation comes after restoring controlled flight.

### Instructor Emphasis

The learner should leave the lesson understanding:

1. **A secondary stall is a second exceedance of critical AOA during recovery.**
2. **It usually happens because the pilot pulls too soon or too aggressively.**
3. **The airplane may still be slow and have poor control effectiveness after the first stall.**
4. **Premature back pressure increases load factor and AOA.**
5. **Power alone does not recover a stalled wing.**
6. **The final recovery must reduce AOA completely and patiently rebuild energy.**
7. **The maneuver is completed no lower than 3,000 feet AGL.**
8. **The best prevention is to recover correctly the first time.**

### Close

A secondary stall is really a lesson in patience.

The error chain is:

**Primary stall → incomplete recovery → premature pull → second stall**

The prevention chain is:

**Reduce AOA → restore airflow → accelerate → then increase load**

Do not trade a correct recovery for an attempt to save altitude too early.

The most important rule is:

**Recover the wing first. Then recover the flightpath.**


## K1 — Aerodynamics

A secondary stall is aerodynamically another exceedance of **critical AOA** during recovery from a preceding stall. If the pilot reduces AOA only briefly or pulls abruptly while the airplane remains slow, the upward pitching motion and renewed load can exceed critical AOA again, sometimes more aggressively than the first stall.

## K2 — Flight Situations

- Abrupt pullout after power-off or power-on stall recovery
- Attempt to minimize altitude loss before speed/control effectiveness return
- Pullout from a spin or steep dive
- Power-only recovery without sufficient AOA reduction
- Startle response or ground-proximity fixation

## K3 — Recognition

Recognize the return of horn, buffet, mushy controls, uncommanded pitch/roll, or renewed loss of lift during the recovery. The second break may feel sharper because pitch rate and load are already increasing.

## K4 — Entry and Minimum Altitude

Complete above **3,000 feet AGL** and plan entry at least **3,500 feet AGL**. Enter a primary stall in the evaluator-specified configuration. Begin a deliberately incomplete recovery, then increase pitch too soon so critical AOA is exceeded a second time.

## K5 — Recovery

Recover from the secondary stall exactly as from any stall: **reduce AOA sufficiently and maintain the input until warning ends**, remove yaw, level wings after airflow is restored, add/confirm power, configure, accelerate, and return smoothly. More forward elevator may be required than during the incomplete first attempt, but avoid low/negative G.

## K6 — Common Errors

Key errors are wrong configuration, insufficient altitude, poor demonstration/recognition/recovery, and failure to explain the hazards of incomplete primary-stall recovery. Additional errors include poor coordination, insufficient AOA reduction, abrupt pullout, and failure to regain situational awareness.

## Risk Management

### Repeated Stall / Spin
**Hazard:** A second critical-AOA exceedance before speed returns. **Recognition:** warning/buffet returns during pullout. **Consequence:** deeper stall, spin, and greater altitude loss. **Mitigation:** complete AOA reduction, coordinate, accelerate, and raise the nose smoothly.

### Environment / Collision / Limitations
**Hazard:** Turbulence or high density altitude reduces margin; fixation interrupts clearing. **Mitigation:** suitable conditions, high entry altitude, continuous scan, configuration/airspeed limits, and prompt termination.

### Startle / Task Prioritization
**Hazard:** Ground-proximity instinct produces an abrupt pull. **Mitigation:** brief that **stall recovery may require altitude**; airplane control and AOA take priority over holding altitude.

## Scenario Application

**Situation:** After a stall break the pilot lowers the nose only until the horn stops, then immediately pulls hard to avoid altitude loss; the horn and buffet return.  
**Desired outcome:** Identify a secondary stall, reduce AOA again and hold it reduced, coordinate, accelerate, then return smoothly to the flightpath.

# Fly

## Setup

- Clear; start ≥**3,500 feet AGL** and complete >**3,000 feet AGL**.
- Autopilot/wing leveler OFF; positive control transfer briefed.
- Use evaluator-specified configuration; observe $V_{FE}$ if flaps are used.
- Brief the **primary stall → incomplete recovery → secondary stall → complete recovery** sequence.

## Instructor Demonstration

1. Enter the primary stall in the specified configuration.
2. Call the first cue and allow the primary stall to occur.
3. Reduce AOA only enough to begin recovery, then deliberately raise the nose too soon.
4. Exceed critical AOA a second time and call **“secondary stall.”**
5. Promptly apply sufficient nose-down elevator to eliminate warning; maintain coordination.
6. Level wings after airflow is restored; add/confirm power.
7. Configure incrementally and accelerate to appropriate $V_X$/$V_Y$.
8. Return to assigned flightpath and explain the prevention lesson.

## Learner Practice

Instructor demonstrates first. Applicant practice is closely guarded and limited to the planned sequence. Require verbal distinction between the **intentionally incomplete first recovery** and the **complete final recovery**.

## Analyze and Correct Common Errors

1. **Failure to establish selected configuration** — Stabilize and verify before the primary stall.
2. **Performing below minimum safe altitude** — Discontinue; protect the 3,000-foot floor.
3. **Inadequate recognition/recovery demonstration** — Call both stalls and show clearly different incomplete versus complete recovery.
4. **Failure to emphasize poor primary-recovery hazards** — Connect early pullout to renewed critical AOA, spin risk, and extra altitude loss.
5. **Uncoordinated primary or secondary stall** — Remove yaw; prevent spin entry.
6. **Insufficient final AOA reduction** — Hold nose-down input until warning is eliminated.
7. **Excessive forward elevator / negative G** — Use enough input to recover, not an abrupt push.
8. **Aileron or power before AOA reduction** — Unload first; then roll and manage power.
9. **Another premature pullout** — Accelerate and regain control effectiveness before increasing load.
10. **Failure to return to desired flightpath** — Reorient, scan, configure, and stabilize after recovery.

## Completion Standards

Demonstrate a primary stall, deliberate incomplete recovery, second critical-AOA exceedance, and prompt complete recovery above **3,000 feet AGL** while maintaining coordination, limitations, effective instruction, and error analysis.

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. What is a secondary stall?
[[answer: A second exceedance of critical AOA during an incomplete or overly aggressive recovery from a preceding stall.]]

### 2. Why may it be more aggressive?
[[answer: The airplane may still be slow while pitch rate and load are increasing rapidly, carrying the wing farther beyond critical AOA.]]

### 3. What is the minimum completion altitude?
[[answer: 3,000 feet AGL.]]

### 4. How is the final recovery different from the deliberately incomplete recovery?
[[answer: The final recovery reduces AOA sufficiently and keeps it reduced until warning ends, restores coordination and roll control, then accelerates before increasing load.]]

### 5. What operational error commonly causes a secondary stall?
[[answer: Pulling abruptly to avoid altitude loss before the airplane has regained adequate airspeed and control effectiveness.]]

### 6. What prevents the maneuver from becoming a spin?
[[answer: Maintaining coordination and promptly removing yaw while reducing AOA; terminate immediately if rotation develops.]]
