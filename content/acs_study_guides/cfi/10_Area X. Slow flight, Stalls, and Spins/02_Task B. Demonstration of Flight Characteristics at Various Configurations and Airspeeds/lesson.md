# Task B. Demonstration of Flight Characteristics at Various Configurations and Airspeeds

**Area X — Slow Flight, Stalls, and Spins**

## Objective

Determine that the instructor applicant understands **flight characteristics and power required at different airspeeds and configurations** appropriate to the airplane flown, applies that knowledge, manages associated risks, demonstrates the required skills, and provides effective instruction.

## Purpose

This demonstration makes **energy management** visible. The applicant compares pitch, power, trim, controllability, and warning cues from maneuvering speed through best glide or reference landing speed to critically slow flight in both **clean** and **landing** configurations.

The pitch-only recovery at an unchanged power setting demonstrates why an airplane deep in the **region of reversed command** may need to trade altitude for airspeed before it can regain a sustainable flightpath.

## Schedule / Equipment

**Suggested Schedule:** **30–40 minutes ground**, **30–40 minutes flight**, and **10 minutes debrief**.

**Equipment:** C172S POH and checklist, CFI ACS, AFH, PHAK, power-required curve, airspeed-limit card, model airplane or whiteboard, and maneuver card.

## Learner Actions / Instructor Actions

**Learner:** Explain energy states, power required, configuration effects, airplane-specific speeds, warning cues, and risks; conduct both demonstrations while teaching; compare the observed power, airspeed, controllability, and altitude loss.

**Instructor:** Verify speed limits and recovery floor; demonstrate each energy state and configuration; require verbal warning acknowledgment; protect against a full stall; and debrief the observed relationships rather than isolated numbers.

## Completion Standards

The applicant conducts and explains the clean- and landing-configuration demonstrations while maintaining **altitude ±100 feet**, **airspeed +5/-0 knots**, **heading ±10°**, and **bank ±5°**, as appropriate; remains above **1,500 feet AGL**; observes all limitations; verbally acknowledges stall warnings; and returns to normal cruise at the assigned altitude and heading.

### Objective

Teach the learner to explain and demonstrate **how airplane flight characteristics, control requirements, and power required change across airspeeds and configurations**, from maneuvering speed through best glide/reference landing speed to the critically slow, immediate-stall boundary; relate those changes to the airplane’s **energy state**; manage the associated risks; and perform both the clean- and landing-configuration demonstrations to the standards of **CFI ACS Task AI.X.B**.

### Primary FAA Reference

**Airplane Flying Handbook (FAA-H-8083-3C), Chapter 4 — Energy Management: Mastering Altitude and Airspeed Control**

The ACS is the governing standard. Chapter 4 supplies the teaching framework that connects the ACS elements into one story:

> **The airplane stores energy as altitude and airspeed. Thrust adds energy, drag removes it, and pitch determines how that energy is distributed.**

The purpose of this Task is to watch that relationship change as the airplane moves from a comfortable energy state toward the **slow edge of the flight envelope**, where drag rises, excess power disappears, control effectiveness decreases, and very little margin remains before the stall.

### Applicable FARs

- **14 CFR §91.9** — Operate within the limitations and procedures of the approved POH/AFM. This applies directly to maneuvering speed, flap-extension limits, configuration, and all other airplane limitations used in this Task.
- **14 CFR §91.103** — Preflight preparation includes performance information pertinent to the flight. Before the maneuver, know the airplane’s weight, applicable maneuvering speed, flap limits, and relevant performance speeds.
- **14 CFR §91.113** — See and avoid remains continuous. Clear the area before the maneuver and maintain an outside traffic scan throughout the demonstration.

### Start With the Airplane as an Energy System

The easiest way to understand this Task is to begin with one question:

**What is happening to the airplane’s energy as it slows?**

The airplane stores mechanical energy in two places:

- **Potential energy — altitude**
- **Kinetic energy — airspeed**

The engine adds energy through thrust. Aerodynamic drag removes energy.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Energy_Balance_Equation.png" alt="Aircraft energy balance equation showing thrust, drag, altitude change, and airspeed change" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>Thrust adds energy, drag removes it, and the difference appears as a change in altitude, airspeed, or both.</figcaption>
</figure>

If thrust exceeds drag, total energy increases. That added energy can become altitude, airspeed, or some combination.

If drag exceeds thrust, total energy decreases. The airplane must give up altitude, airspeed, or both.

If thrust equals drag, total energy remains constant.

That leads directly to the two primary energy controls.

**Throttle controls total energy.** Increasing thrust relative to drag adds energy; reducing thrust relative to drag removes it.

**Elevator controls energy distribution.** Pitch changes determine how the available energy is divided between altitude and airspeed.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Energy_Control_Map.png" alt="Energy control map showing altitude and airspeed changes from elevator and throttle" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>The throttle changes total energy; the elevator redistributes energy between altitude and airspeed.</figcaption>
</figure>

That distinction matters because the ACS eventually requires the airplane to accelerate **without changing power**. The only way to do that is to exchange altitude for airspeed with pitch.

Before getting there, however, the airplane must first be moved from a normal energy state toward the slow edge of the envelope.

---

### Why Power Required Changes as Airspeed Changes

In steady level flight:

- **Lift must balance weight**
- **Thrust must balance drag**

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Four_Forces_Flight.png" alt="Four forces acting on an airplane in level flight" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>Steady level flight requires lift to balance weight and thrust to balance drag.</figcaption>
</figure>

At higher airspeed, the wing can produce the required lift at a relatively low angle of attack.

As airspeed decreases, the wing must operate at a progressively higher **angle of attack** to continue producing enough lift to support the airplane.

That is the first major connection in the energy story:

**Slower airspeed → higher AOA → more induced drag → more power required.**

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Lift_v_Drag.png" alt="Lift and drag relationships as angle of attack increases" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>As AOA rises, induced drag becomes increasingly important. Lift continues increasing only until CLmax and critical AOA.</figcaption>
</figure>

At high airspeed, parasite drag dominates. Slowing initially reduces total drag and therefore reduces the power required to maintain level flight.

Eventually the airplane reaches the **minimum power required speed**.

Slow below that point and the trend reverses: induced drag rises so rapidly that **slower flight now requires more power**.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Power_Curve.png" alt="Power curve for a propeller airplane showing reverse command" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>The minimum-power point separates the region of normal command from the region of reversed command.</figcaption>
</figure>

This divides the curve into two regions:

**Region of normal command — front side:**  
Faster flight generally requires more power; slowing toward minimum power requires less.

**Region of reversed command — backside:**  
Slower flight requires more power.

This is the central aerodynamic relationship the ACS demonstration is designed to make visible.

---

### The Same Power Can Support Two Different Airspeeds

A single power setting can intersect the power-required curve at two different airspeeds—one on the backside and another on the front side.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Energy_Management_Power_Curve.png" alt="Power available and power required curves showing front side and backside of the power curve" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>The same power setting can support a slow, high-AOA condition on the backside and a faster, lower-drag condition on the front side.</figcaption>
</figure>

This explains the ACS pitch-only portion.

At the critically slow condition, the airplane is at:

- low airspeed,
- high AOA,
- high induced drag,
- and a relatively high power requirement.

Without changing power, lower the nose.

AOA decreases.

Induced drag decreases.

The airplane descends and converts some altitude into airspeed.

As airspeed increases and drag decreases, the same power setting can support a **new, faster steady-state airspeed**.

That is not simply “pushing the nose over.”

It is an energy exchange:

**potential energy → kinetic energy**

and an aerodynamic improvement:

**lower AOA → lower induced drag → lower power required**

The applicant notes the **new airspeed and altitude lost** because those observations prove the energy exchange that just occurred.

---

### AOA Is the Link Between Airspeed, Drag, and the Stall Boundary

Now the critical-AOA requirement in the ACS fits directly into the same story.

As the airplane slows in level flight, AOA must increase to maintain lift.

That increasing AOA is what drives induced drag upward and moves the airplane farther onto the backside of the power curve.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Critical_AOA.png" alt="Airflow separation as the wing approaches and exceeds critical angle of attack" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>Slowing in level flight requires increasing AOA. The process cannot continue indefinitely; at critical AOA, the wing stalls.</figcaption>
</figure>

The ACS does not define the slow end of this Task by one memorized airspeed.

Instead, the airplane is slowed until reaching a condition where:

**Any further increase in AOA, increase in load factor, or reduction in power would result in an immediate stall.**

That wording describes the remaining **energy and aerodynamic margin**.

At that point:

- AOA is very near critical,
- induced drag is high,
- power required is high,
- excess power is small or nonexistent,
- airspeed margin is small,
- maneuvering margin is small.

So the stall warning is not an unrelated item that happens to appear in the ACS. It is the cockpit indication that the airplane is approaching the **slow boundary of the sustainable energy state**.

Every stall-warning indication must be verbally acknowledged.

---

### Load Factor Consumes the Remaining Energy Margin

The ACS specifically says that, at the critically slow condition, an **increase in load factor** would produce an immediate stall.

That is because a turn increases the lift requirement.

In a level turn, part of the lift vector is used horizontally to turn the airplane. To keep the vertical component equal to weight, total lift must increase.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Load_Factor.png" alt="Load factor in a banked level turn" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>Increasing bank in level flight increases load factor and therefore the lift the wing must produce.</figcaption>
</figure>

More lift at the same slow airspeed requires:

**more AOA → more induced drag → more power required**

It also increases stall speed.

So load factor does not merely “increase stall speed.” In the energy story, it **raises the energy demand at exactly the point where the airplane has very little excess power available**.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Energy_Management_Power_Curve_Increasing_Load_Factor.png" alt="Power required curve moving upward as load factor increases" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>Increasing load factor raises induced drag and shifts the power-required curve upward, reducing excess-power margin.</figcaption>
</figure>

This is why maneuvering at critically slow airspeeds must be deliberate:

- shallow banks,
- coordinated controls,
- smooth pitch inputs,
- no abrupt increase in load factor.

A steep or abrupt turn can push the airplane beyond the remaining energy and AOA margin even though the power setting has not changed.

---

### Weight and CG Change the Energy Requirement Before the Maneuver Begins

The same idea applies to **weight and center of gravity**.

A heavier airplane requires more lift.

To produce more lift at a given airspeed, the wing must operate at a higher AOA.

That means:

**more weight → more required lift → higher AOA → more induced drag → more power required**

Weight also increases stall speed.

Center of gravity changes the load the wing must carry.

With a forward CG, the horizontal tail generally must produce a larger downward force. The wing must then produce enough lift to support both airplane weight and that additional tail downforce.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Center_Gravity_Center_Lift_Distribution_Balance.png" alt="Forward and aft CG effects on tail downforce and wing lift" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>A forward CG increases tail downforce and the lift demanded from the wing, increasing the aerodynamic work required to maintain the same flight condition.</figcaption>
</figure>

Again, the ACS factors tie directly back to energy:

**Anything that increases required lift increases AOA and induced drag, which increases power required and reduces excess-energy margin.**

Weight also determines the correct maneuvering speed.

For the C172S:

| Weight | Maneuvering Speed |
|---|---:|
| **2,550 lb** | **105 KIAS** |
| **2,200 lb** | **98 KIAS** |
| **1,900 lb** | **90 KIAS** |

So the demonstration begins at **weight-appropriate VA**, not automatically at 105 KIAS.

---

### Configuration Changes the Power Required for the Same Energy State

The ACS requires the demonstration twice because changing configuration changes the airplane’s aerodynamic cost of maintaining the same altitude and airspeed.

Flaps change:

- camber,
- lift,
- drag,
- pitch attitude,
- trim,
- stall speed,
- and power required.

Extending flaps generally increases both lift capability and drag. The additional drag means more energy is being removed from the airplane for a given flight condition.

To maintain altitude and airspeed, thrust must increase enough to match the new drag.

That is why the landing-configuration demonstration has a different:

- sight picture,
- power setting,
- trim requirement,
- stall boundary,
- and pitch response.

For the C172S:

- **VFE at 10°: 110 KIAS**
- **VFE from more than 10° through 30°: 85 KIAS**
- **Best glide: 68 KIAS**
- **Normal flaps-down approach: 60–70 KIAS**
- **VS1 reference: 48 KIAS**
- **VS0 reference: 40 KIAS**

The stall speeds are references, not target speeds for the critically slow portion. The actual ACS endpoint is the **immediate-stall boundary** recognized through aircraft cues and stall-warning indications.

Because the C172S has fixed landing gear, landing-gear operating/extended speeds are **not applicable** for this airplane, but the applicant should recognize that they are part of the ACS concept for retractable-gear airplanes.

### Controllability Changes Because the Airplane Has Less Kinetic Energy

As the airplane slows, the control surfaces experience less dynamic pressure.

The result is:

- reduced control effectiveness,
- larger control deflections for the same response,
- heavier reliance on proper trim,
- less crisp pitch and roll response,
- and less margin for error.

That is why the ACS asks about **controllability**, not merely stall speed.

The low-energy airplane is not simply “going slower.” It is also less responsive at the same time that it is operating closer to critical AOA.

This changes the required control inputs across the envelope.

At faster airspeeds:

- smaller control inputs produce stronger responses,
- less AOA is required for level flight,
- and the airplane normally has greater excess-energy margin.

As the airplane slows:

- more aft elevator is required to maintain altitude,
- more trim is required to relieve that pressure,
- more power is required once established on the backside,
- and control inputs must become smoother and more deliberate.

In turns, load factor increases the energy demand.

In climbs, the airplane needs positive excess power—power available greater than power required.

In descents, the airplane can operate with negative excess power and allow stored altitude to decrease.

The same pitch, power, and trim principles therefore apply to **level flight, turns, climbs, and descents**; only the demanded path-speed energy state changes.

---

### Yaw and Coordination Also Have an Energy Cost

At critically slow airspeeds, coordination matters for two reasons:

1. uncoordinated flight increases drag and wastes energy;
2. near critical AOA, yaw can allow one wing to stall before the other and create spin potential.

The first connection is energy.

Any unnecessary yaw or slip/skid adds drag.

More drag means a higher power requirement to maintain the same altitude and airspeed.

The second connection is loss of control.

Near the slow edge, little AOA margin remains. Add yaw and the airplane may reach critical AOA asymmetrically.

That is why the ACS connects:

**yaw effects → controllability → stalls and spins**

rather than treating them as separate subjects.

---

### Left-Turning Tendencies Become Strongest in the Lowest-Energy Condition

The critically slow condition often combines:

- high AOA,
- relatively high power,
- and low airspeed.

Those are exactly the conditions that make left-turning tendencies most noticeable.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Left_Turning_Tendencies.png" alt="Four left-turning tendencies" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>High power and high AOA make the major left-turning tendencies especially noticeable during critically slow flight.</figcaption>
</figure>

#### Torque Reaction

High power increases the airplane’s reaction to propeller rotation, producing a left-roll tendency.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Left_Truning_Tendency_Torque_Reaction.png" alt="Torque reaction causing left roll tendency" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>Torque reaction becomes stronger as power increases.</figcaption>
</figure>

#### Spiraling Slipstream

The propeller slipstream strikes the left side of the vertical tail and yaws the nose left.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Left_Truning_Tendency_Corkscrew.png" alt="Spiraling slipstream around the fuselage" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>High power strengthens the corkscrewing slipstream and its left-yaw effect.</figcaption>
</figure>

#### P-Factor

At high AOA, the descending propeller blade produces more thrust than the ascending blade, creating a left-yawing moment.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Left_Truning_Tendency_PFactor.png" alt="P-factor at high angle of attack" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>The high AOA used in critically slow flight increases asymmetric propeller loading.</figcaption>
</figure>

#### Gyroscopic Precession

Gyroscopic precession may appear during pitch changes, although in this C172S demonstration it is normally less significant than torque, spiraling slipstream, and P-factor.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Left_Truning_Tendency_Gyroscopic_Precession.png" alt="Gyroscopic precession on a propeller airplane" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>Gyroscopic precession depends on the airplane and the direction and rate of pitch change.</figcaption>
</figure>

The energy-management consequence is that increasing power does not simply “add energy.”

At very slow airspeed, that power also creates stronger yawing and rolling tendencies that require control input.

The practical teaching point is:

**As power and AOA increase, anticipate increasing right rudder and maintain coordination.**

---

### Adverse Yaw Matters More as Control Effectiveness Decreases

Ailerons also create yaw.

The down-going aileron increases lift and induced drag on its wing. That drag initially yaws the airplane opposite the direction of the desired turn.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Adverse_Yaw_Lift_Diagram.png" alt="Adverse yaw caused by differential lift and drag" loading="lazy">
    <figcaption>Larger aileron deflections at slow airspeeds can create greater differential drag and adverse yaw.</figcaption>
</figure>

At critically slow airspeed, reduced control effectiveness can require larger aileron deflections.

That can increase adverse yaw.

So the airplane may simultaneously have:

- less control effectiveness,
- more rudder demand from left-turning tendencies,
- more adverse yaw from aileron use,
- high AOA,
- high induced drag,
- and very little stall margin.

That is why **coordination is part of energy management and loss-of-control prevention**, not a separate technique bolted onto the lesson.

---

### Environmental Conditions Change Either Power Available or Power Required

Environmental effects fit the same framework.

**High density altitude** reduces engine and propeller performance, reducing power available.

The power-required curve may not change much, but the power-available curve moves downward.

That reduces excess power everywhere and makes the backside more hazardous because the airplane reaches the point of **power required ≈ power available** sooner.

**Turbulence** can create rapid changes in AOA and load factor. Near the immediate-stall boundary, a gust can use the remaining AOA margin without the pilot intentionally changing pitch.

**Microbursts and wind shear** can rapidly change both airspeed and flightpath, producing major energy-state errors faster than the airplane may be able to correct them.

The risk is therefore not simply “bad weather.”

The risk is that the environment can:

- decrease available energy,
- increase required energy,
- or abruptly redistribute the airplane’s altitude and airspeed.

---

### Airspeed and Stall-Warning Instruments Tell the Pilot Where the Airplane Is in the Energy Story

The airspeed indicator is the primary cockpit reference for kinetic-energy state, but it has limitations and must be interpreted with aircraft feel and outside attitude.

Know the airplane’s marked operating ranges and limitations and do not exceed them while changing configuration.

The stall-warning system is different.

It does not measure energy directly. It warns that the wing is approaching critical AOA.

During this Task, the warning is expected near the critically slow boundary, but it must never become background noise.

**Every stall-warning indication is verbally acknowledged.**

The applicant should also recognize that buffet, horn, and other warning cues have operating ranges and limitations; the absence of one expected cue does not eliminate the need to manage AOA.

## Fly the ACS Story — Clean Configuration

The clean demonstration now becomes a practical walk across the power curve.

### 1. Start at Weight-Appropriate VA

Clear the area and select an altitude that allows the maneuver to be completed no lower than **1,500 feet AGL**.

Establish the evaluator-assigned heading and altitude.

Determine the correct C172S VA for actual weight:

- 2,550 lb — **105 KIAS**
- 2,200 lb — **98 KIAS**
- 1,900 lb — **90 KIAS**

Flaps remain **UP**.

Stabilize in level flight and describe:

- pitch attitude,
- power,
- trim,
- control feel,
- coordination.

This is the starting energy state: relatively high kinetic energy, low AOA, and comfortable control authority.

### 2. Slow to Best Glide

Reduce power while maintaining altitude.

As the airplane slows, gradually increase pitch to keep altitude constant and re-trim as needed.

Stabilize at **68 KIAS best glide** and note the **power setting required** to maintain level flight.

This gives a repeatable reference near the low-drag portion of the envelope before moving farther onto the backside.

### 3. Continue to the Immediate-Stall Boundary

Continue slowing while maintaining altitude.

Watch the entire energy story appear in the airplane:

- airspeed decreases,
- kinetic energy decreases,
- AOA increases,
- induced drag increases,
- power required increases,
- control effectiveness decreases,
- trim changes,
- rudder demand increases,
- stall margin decreases.

Continue until reaching the ACS condition where:

**any further increase in AOA, increase in load factor, or reduction in power would result in an immediate stall.**

Maintain level flight and note:

- **airspeed**
- **power setting**

Verbally acknowledge stall-warning indications.

This is the slow edge of the demonstrated energy state.

### 4. Trade Altitude for Airspeed — Without Changing Power

Now demonstrate the reason this Task exists.

**Do not change power.**

Lower the pitch attitude.

Reducing AOA reduces induced drag.

Allow the airplane to accelerate.

The airplane will lose some altitude because potential energy is being exchanged for kinetic energy.

Continue until the airplane reaches a faster airspeed and can again be established in steady level flight with the **same power setting**.

Note:

- **new airspeed**
- **altitude lost**

The teaching point is:

**Same power + lower AOA + lower induced drag = a faster sustainable airspeed.**

### 5. Return to Cruise

Return to normal cruise at the altitude and heading specified by the evaluator.

## Fly the Same Energy Story — Landing Configuration

The landing demonstration is the same experiment with a different drag configuration.

### 1. Start Again at Weight-Appropriate VA

Establish and maintain the correct VA for actual weight.

Describe pitch, power, and trim.

### 2. Configure Within the Airplane’s Limits

Slow below **110 KIAS** before extending 10° flaps.

Slow below **85 KIAS** before extending flaps beyond 10°.

Extend flaps fully to **30°**.

As configuration changes, explain what changed in the energy system:

- drag increased,
- required power changed,
- pitch attitude changed,
- trim changed,
- stall speed changed,
- and the sight picture changed.

### 3. Establish Reference Landing Speed

Slow to and maintain the evaluator-assigned reference landing speed.

For the C172S, the normal flaps-down approach range is **60–70 KIAS**.

Maintain level flight and note the **power setting required**.

### 4. Continue to the Immediate-Stall Boundary

Continue slowing while maintaining altitude and coordination.

Again observe:

- increasing AOA,
- increasing induced drag,
- increasing power required,
- decreasing control effectiveness,
- decreasing energy and stall margin.

Stop at the point where any further:

- increase in AOA,
- increase in load factor,
- or reduction in power

would result in an immediate stall.

Maintain level flight and note:

- **airspeed**
- **power setting**

Verbally acknowledge stall-warning indications.

### 5. Trade Altitude for Airspeed at the Same Power

Without changing power:

- lower pitch,
- reduce AOA,
- allow the airplane to accelerate,
- accept the required altitude loss,
- and reestablish level flight at the new faster airspeed.

Note:

- **new airspeed**
- **altitude lost**

The principle has not changed. The landing configuration simply changes the drag and power relationship.

### 6. Return to Cruise

Retract flaps incrementally within limitations.

Re-trim as required.

Return to normal cruise at the altitude and heading specified by the evaluator.

### Risk Management — Protect the Energy Margin

The risks in this Task all relate to either **losing awareness of the airplane’s energy state** or allowing the airplane to move beyond a limitation while the margin is small.

#### Airspeed Limits

Know and respect:

- VA for actual weight,
- VFE,
- best glide,
- reference landing speed,
- stall-speed references,
- and all airspeed-indicator markings.

Configuration changes must occur below the applicable limits.

#### Reversed Command

On the backside, slowing requires more power.

If the airplane is already near maximum available power, additional throttle may not be enough.

The airplane may have to **descend first to regain airspeed**, reduce AOA and drag, and move back to a condition with positive excess power.

#### Critical AOA and Stall Warning

The Task deliberately approaches critical AOA.

Do not chase altitude with excessive back pressure when the airplane is already slow.

That increases AOA, induced drag, and energy demand at exactly the wrong time.

Acknowledge every stall-warning indication.

#### Critically Slow Maneuvering

Keep banks shallow and inputs smooth.

An increase in load factor increases power required and stall speed.

Uncoordinated flight adds drag and creates spin potential.

#### Environmental Effects

High density altitude reduces power available.

Turbulence can increase AOA and load factor.

Wind shear or a microburst can rapidly alter airspeed and vertical path.

All three can reduce the time or energy available for recovery.

#### Collision Hazards

The maneuver requires substantial attention inside and outside.

Clear before beginning and continue an outside scan throughout.

### Teaching Close

The entire Task can be brought back to one progression:

**Start fast with plenty of kinetic energy and low AOA.**

Slow the airplane while holding altitude and kinetic energy decreases.

To keep producing the required lift, AOA increases.

As AOA rises, induced drag rises.

Past minimum power required, slower flight demands more power.

Load factor, weight, forward CG, flap drag, yaw, and environmental conditions can all increase the energy demand or reduce the energy available.

At the critically slow boundary, the airplane has very little AOA and excess-power margin left.

That is why more back pressure is the wrong answer.

**Lowering the nose reduces AOA and induced drag, trades altitude for airspeed, and moves the airplane back toward a more sustainable energy state.**

That is the energy story—but the purpose of telling it is to teach and demonstrate **every element of ACS Task AI.X.B**.



## K1 — Purpose and Procedure

This Task is a **demonstration**, not merely sustained slow flight. In each configuration, the applicant deliberately moves through distinct energy states, notes the power and control requirements, reaches the stall-warning boundary, verbally acknowledges it, and then recovers initially by **lowering pitch without changing power**.

[[highlight:
Task A versus Task B |
**Task A:** maneuver warning-free at a stable slow-flight speed. **Task B:** demonstrate changing characteristics down to the immediate-stall boundary, acknowledge the warning, then show how pitch-only acceleration changes the energy state.
]]

## K2 — Power Required from Cruise to Critical AOA

At cruise speed, parasite drag is the larger component of total drag. As speed decreases, parasite drag falls but induced drag rises. Near critically slow speed, induced drag and **power required** become high even though airspeed is low.

The demonstration pauses at selected speeds so the learner can compare:

- **Pitch attitude**
- **Power required for level flight**
- **Trim and control pressure**
- **Control effectiveness**
- **Yaw and coordination demand**
- **Warning cues and margin to critical AOA**

## K3 — Minimum Power Required and Reversed Command

The bottom of the power-required curve is the **minimum power required speed**. It separates:

- **Normal command:** faster flight requires more power; slower flight requires less.
- **Reversed command:** slower flight requires more power because induced drag rises rapidly.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
	<img src="/knowledge/Power_Curve.png" alt="Power-required curve showing minimum power and the region of reversed command" loading="lazy">
	<figcaption>Below minimum power required, slower level flight requires more power and becomes speed-unstable.</figcaption>
</figure>

The C172S does not publish minimum power required speed. **68 KIAS best glide** is a useful, observable reference near minimum drag, but it is not identical to minimum power required.

## K4 — Aerodynamic Relationships

As speed decreases in level flight, AOA and induced drag increase. Higher **weight**, forward **CG**, increased **load factor**, or an uncoordinated condition requires additional lift or changes stall behavior. At high AOA and power, left-turning tendencies increase while control effectiveness decreases.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
	<img src="/knowledge/Critical_AOA.png" alt="Airflow separation as angle of attack approaches and exceeds critical angle" loading="lazy">
	<figcaption>Critically slow flight is an AOA condition; the warning and stall do not occur at one universal speed.</figcaption>
</figure>

If power required exceeds power available, pitch alone cannot preserve altitude indefinitely. The pilot must **reduce AOA and trade altitude for airspeed** to reach a more favorable energy state.

## K5 — Pitch, Power, and Trim Inputs

From cruise toward critically slow flight:

- **Pitch attitude rises** to maintain altitude as speed decreases.
- **Power initially falls**, then rises after passing minimum power required.
- **Trim changes** reduce sustained pressure only after attitude and power are established.
- **Control displacement increases** as airflow and effectiveness decrease.
- **Right rudder demand increases** with high power and AOA.

During turns, climbs, and descents, each control change has a larger effect on the already-small energy margin. Make smooth inputs and continuously preserve coordination.

## K6 — Configuration Changes

Extending flaps changes camber, lift, drag, pitch, trim, and stall characteristics. In the landing configuration, the C172S generally requires a different pitch attitude and power setting at the same airspeed than when clean. Extend and retract flaps **incrementally**, within $V_{FE}$, while anticipating balloon/sink and trim change.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
	<img src="/knowledge/Slow_Flight.png" alt="Slow flight showing low airspeed, high angle of attack, high power, and constant altitude" loading="lazy">
	<figcaption>Configuration changes alter the sight picture and power required, but coordinated AOA control remains fundamental.</figcaption>
</figure>

The C172S has **fixed landing gear**, so landing-gear operating and extended speeds are **not applicable**. State that explicitly rather than inventing a value.

## K7 — C172S Airspeeds and Indicator Markings

| Airspeed | C172S value | Significance |
|---|---:|---|
| **$V_A$** | **105 / 98 / 90 KIAS** | At 2,550 / 2,200 / 1,900 lb; decreases with weight |
| **$V_{FE}$** | **110 KIAS at 10°** | Maximum with 10° flaps |
| **$V_{FE}$** | **85 KIAS beyond 10°** | Maximum from 10° through 30° |
| **Best glide** | **68 KIAS** | Flaps up; clean-demonstration intermediate point |
| **Normal approach, flaps 30°** | **60–70 KIAS** | Landing-configuration reference range |
| **Short-field approach, flaps 30°** | **61 KIAS** | Published short-field reference |
| **$V_{S1}$ reference** | **48 KIAS** | Lower green arc; max-weight, forward-CG, flaps-up reference |
| **$V_{S0}$ reference** | **40 KIAS** | Lower white arc; max-weight landing-configuration reference |

The **white arc (40–85 KIAS)** is the full-flap operating range; the **green arc (48–129 KIAS)** is the normal operating range. Airspeed indications vary with position/calibration error, and neither the colored-arc boundary nor a published 1G stall speed replaces AOA and warning awareness.

## K8 — Common Errors

### Configuration and Speed Errors

**Error:** Wrong configuration, improper entry, skipped best-glide/reference speed, or exceeded limitation.  
**Correction:** Brief the sequence and speeds before entry; call each gate and verify configuration before continuing.

### Recovery and Warning Errors

**Error:** Recovering with power instead of demonstrating pitch-only acceleration, or failing to acknowledge a warning.  
**Correction:** Call the warning immediately; hold power fixed; lower pitch; note speed gained and altitude lost; then complete recovery.

### Control and Scan Errors

**Error:** Altitude/heading excursions, poor coordination, torque correction, trim, or traffic scan.  
**Correction:** Use outside attitude references, coordinated inputs, stabilized trim, and concise teaching callouts.

## Risk Management

### Airspeed Knowledge and Limit Exceedance

**Hazard:** Misidentifying $V_A$, $V_{FE}$, best glide, approach, or stall references.  
**Recognition:** Configuration changes without speed confirmation or use of maximum-gross $V_A$ at a lighter weight.  
**Consequence:** Structural exceedance, configuration damage, or unsafe demonstration.  
**Mitigation:** Calculate weight-adjusted $V_A$, brief all gates, and cross-check the POH and airspeed indicator before flight.

### Reversed Command and Critical AOA

**Hazard:** Power required approaches or exceeds power available while AOA margin disappears.  
**Recognition:** High power, increasing pitch, continued speed decay, sink, poor control response, horn, or buffet.  
**Consequence:** Stall, spin, or loss of control.  
**Mitigation:** Maintain altitude margin, remain coordinated, acknowledge every warning, and lower pitch immediately at the planned recovery point.

### Warning-System Limitations

**Hazard:** Overreliance on one indication or normalization of a continuous warning.  
**Recognition:** Instrument fixation or failure to call horn/buffet.  
**Consequence:** Unrecognized critical AOA.  
**Mitigation:** Integrate sight, sound, feel, buffet, control response, airspeed trend, and installed warning devices.

### Environment, Collision, and Critically Slow Maneuvering

**Hazard:** Turbulence, microbursts, high density altitude, traffic, distraction, or aggressive maneuvering.  
**Recognition:** Uncommanded speed/AOA change, inadequate excess power, traffic conflict, or lost orientation.  
**Consequence:** Stall, inability to arrest descent, collision, or airspace deviation.  
**Mitigation:** Use conservative conditions, increase margins, clear continuously, limit maneuvering, and terminate whenever stable control cannot be assured.

## Scenario Application

**Situation:** In landing configuration at high density altitude, the airplane is at maximum allowable power, airspeed is decaying, and the pilot pulls to stop a descent.

**Discuss / Decide:** Why can more pitch worsen both airspeed and flightpath, and what energy exchange remains available?

**Desired Learner Outcome:** Recognize that power required may exceed power available. **Reduce AOA and accept altitude loss to regain airspeed**, then stabilize or go around from the improved energy state.

# Fly

## Setup

- **Clear:** Complete clearing turns and maintain an outside traffic scan.
- **Altitude:** Plan sufficient height for both demonstrations and complete no lower than **1,500 feet AGL**.
- **Heading / tolerances:** Note the assigned heading and altitude; brief **±100 feet**, **+5/-0 knots**, **±10° heading**, and **±5° bank**.
- **C172S gates:** Weight-adjusted $V_A$ **105/98/90 KIAS**; best glide **68 KIAS**; $V_{FE}$ **110 KIAS at 10° / 85 KIAS beyond 10°**; landing reference **60–70 KIAS at flaps 30°** or evaluator-assigned speed.
- **Warning brief:** Verbalize horn/buffet immediately; recover before a full stall.

## Instructor Demonstration — Clean Configuration

1. **$V_A$:** Gear fixed; flaps up. Establish the applicable **105/98/90 KIAS** and describe pitch, power, and trim.
2. **Best glide:** Reduce power and maintain altitude while slowing to **68 KIAS**; stabilize and note required power.
3. **Critically slow:** Continue slowing toward the immediate-stall boundary; maintain level flight and coordination; note the higher power, pitch, trim, and reduced controllability.
4. **Warning:** Verbally acknowledge the first valid stall warning.
5. **Pitch-only acceleration:** Without changing power, lower pitch and accelerate until level flight is reestablished; note the new airspeed and altitude lost.
6. **Return:** Restore normal cruise at the assigned altitude and heading.

## Instructor Demonstration — Landing Configuration

1. **$V_A$:** Begin at the applicable weight-adjusted maneuvering speed and describe the energy state.
2. **Configure:** Reduce speed below **110 KIAS** before 10° flaps and below **85 KIAS** before extending beyond 10°; extend to **30°** incrementally. Landing gear is fixed.
3. **Reference landing speed:** Stabilize at the evaluator-assigned speed; for the C172S, **60–70 KIAS with flaps 30°** is the normal-approach range. Note required power.
4. **Critically slow:** Continue slowing toward the immediate-stall boundary while maintaining level flight and coordination; note airspeed and power.
5. **Warning:** Verbally acknowledge the first valid stall warning.
6. **Pitch-only acceleration:** Without changing power, lower pitch and accelerate until level flight is reestablished; note airspeed and altitude lost.
7. **Return:** Retract flaps incrementally within limitations and return to normal cruise at the assigned altitude and heading.

## Learner Practice

Practice the **clean demonstration completely**, debrief the observations, then conduct the **landing demonstration**. Require concise callouts at every speed/configuration gate and a comparison of power required, pitch attitude, control effectiveness, yaw, and altitude loss.

## Analyze and Correct Common Errors

1. **Failure to establish the specified configuration** — Verify clean or full landing configuration before the applicable comparison point.
2. **Improper entry technique, including not beginning at weight-adjusted $V_A$** — Calculate and brief the applicable speed before entry.
3. **Failure to establish and maintain best glide before continuing to critically slow airspeed** — Pause at **68 KIAS**, stabilize, and note power.
4. **Excessive altitude or heading variation** — Use outside references and small coordinated corrections; do not rush the speed gates.
5. **Improper recovery using power instead of pitch** — Hold the power setting fixed and lower pitch as the demonstration requires.
6. **Uncoordinated flight controls** — Center the ball and coordinate every pitch/bank/power change.
7. **Improper correction for torque effect** — Anticipate increasing right rudder with power and AOA.
8. **Improper trim technique** — Stabilize attitude and power before trimming; do not use trim to fly through changing states.
9. **Unintentional stalls or unacknowledged stall indications** — Call the warning immediately and lower pitch before the full stall develops.
10. **Lack of familiarity with or exceeding airspeed limitations** — Use the POH speed card and call each limit before configuration change.

## Completion Standards

Conduct both demonstrations in sequence, observe all limitations, verbally acknowledge warning indications, demonstrate pitch-only acceleration at unchanged power, complete above **1,500 feet AGL**, maintain applicable ACS tolerances, return to the assigned altitude/heading, and teach and correct effectively.

# Review

## Oral / Checkride Review

### 1. What is the purpose of this demonstration?

[[answer:
To compare the power, pitch, trim, controllability, yaw, warning cues, and energy behavior of the airplane at progressively slower speeds in clean and landing configurations.
]]

### 2. What separates normal command from reversed command?

[[answer:
The minimum power required speed. Above it, faster flight requires more power; below it, slower level flight requires more power because induced drag rises rapidly.
]]

### 3. Why pause at 68 KIAS in the clean C172S demonstration?

[[answer:
It is the published best-glide speed and provides a repeatable reference near minimum drag from which to compare the higher power required at critically slow speed. It is not exactly minimum power required speed.
]]

### 4. Why must power remain unchanged during the initial recovery?

[[answer:
Holding power fixed demonstrates that lowering pitch trades altitude for airspeed and moves the airplane to a state where less power is required for level flight.
]]

### 5. Which C172S limitations govern flap extension?

[[answer:
Do not exceed 110 KIAS with 10° flaps or 85 KIAS when extending beyond 10° through 30°.
]]

### 6. What landing-configuration reference speed applies?

[[answer:
Use the evaluator-assigned speed. The C172S POH normal-approach range with flaps 30° is 60–70 KIAS; 61 KIAS is the published short-field approach speed.
]]

### 7. What must the applicant do at a stall warning?

[[answer:
Verbally acknowledge it immediately, then perform the specified pitch-only acceleration before a full stall develops.
]]

### 8. Why can high density altitude make the low-energy state unrecoverable without altitude loss?

[[answer:
Power available is reduced. If power required exceeds power available, the pilot must reduce AOA and exchange altitude for airspeed to move to a sustainable energy state.
]]
