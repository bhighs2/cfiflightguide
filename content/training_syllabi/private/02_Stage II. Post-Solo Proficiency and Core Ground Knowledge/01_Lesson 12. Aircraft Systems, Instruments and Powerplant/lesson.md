# Lesson 12. Aircraft Systems, Instruments and Powerplant

**Lesson:** 12  
**Stage:** Stage II — Post-Solo Proficiency and Core Ground Knowledge  
**Type:** GROUND + FLIGHT  
**Target Proficiency:** Introduced (instruments / VOR / hood) · Developing (systems)

## Objective

Develop the learner's understanding of the C172S powerplant and systems — electrical, fuel, pitot-static, and vacuum — and how each flight instrument works and what powers it, so that failures are diagnosed rather than merely reacted to. The learner will be introduced to the VOR receiver at the systems level and to attitude instrument flying under the hood, framed around the inadvertent VMC-into-IMC defense.

## Completion Standards

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

- Describe the C172S powerplant, electrical, fuel, pitot-static, and vacuum systems and how they interrelate.
- Explain how each flight instrument works and which system drives it — pitot-static (ASI, altimeter, VSI), gyroscopic (attitude, heading, turn), and the magnetic compass.
- Recognize the symptoms of a blocked pitot, blocked static, and vacuum failure, and state which instruments are affected.
- Apply the magnetic-compass acceleration (ANDS) and turning (UNOS) errors.
- Describe the VOR system at the instrument level: the receiver, the CDI, the OBS, the TO/FROM indication, nav-source selection, and the antenna/receiver/display chain.
- Tune and identify a VOR, center the needle, and interpret what the indication means.
- Maintain aircraft control by reference to instruments alone for basic attitude flight, and execute a controlled 180° turn under the hood.
- Explain why the hood work is an introduction and a defense against inadvertent VMC into IMC.

## Why This Matters

A pilot who knows what each instrument is telling them — and what powers it — is a pilot who can diagnose a failure instead of being surprised by it. A blocked pitot or a failed vacuum pump does not announce itself; it announces itself through an instrument that is now lying. Understanding the system behind the gauge is what turns a confusing panel into a solvable problem.

The hood work introduces the other half of that safety net. Inadvertent VFR flight into IMC is a leading killer in general aviation, and the defense is the ability to control the airplane by instruments alone, level off, and turn back to visual conditions. This lesson introduces that skill — not to instrument-fly, but to survive the day the horizon disappears.

## Knowledge

### 1. The Powerplant and Systems

The C172S powerplant produces thrust and drives the systems that support flight. The electrical system powers the panel and avionics; the fuel system feeds the engine; the pitot-static and vacuum systems drive the flight instruments.

Know the layout: what the alternator and battery do, how fuel flows and is selected, and which systems the flight instruments depend on.

### 2. The Pitot-Static Instruments

The pitot-static system drives three instruments. The **ASI** uses pitot against static pressure; the **altimeter** and **VSI** use static pressure alone. Understand each, and what a blockage does:

- **Blocked pitot, drain clear:** the ASI drops toward zero as pitot pressure is lost.
- **Blocked static:** the altimeter freezes, and the ASI and VSI read incorrectly with altitude changes.

### 3. The Gyroscopic Instruments and the Compass

The **attitude indicator** and **heading indicator** are gyroscopically stabilized, driven by the vacuum or electrical system. The **turn coordinator** is electrically driven. The **magnetic compass** needs no power but has acceleration (**ANDS**) and turning (**UNOS**) errors from magnetic dip.

A vacuum failure takes the attitude and heading indicators. Know which instruments remain — and which are lying.

### 4. The VOR at the Instrument Level

The VOR is a receiver and a display. The pilot interacts with the **receiver** (tune the frequency), the **CDI** (the course-deviation needle), the **OBS** (select the course), and the **TO/FROM** flag. The antenna, receiver, and display form a chain from the ground station to the cockpit.

- **Tune and identify:** set the frequency and confirm the station by its Morse identifier before trusting the needle.
- **Center the needle:** when the CDI centers, you are on the selected radial; the TO/FROM flag tells you whether the selected course takes you toward or away from the station.
- **Signal limitations:** line-of-sight, and the signal can be weak or erroneous at low altitude or distance.

This lesson stays at the instrument level — what the VOR is and how to read it. Navigation mastery comes later.

[[pdf: /knowledge/Pilots_Handbook_of_Aeronautical_Knowledge.pdf | start=203 | end=231]]

### 5. Attitude Instrument Flying — The Introduction

Attitude instrument flying controls the airplane by reference to the instruments alone, using **attitude plus power to produce performance**. Establish the attitude and power for the desired flight condition; the performance instruments confirm the result.

The scan is selective and brief — no fixation. The defense against inadvertent VMC into IMC is exactly this: trust the instruments over the body, level the airplane, and make a controlled 180° turn back to visual conditions. This lesson introduces the skill; Lesson 18 develops it.

## Risk Management

### Spatial Disorientation

#### Hazard

Without the natural horizon, the body's vestibular system produces false sensations of attitude and motion.

#### Consequence

The pilot trusts the illusion over the instruments and loses control — the core of the fatal VMC-into-IMC accident.

#### Recognition

A sensation that contradicts the instruments — a feeling of a turn or climb the panel does not show.

#### Mitigation

Trust the instruments, not the body. Fly attitude plus power and let the performance instruments confirm.

### Inadvertent VMC Into IMC

#### Hazard

Continued VFR flight into deteriorating weather until the horizon is lost.

#### Consequence

Spatial disorientation and loss of control — a leading cause of fatal GA accidents.

#### Recognition

Fading horizon, lowering ceiling, and reduced visibility with a temptation to continue.

#### Mitigation

Avoid it first — divert or land before the horizon is lost. If it happens, commit to the instruments, level off, and turn 180° back to VMC.

### Fixation and Chasing the Instruments

#### Hazard

Staring at a single instrument, or making repeated large corrections chasing a needle.

#### Consequence

Overcontrol, oscillation, and a scan that collapses.

#### Recognition

Attention locked on one gauge; large, repeated control inputs.

#### Mitigation

A brief, selective scan. Small corrections. Attitude plus power, then confirm — not chase.

### System Failure Misdiagnosis

#### Hazard

Not knowing which system powers a failed instrument, so the wrong conclusion is drawn.

#### Consequence

Trusting a lying gauge, or discarding a good one.

#### Recognition

Conflicting indications with no diagnosis of the cause.

#### Mitigation

Know what powers each instrument. A blocked pitot, blocked static, or vacuum failure has a recognizable signature — diagnose before you act.

### VOR Misuse

#### Hazard

Tuning a VOR without identifying it, or trusting a weak or erroneous signal.

#### Consequence

Navigating by the wrong station or a false needle.

#### Recognition

No Morse identifier confirmed, or a needle that disagrees with the known position.

#### Mitigation

Tune, identify, then trust. Cross-check the needle against position, and treat an unidentified or implausible signal as unusable.

### Unfamiliar Non-Towered Pattern

#### Hazard

Pattern work at a new, non-towered field (11R) with self-announced traffic and an unfamiliar layout.

#### Consequence

A traffic conflict or a pattern flown to the wrong runway or direction.

#### Recognition

An unfamiliar field, other traffic self-announcing, or uncertainty about the active runway.

#### Mitigation

Review the field before arrival, overfly to determine the active, self-announce on CTAF, and keep the outside scan dominant.

## Skills and Procedures

### Systems and Instruments Review

#### Purpose

Describe the C172S systems and how each flight instrument works and is powered, and diagnose the common failures.

#### Procedure

1. Trace the powerplant, electrical, fuel, pitot-static, and vacuum systems.
2. For each instrument, state what it shows and what powers it.
3. Diagnose a blocked pitot, a blocked static, and a vacuum failure from the panel.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Walk the panel and the systems, mapping each instrument to its source.

#### Learner Practice

The learner explains each system and instrument and diagnoses the failure signatures, with coaching.

#### Common Errors

- Not knowing what powers each instrument.
- Confusing a blocked pitot with a blocked static.
- Trusting a failed gauge instead of diagnosing the failure.
- Treating the magnetic compass as error-free.

#### Completion Standard

The learner can explain the systems, map each instrument to its power source, and diagnose the common failures, with coaching.

### VOR at the Instrument Level

#### Purpose

Use the VOR at the instrument level — tune, identify, center the needle, and interpret the indication.

#### Procedure

1. Tune the VOR frequency and identify the station by its Morse identifier.
2. Select a course with the OBS.
3. Center the CDI needle and interpret the TO/FROM indication.
4. Understand the antenna/receiver/display chain and the signal limitations.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate tuning, identifying, and centering the needle, interpreting TO/FROM.

#### Learner Practice

The learner tunes and identifies a VOR, centers the needle, and interprets the indication in flight, with coaching.

#### Common Errors

- Tuning without identifying the station.
- Misreading the TO/FROM flag.
- Chasing the needle with large heading changes.
- Trusting a weak or erroneous signal.

#### Completion Standard

The learner tunes and identifies a VOR, centers the needle, and interprets the indication, with coaching. Instrument level only — not navigation mastery.

### Attitude Instrument Flying (Hood) — Introduction

#### Purpose

Introduce control of the airplane by reference to instruments alone, as the defense against inadvertent VMC into IMC.

#### Procedure

1. Establish straight-and-level flight by instruments using attitude plus power.
2. Make gentle turns, climbs, and descents by instruments with a selective scan.
3. Simulate the loss of the horizon: trust the instruments, level the airplane, and make a controlled 180° turn.
4. Return to visual references and to VMC.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate attitude instrument flight and the controlled 180° turn under the hood.

#### Learner Practice

The learner maintains control under the hood through basic attitude maneuvers and a 180° turn, with coaching.

#### Common Errors

- Fixation on a single instrument.
- Chasing the needles with large corrections.
- Trusting the body over the instruments.
- Losing the scan during the 180° turn.

#### Completion Standard

The learner maintains control of the airplane under the hood for basic attitude maneuvers and a controlled 180° turn, with coaching. This is an introduction, not proficiency.

## Scenario Application

### Scenario 1 — The ASI Reads Low

**Situation:**

On climbout, the airspeed indicator reads lower than expected for the pitch attitude, and the reading keeps decaying.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- Which system could be at fault, and which instrument is affected?
- How do you confirm it without the ASI?
- What is the safe response?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Suspect a blocked pitot. Cross-check with pitch attitude, power, and the other instruments; fly attitude plus power. The ASI is one input — the airplane flies the same regardless.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 2 — The Frozen Altimeter

**Situation:**

In cruise, you notice the altimeter has not moved despite a long descent, and the VSI reads zero throughout.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What single failure explains both?
- Which instruments can you still trust?
- What does the POH provide for this?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

A blocked static port freezes the altimeter and affects the ASI and VSI. Use the alternate static source per the POH, and trust the gyroscopic instruments and the compass. Diagnose the system, not just the symptom.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 3 — The Unidentified VOR

**Situation:**

En route to 11R, you tune a VOR and center the needle, but you never listened to the Morse identifier.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What is wrong with trusting this needle?
- What does "tune and identify" protect against?
- What do you do now?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

An unidentified VOR may be the wrong station, off the air, or unreliable. Tune, identify, then trust. Identify the station now; if it cannot be positively identified, treat the signal as unusable.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 4 — The Horizon Disappears

**Situation:**

On the return leg, lowering cloud and haze erode the horizon until outside references are gone.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What is the immediate priority?
- What does the body tell you, and what do the instruments tell you?
- What is the maneuver that gets you back to VMC?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Commit to the instruments — attitude plus power — and level the airplane. Trust the instruments over the body's illusions. Make a controlled 180° turn back to visual conditions. This is the defense the hood work introduced.

**Expected Performance:** Introduced.

## Review and Evaluation

### Review Questions

1. Which instruments are pitot-static, which are gyroscopic, and what powers each?
2. What does a blocked pitot do to the ASI, and what does a blocked static port do to the panel?
3. What do ANDS and UNOS describe, and when do they appear?
4. What are the parts of the VOR the pilot interacts with, and what does "tune and identify" mean?
5. What does the CDI needle show, and what does the TO/FROM flag tell you?
6. What is the defense if the horizon is lost, and why is the hood work only an introduction?

[[answer:
1. Pitot-static: ASI (pitot and static), altimeter and VSI (static). Gyroscopic: attitude and heading indicators (vacuum or electrical) and the turn coordinator (electrical). The magnetic compass needs no power. Know what powers each gauge.
2. A blocked pitot lets the ASI drop toward zero as pitot pressure is lost. A blocked static port freezes the altimeter and makes the ASI and VSI read incorrectly with altitude changes; use the alternate static source per the POH.
3. ANDS — Accelerate North, Decelerate South — the compass acceleration error on easterly/westerly headings. UNOS — Undershoot North, Overshoot South — the turning error. Both come from magnetic dip.
4. The receiver, the CDI with the OBS, and the TO/FROM flag. "Tune and identify" means set the frequency and confirm the station by its Morse identifier before trusting the needle.
5. The CDI shows lateral deviation from the selected radial — centered means on it. The TO/FROM flag tells you whether the selected course takes you toward or away from the station.
6. Trust the instruments, level the airplane with attitude plus power, and make a controlled 180° turn back to VMC. The hood work is an introduction to that survival skill; the full maneuver set is developed in Lesson 18.
]]

- Applies ANDS and UNOS to the magnetic compass.
- Tunes and identifies a VOR, centers the needle, and interprets TO/FROM at the instrument level.
- Maintains control under the hood for basic attitude maneuvers and a 180° turn.
- Understands the hood work as an inadvertent-IMC defense, not instrument training.

# Flight Card

## Flight Setup

### Lesson Target

**Target Proficiency: Introduced (instruments / VOR / hood) · Developing (systems)**

Primary lesson target:

**Apply the systems-and-instruments ground school in the airplane, use the VOR at the instrument level en route to 11R, fly pattern work at the non-towered field, and receive an introduction to attitude instrument flying under the hood on the return.**

Structure:

**KDWH → 11R (en route, VMC) with VOR work · pattern work at 11R (VMC) · 11R → KDWH under the hood (introduction only).**

Boundary:

**The hood work is an introduction to the inadvertent-IMC defense — attitude by instruments and a controlled 180° turn — not the full basic-instrument maneuver set. That is Lesson 18.**

### Scope and Flexibility

This is an instructional plan, not a requirement to complete every element.

If weather, workload, or learner state require it:

- shorten the VOR work or the hood work,
- favor a clean, understood introduction over a rushed, full one,
- and keep the hood work brief and positive — it is an introduction.

Do not expand the hood work into the full maneuver set or partial-panel work.

---

## Preflight and Cockpit

### Systems and Flight Briefing Before Start

#### Procedure

1. Complete the C172S preflight/checklist.
2. Review the systems and what powers each instrument.
3. Brief the route to 11R, the VOR to be used, and the non-towered pattern.
4. Brief the hood work for the return: attitude plus power, a controlled 180° turn, return to VMC.
5. Confirm the view-limiting device and the safety-pilot arrangement.

#### Common Errors

- Not briefing the route or the VOR before departure.
- Unclear on the safety-pilot and hood plan.

---

## En Route to 11R — VOR Work (VMC)

#### Procedure

1. Depart KDWH and establish en route toward 11R.
2. Tune the VOR frequency and identify the station by its Morse identifier.
3. Select a course with the OBS and center the CDI needle.
4. Interpret the TO/FROM indication against the known position.
5. Discuss the antenna/receiver/display chain and the signal limitations.

#### Common Errors

- Tuning without identifying.
- Misreading TO/FROM.
- Chasing the needle with large heading changes.

---

## Pattern Work at 11R (VMC)

#### Procedure

1. Review 11R before arrival; overfly to determine the active runway.
2. Self-announce on CTAF and fly the pattern per Lesson 3.
3. Takeoffs and landings at the non-towered field.

#### Common Errors

- Unfamiliar-field disorientation.
- Not self-announcing or scanning at a non-towered field.

---

## Return to KDWH — Hood Introduction

#### Procedure

1. Climb to a safe altitude with the safety pilot watching for traffic.
2. Don the view-limiting device.
3. Establish straight-and-level flight by instruments using attitude plus power.
4. Make gentle turns, climbs, and descents with a selective scan.
5. Simulate the lost horizon: trust the instruments, level off, and make a controlled 180° turn.
6. Remove the hood, return to visual references, and return to KDWH.

#### Common Errors

- Fixation on a single instrument.
- Chasing the needles.
- Trusting the body over the instruments.
- Losing the scan in the turn.

---

## Return, Shutdown and Securing

#### Procedure

1. Rejoin the KDWH pattern per Lesson 3 and tower per Lesson 4.
2. Land, exit, fully clear, then checklist, and reorient.
3. Taxi to parking; C172S shutdown, secure, postflight, log discrepancies.
4. Log the cross-country and the instrument-instruction (hood) time.

#### Common Errors

- Heads-down before clear of the runway.
- Not logging the cross-country and hood time.

---

## Flight Completion Criteria

The flight portion is satisfactory when the learner demonstrates, with coaching:

- a developing ability to describe the systems and diagnose the common failures,
- tuning and identifying a VOR, centering the needle, and interpreting TO/FROM at the instrument level,
- a safe pattern at the non-towered 11R,
- maintained control under the hood for basic attitude maneuvers and a controlled 180° turn,
- and an understanding that the hood work is an introduction and an inadvertent-IMC defense.

The lesson may be repeated when the systems knowledge, VOR use, or hood control are not yet at the introductory standard.
