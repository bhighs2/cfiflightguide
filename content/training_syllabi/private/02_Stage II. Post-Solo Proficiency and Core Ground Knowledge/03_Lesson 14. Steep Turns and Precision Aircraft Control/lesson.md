# Lesson 14. Steep Turns and Precision Aircraft Control

**Lesson:** 14  
**Stage:** Stage II — Post-Solo Proficiency and Core Ground Knowledge  
**Type:** GROUND + FLIGHT  
**Target Proficiency:** Developing → ACS Ready

## Objective

Develop precision aircraft control by flying steep turns to the Private Pilot ACS standard and the four basic instrument maneuvers under the hood to their ACS tolerances. The learner will hold a 45°–60° bank steep turn to the entry altitude, airspeed, and rollout-heading standards by outside reference, and will fly straight-and-level, constant-airspeed climbs and descents, and turns to headings by instruments alone to the ACS standards.

## Completion Standards

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

- Explain the aerodynamics of a steep turn — the load factor and stall-speed increase with bank — and why power and back-pressure are added.
- Fly a steep turn to the ACS standard: maintain the entry altitude **±100 feet**, airspeed **±10 knots**, bank **±5°**, and roll out on the entry heading **±10°**.
- Establish the correct entry airspeed at or below maneuvering speed (Va) before a steep turn.
- Under the hood, maintain straight-and-level flight: altitude **±200 feet**, heading **±20°**, airspeed **±10 knots**.
- Under the hood, fly a constant-airspeed climb and a constant-airspeed descent, leveling off at the assigned altitude and then maintaining **±200 feet**, **±20°**, **±10 knots**.
- Under the hood, fly turns to headings: a standard-rate turn, rolling out on the assigned heading **±10°**, maintaining altitude **±200 feet** and airspeed **±10 knots**.
- Apply attitude-plus-power-equals-performance to hold the instrument tolerances.

## Why This Matters

Precision is the difference between flying the airplane and being flown by it. The steep turn demands it by outside reference: at 45°–60° of bank the load factor climbs, the stall speed rises, and the airplane wants to descend — holding altitude, airspeed, and bank to a tight tolerance while rolling out on the entry heading is genuine aircraft control. It is also the Private Pilot practical-test standard.

The hood work demands the same precision by instruments. Holding ±200 feet, ±20 degrees, and ±10 knots with no outside reference is the discipline that Lesson 12 introduced, now held to a real standard. A pilot who can hold a tolerance, by eyes or by instruments, is a pilot in command of the airplane.

## Concepts to Understand

## Knowledge

### 1. Steep-Turn Aerodynamics

In a steep turn, the bank angle increases the load factor, which raises the stall speed and adds drag. At 60° of bank the load factor is 2 G and the stall speed rises by roughly 40 percent. The entry is managed with **bank, back-pressure, and power**: establish the bank, add back-pressure to hold altitude against the load factor, and add power to offset the added drag.

Altitude deviation is the primary error. Correct it with proportional elevator back-pressure while the ailerons hold the bank constant. The maneuver is flown by **outside visual reference**, not the instruments.

### 2. The Steep-Turn ACS Standard

The Private Pilot ACS standard for the steep turn:

- Bank angle 45°–60°, held within **±5°**.
- Maintain the entry altitude within **±100 feet**.
- Maintain airspeed within **±10 knots**.
- Roll out on the entry heading within **±10°**.

Enter at or below maneuvering speed (Va) so a full or abrupt control input will not overstress the airplane.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/ACS_Private_Pilot.pdf | start=44 | end=44]]

[[pdf: /knowledge/POH-Cessna-172S.pdf | start=81 | end=81]]

### 3. The Hood Maneuvers and Their Tolerances

The four basic instrument maneuvers, each held to its ACS tolerance by instruments alone:

- **Straight-and-level:** altitude **±200 feet**, heading **±20°**, airspeed **±10 knots**.
- **Constant-airspeed climb:** climb at a constant airspeed, level off at the assigned altitude, then maintain **±200 feet**, **±20°**, **±10 knots**.
- **Constant-airspeed descent:** descend at a constant airspeed, level off at the assigned altitude, then maintain **±200 feet**, **±20°**, **±10 knots**.
- **Turns to headings:** a standard-rate turn, rolling out on the assigned heading **±10°**, maintaining altitude **±200 feet** and airspeed **±10 knots**.

Attitude plus power produces performance. Set the attitude and power for the flight condition; the instruments confirm — do not chase the needles.

## Risk Management

### Overstress / Exceeding Va

#### Hazard

Entering a steep turn above maneuvering speed or making abrupt control inputs.

#### Consequence

Structural overstress of the airplane.

#### Recognition

An entry airspeed above Va, or abrupt control inputs in the turn.

#### Mitigation

Enter at or below Va. Smooth, proportional control inputs — no abrupt full deflections.

### The Steep-Turn Stall Margin

#### Hazard

The rising load factor and stall speed bring the accelerated-stall boundary closer as the bank steepens.

#### Consequence

An accelerated stall at low altitude if the margin is ignored.

#### Recognition

Buffet or a stall warning as the bank and back-pressure build, or airspeed decaying.

#### Mitigation

Apply Lesson 5: respect the load-factor / stall-speed relationship. Manage airspeed with power and attitude, and reduce bank if the margin erodes.

### Losing Altitude — the Diving Steep Turn

#### Hazard

Failing to add back-pressure as the bank increases, so the airplane descends through the turn.

#### Consequence

Altitude loss — the primary steep-turn error — and a busted tolerance.

#### Recognition

The nose dropping and altitude unwinding as the bank steepens.

#### Mitigation

Bank, back-pressure, power on entry. Catch the altitude early with pitch while the ailerons hold the bank.

### Spatial Disorientation in the Hood

#### Hazard

Without outside reference, the body's illusions mislead, or the scan collapses into fixation.

#### Consequence

Loss of the tolerance, overcontrol, or disorientation.

#### Recognition

A sensation contradicting the instruments, or attention locked on one gauge.

#### Mitigation

Trust the instruments over the body. A brief, selective scan. Attitude plus power, then confirm.

## Skills and Procedures

### Steep Turns

#### Purpose

Fly a 360° steep turn at 45°–60° bank to the ACS standard, by outside visual reference.

#### Procedure

1. Clear the area; establish the entry at or below Va.
2. Roll into 45°–60° bank; add back-pressure and power as the bank builds.
3. Hold the bank constant with ailerons; hold altitude with proportional back-pressure.
4. Scan outside for traffic; note the entry heading.
5. Roll out on the entry heading, reducing back-pressure and power.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate a steep turn in each direction, verbalizing bank, back-pressure, power, and the rollout on the entry heading.

#### Learner Practice

The learner flies steep turns in both directions with coaching, toward the ACS standard.

#### Common Errors

Common errors when performing steep turns are:

1. Not clearing the area.
2. Inadequate pitch control on entry or rollout.
3. Gaining or losing altitude.
4. Failure to maintain constant bank angle.
5. Poor flight control coordination.
6. Ineffective use of trim.
7. Ineffective use of power.
8. Inadequate airspeed control.
9. Becoming disoriented.
10. Performing by reference to the flight instruments rather than visual references.
11. Failure to scan for other traffic during the maneuver.
12. Attempting to start recovery prematurely.
13. Failure to stop the turn on the designated heading.

#### Completion Standard

The learner flies steep turns toward the ACS standard — ±100 feet, ±10 knots, bank ±5°, rollout on the entry heading ±10° — by outside reference, with coaching.

### Hood Straight-and-Level

#### Purpose

Maintain straight-and-level flight by instruments alone to the ACS tolerance.

#### Procedure

1. Establish straight-and-level flight; set attitude and power.
2. Maintain altitude ±200 feet, heading ±20°, airspeed ±10 knots.
3. Use a brief, selective scan; small corrections.

#### Common Errors

- Fixation on one instrument.
- Chasing the needles.
- Trusting the body over the instruments.

#### Completion Standard

The learner maintains straight-and-level flight under the hood toward ±200 feet, ±20°, ±10 knots, with coaching.

### Hood Constant-Airspeed Climb and Descent

#### Purpose

Fly a constant-airspeed climb and a constant-airspeed descent by instruments, leveling off at an assigned altitude and holding the tolerance.

#### Procedure

1. Establish the climb or descent at the assigned constant airspeed, by instruments.
2. Level off at the assigned altitude.
3. Maintain altitude ±200 feet, heading ±20°, airspeed ±10 knots.

#### Common Errors

- Leveling off early or late.
- Airspeed drifting in the climb or descent.
- Losing the heading on the level-off.

#### Completion Standard

The learner flies a constant-airspeed climb and descent, leveling off at the assigned altitude and holding the tolerance, with coaching.

### Hood Turns to Headings

#### Purpose

Fly standard-rate turns to assigned headings by instruments, holding altitude and airspeed.

#### Procedure

1. Establish a standard-rate turn by instruments.
2. Hold altitude ±200 feet and airspeed ±10 knots through the turn.
3. Roll out on the assigned heading ±10°.

#### Common Errors

- Not holding a standard rate.
- Losing altitude or airspeed in the turn.
- Overshooting or undershooting the assigned heading.

#### Completion Standard

The learner flies standard-rate turns to assigned headings, rolling out ±10°, holding altitude and airspeed to tolerance, with coaching.

## Scenario Application

### Scenario 1 — The Diving Steep Turn

**Situation:**

Rolling into a 50° steep turn, you watch the bank and feel the nose drop. The altimeter unwinds 150 feet.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What is the primary steep-turn error, and what causes it?
- Which control fixes the altitude, and which holds the bank?
- What is the entry sequence that prevents it?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Altitude deviation is the primary error. Back-pressure (pitch) fixes the altitude while the ailerons hold the bank constant. Enter with bank, back-pressure, and power together so the descent never starts.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 2 — The Hot Entry

**Situation:**

You enter a steep turn 15 knots above maneuvering speed and pull hard to hold altitude.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What is the risk of a steep turn entered above Va?
- What does Va protect against?
- What is the correct entry airspeed discipline?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Above Va, a full or abrupt control input can overstress the airplane — and a steep turn adds load factor. Enter at or below Va, then use smooth, proportional inputs.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 3 — The Wandering Hood Altitude

**Situation:**

Under the hood in straight-and-level, you find yourself 250 feet low after staring at the attitude indicator.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What caused the altitude error?
- What does a brief, selective scan prevent?
- How do you correct back to the assigned altitude?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Fixation collapsed the scan. A brief, selective scan catches the drift early. Correct with a small pitch change and power, confirm on the instruments, and return to the scan — do not chase.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 4 — The Overshoot on the Rollout

**Situation:**

On a hood turn to an assigned heading, you roll out 15° past the heading.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What causes a rollout overshoot?
- What does the standard-rate turn give you?
- How do you time the rollout?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

A rollout started too late overshoots. A standard-rate turn gives a predictable heading change, so the rollout begins before the heading — lead it by roughly the bank's effect. Roll out on the assigned heading ±10°.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.
