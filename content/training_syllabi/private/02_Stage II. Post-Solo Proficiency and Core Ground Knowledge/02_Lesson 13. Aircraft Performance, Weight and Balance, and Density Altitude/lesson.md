# Lesson 13. Aircraft Performance, Weight and Balance, and Density Altitude

**Lesson:** 13  
**Stage:** Stage II — Post-Solo Proficiency and Core Ground Knowledge  
**Type:** GROUND + FLIGHT  
**Target Proficiency:** Developing

## Objective

Develop the learner's ability to plan a flight using the C172S performance charts, to compute and correct weight and balance, and to understand and apply density altitude. The learner will compute the day's performance numbers, fly a cross-country to 60R applying them, and — when the instructor simulates an engine failure near the airport — apply the engine-failure ABCs and land at 60R.

## Completion Standards

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

- Use the C172S performance charts to compute takeoff, climb, cruise, and landing performance for the day's conditions.
- Define pressure altitude, density altitude, and indicated altitude, and explain how density altitude affects performance.
- Compute the weight and balance for the flight and correct an out-of-CG loading.
- Explain the conditions that raise density altitude — high, hot, heavy, humid — and their effect on takeoff and climb.
- Apply the planned performance numbers in flight and recognize when actual performance differs.
- Respond to a simulated engine failure near the airport by applying the ABCs and landing at the field.
- Fly a safe pattern at a non-towered field.

## Why This Matters

Performance numbers are not abstract — they are the difference between a takeoff that clears the trees and one that does not. Density altitude is the silent factor: on a high, hot, humid day, the airplane performs as though it is thousands of feet higher than the field elevation, and a pilot who has not computed it can run out of runway without ever understanding why. Weight and balance is the other quiet limit — an out-of-CG airplane can be uncontrollable, and an overweight one may not climb at all.

This lesson ties the planning to the flying: compute the numbers on the ground, then fly them. And when the instructor quietly pulls the power near the destination, the learner discovers that the emergency skills are not a separate lesson — they are part of every approach.

## Knowledge

### 1. Altitude Types and Density Altitude

Three altitudes are not the same: **indicated altitude** (read from the altimeter), **pressure altitude** (above the standard datum, 29.92 inHg), and **density altitude** (pressure altitude corrected for nonstandard temperature). Density altitude is the altitude the airplane "feels."

Density altitude rises with **high elevation, high temperature, and humidity** — and the higher it is, the longer the takeoff roll, the weaker the climb, and the less power the engine makes. On a high, hot, heavy, humid day, a sea-level runway can perform like a mountain strip.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Pilots_Handbook_of_Aeronautical_Knowledge.pdf | start=257 | end=284]]

### 2. Aircraft Performance and the Charts

The C172S performance charts give takeoff, climb, cruise, and landing numbers for the day's actual conditions — pressure altitude, temperature, and weight. Using them is the difference between knowing and guessing.

Read the chart for the conditions, apply the correction factors, and carry the result into the flight. When actual performance differs from the plan, recognize it and act.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/POH-Cessna-172S.pdf | start=113 | end=135]]

[[pdf: /knowledge/Pilots_Handbook_of_Aeronautical_Knowledge.pdf | start=257 | end=284]]

### 3. Weight and Balance

Weight and balance is two limits, not one: **total weight** must be within limits, and the **center of gravity** must be within the CG envelope. An overweight airplane may not climb; an out-of-CG airplane may be uncontrollable.

Compute the weight and moment for the load, plot it on the envelope, and if it falls outside, correct the loading — move or remove load — until it is within limits.

## Risk Management

### High Density Altitude

#### Hazard

High elevation, high temperature, and humidity raise density altitude, degrading takeoff and climb performance.

#### Consequence

A longer takeoff roll than expected, a weak climb, or running out of runway.

#### Recognition

A high computed density altitude, a hot day, or a takeoff roll that is longer than the plan.

#### Mitigation

Compute the numbers for the actual conditions before flight. If the runway is too short for the density altitude and load, wait for cooler conditions or reduce load.

### Out-of-Limits Weight or CG

#### Hazard

Operating above gross weight or outside the CG envelope.

#### Consequence

Reduced climb, degraded control, or an uncontrollable airplane.

#### Recognition

A computed weight over the limit, or a CG that plots outside the envelope.

#### Mitigation

Compute the weight and balance before every flight. Correct an out-of-limits loading before departing — move or remove load.

### The Surprise Engine Failure

#### Hazard

An unexpected engine failure near the airport startles the learner and disrupts the approach.

#### Consequence

Denial or delay, a poorly flown approach, or a stall from stretching the glide.

#### Recognition

The learner freezing, reaching for the checklist before flying, or pulling to stretch toward the field.

#### Mitigation

ABCs — best glide, best field, checklist — fly the airplane first. The instructor manages the maneuver so the field is makeable; the learner applies the training.

### Unfamiliar-Field Pattern

#### Hazard

Pattern work at a new, non-towered field (60R) with an unfamiliar layout.

#### Consequence

A traffic conflict or a pattern flown to the wrong runway or direction.

#### Recognition

An unfamiliar field, self-announced traffic, or uncertainty about the active runway.

#### Mitigation

Review the field before arrival, overfly to determine the active, self-announce on CTAF, and keep the outside scan dominant.

### Skipping the Computation

#### Hazard

Departing without computing performance or weight and balance because "it always worked before."

#### Consequence

Discovering a too-short runway or an out-of-limits airplane in flight.

#### Recognition

A takeoff or climb that does not match the day, or a loading never plotted.

#### Mitigation

Compute performance and weight and balance before every flight. No exceptions for familiar runways or routine loads.

## Skills and Procedures

### Performance and Density-Altitude Computation

#### Purpose

Compute the day's takeoff, climb, cruise, and landing performance using the C172S charts and the actual density altitude.

#### Procedure

1. Determine the pressure altitude from the altimeter setting and field elevation.
2. Correct for temperature to find the density altitude.
3. Use the C172S charts to compute takeoff, climb, cruise, and landing performance for the density altitude and weight.
4. Compare the numbers to the available runway and the day's conditions.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate the density-altitude and performance computation with the day's actual numbers.

#### Learner Practice

The learner computes the day's density altitude and performance with coaching.

#### Common Errors

- Confusing pressure altitude with density altitude.
- Using standard-day numbers instead of the actual conditions.
- Misreading the chart or skipping the correction factors.
- Computing the numbers and then not flying them.

#### Completion Standard

The learner computes the day's density altitude and performance correctly and explains the effect on the flight, with coaching.

### Weight and Balance Computation

#### Purpose

Compute the weight and balance for the flight and correct an out-of-limits loading.

#### Procedure

1. Determine the weight of each item — occupants, fuel, baggage.
2. Compute the total weight and the total moment.
3. Plot the loaded weight and moment on the CG envelope.
4. If outside the envelope, move or remove load and recompute until within limits.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate the computation and the envelope plot.

#### Learner Practice

The learner computes the weight and balance and corrects a sample out-of-CG loading, with coaching.

#### Common Errors

- Computing weight but not CG.
- Plotting outside the envelope and departing anyway.
- Arithmetic errors in the moment computation.
- Not recomputing after a loading change.

#### Completion Standard

The learner computes the weight and balance correctly, plots it on the envelope, and corrects an out-of-limits loading, with coaching.

### Simulated Engine Failure — ABCs Applied

#### Purpose

Respond to a surprise simulated engine failure near the airport by applying the ABCs and landing at the field.

#### Procedure

1. On the simulated failure, establish best glide and trim.
2. Commit to the field — the destination — as the best field.
3. Run the checklist only once the airplane is flying and the field is chosen.
4. Fly the approach and land at 60R.

#### Instructor Demonstration

The instructor initiates the simulated failure at a controlled point and manages it so the field is makeable.

#### Learner Practice

The learner responds to the surprise failure with the ABCs and lands at the field, with coaching.

#### Common Errors

- Denial or delay on the unexpected failure.
- Not holding best glide.
- Checklist before the airplane and the field.
- Stretching the glide toward a stall.

#### Completion Standard

The learner responds to the surprise failure promptly, establishes best glide, commits to the field, and lands at 60R — with coaching.

## Scenario Application

### Scenario 1 — The Hot Afternoon Departure

**Situation:**

It is 95°F at KDWH, the field is near sea level, and you are at near-gross weight. A pilot says, "It's not a real runway-length problem at this elevation."

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What does the high temperature do to the density altitude?
- Why can a sea-level field perform like a mountain strip?
- What do you compute before you go?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

High temperature raises density altitude sharply even at low elevation. Compute the density altitude and the takeoff/climb numbers for the actual conditions — the runway performs to the density altitude, not the field elevation.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 2 — The Heavy Bag

**Situation:**

Loading for the flight, a heavy bag goes in the back of the baggage compartment. The computed CG plots aft of the limit.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- Is the airplane legal to fly?
- How do you correct an out-of-CG loading?
- Why does an aft CG matter?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

No — an out-of-envelope CG is not acceptable. Move the bag forward, redistribute, or remove load, then recompute. An aft CG degrades stability and can make recovery from a stall impossible.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 3 — The Quiet Power Reduction

**Situation:**

On approach to 60R, the engine goes quiet. You did not expect it.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What are your first three actions, in order?
- Where is your field?
- When do you touch the checklist?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

ABCs — best glide, best field (60R), checklist. Fly the airplane first. Commit to the field, manage the approach, and land. The checklist waits until the airplane is flying and the field is chosen.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 4 — Performance Off the Plan

**Situation:**

On the takeoff roll at 60R for the return, the acceleration is noticeably slower than the computed numbers predicted.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What could cause the difference?
- What do you do with runway remaining?
- What does "compute it, then fly it" demand of you here?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Actual performance can differ from the plan — wind, temperature rise, load. If the acceleration is wrong and runway is insufficient, reject the takeoff while you can. The numbers are a plan; the airplane tells you the truth.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

## Preflight and Cockpit

### Performance and Loading Briefing Before Start

#### Procedure

1. Determine the day's pressure altitude and temperature; compute the density altitude.
2. Compute the takeoff, climb, cruise, and landing performance for the conditions and weight.
3. Compute the weight and balance and plot it on the CG envelope.
4. Brief the route to 60R, the field, and the non-towered pattern.
5. Confirm the planned numbers are the plan the flight will be held to.

#### Common Errors

- Not computing density altitude or performance for the actual conditions.
- Computing weight but not CG.
- Departing with a loading outside the envelope.

---

## En Route to 60R

#### Procedure

1. Depart KDWH and establish en route toward 60R.
2. Apply the planned cruise performance.
3. Monitor actual against planned — recognize and account for any difference.

#### Common Errors

- Computing the numbers and then not flying them.
- Not recognizing when actual performance differs from the plan.

---

## Pattern Work at 60R

#### Procedure

1. Review 60R before arrival; overfly to determine the active runway.
2. Self-announce on CTAF and fly the pattern per Lesson 3.
3. Takeoffs and landings at the non-towered field.

#### Common Errors

- Unfamiliar-field disorientation.
- Not self-announcing or scanning at a non-towered field.

---

## Simulated Engine Failure Near 60R

#### Procedure

1. At a controlled point near the field, the instructor simulates an engine failure without warning.
2. The learner establishes best glide, commits to 60R as the best field, and runs the checklist once flying.
3. The learner flies the approach and lands at 60R.

#### Common Errors

- Denial or delay on the unexpected failure.
- Not holding best glide.
- Checklist before the airplane and the field.
- Stretching the glide toward a stall.

---

## Return, Shutdown and Securing

#### Procedure

1. Depart 60R and return to KDWH.
2. Rejoin the pattern per Lesson 3 and tower per Lesson 4.
3. Land, exit, fully clear, then checklist, and reorient.
4. Taxi to parking; C172S shutdown, secure, postflight, log discrepancies.
5. Log the cross-country time.

#### Common Errors

- Heads-down before clear of the runway.
- Not logging the cross-country time.

---

## Flight Completion Criteria

The flight portion is satisfactory when the learner demonstrates, with coaching:

- the day's performance and weight-and-balance computed correctly and applied in flight,
- recognition of density altitude and its effect on the day's flying,
- a safe pattern at the non-towered 60R,
- a prompt ABCs response to the surprise engine failure, landing at the field,
- and recognition of when actual performance differs from the plan.

The lesson may be repeated when the computation, its application, or the engine-failure response are not yet at the developing standard.
