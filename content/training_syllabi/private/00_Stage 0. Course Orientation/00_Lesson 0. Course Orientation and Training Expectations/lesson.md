# Lesson 0. Course Orientation and Training Expectations

## Lesson Information

- **Stage:** Stage 0 — Course Orientation
- **Lesson Type:** GROUND
- **Estimated Ground:** 1.0–1.5 hours
- **Estimated Flight:** None
- **Prerequisites:** Enrollment in the Private Pilot training syllabus
- **Target Proficiency:** Introduced

## Objective

Establish a clear understanding of how Private Pilot training will be conducted, how Flight Guide lessons are structured, what is expected of the learner and instructor, how progress will be evaluated, how FAA training and certification standards fit together, and how safety and risk management are incorporated from the first lesson.

The learner should leave this lesson understanding that pilot training is not simply the accumulation of flight hours. Training is a progressive development of knowledge, risk management, judgment, and aircraft-control skill leading to safe independent operation and eventual demonstration of the Private Pilot Airman Certification Standards.

## Completion Standards

At the completion of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

- Explain the overall progression of the Private Pilot training syllabus.
- Explain the purpose of the **Overview, Prepare, Brief, Fly, and Debrief** sections.
- Distinguish between regulatory minimum experience and actual proficiency.
- Describe the learner's responsibilities before, during, and after each lesson.
- Describe the instructor's responsibilities for instruction, evaluation, safety, endorsements, and training records.
- Explain the purpose of the FAA regulations, ACS, AIM, FAA handbooks, and advisory circulars at a basic level.
- Describe the Flight Guide proficiency scale.
- Explain the three-step positive exchange of flight controls.
- Explain why personal fitness, preparation, and willingness to speak up are essential to safe flight training.
- Describe the broad path from initial training through solo, cross-country, knowledge testing, practical-test preparation, and certification.
- Identify the next lesson and the preparation required before beginning aircraft training.

Detailed regulatory instruction occurs in later lessons. Lesson 0 establishes where these requirements fit in the overall training process.

### ACS

This lesson introduces the **Private Pilot Airplane Airman Certification Standards (ACS)** as the ultimate certification standard for the course.

The ACS integrates three dimensions of pilot performance:

- **Knowledge**
- **Risk Management**
- **Skill**

Flight Guide lessons progressively develop all three. Completion of a lesson means the learner has met that lesson's completion standards; it does not necessarily mean the learner is yet at practical-test standards in every associated ACS element.

## Why This Matters

A student who understands the training process can participate actively in it.

Good flight training is progressive. Each lesson should build on what came before, and each new skill should be connected to the knowledge and judgment required to use it safely. The goal is not merely to pass a practical test. The goal is to develop the habits, judgment, discipline, and aircraft-control skills of a safe pilot.

From the first lesson forward:

- Preparation matters.
- Procedures matter.
- Questions are encouraged.
- Risk management is part of every flight.
- Standards are not lowered simply to maintain a schedule.
- Solo and the practical test occur when the required training, experience, endorsements, and proficiency are in place.

## Concepts to Understand

Before the lesson, be prepared to discuss these ideas at a basic level:

- A Private Pilot certificate requires **knowledge, training, experience, judgment, and demonstrated proficiency**.

- Solo is a major milestone, but it is not the end goal.
- The FAA establishes regulatory requirements.
- The ACS establishes certification standards.

## Knowledge

### 1. What You Are Training For

The immediate goal of the course is eligibility and readiness for a **Private Pilot Certificate — Airplane Single-Engine Land**.

That certificate is earned through a combination of:

- Required aeronautical knowledge.
- Required ground and flight training.
- Required aeronautical experience.
- Required instructor endorsements.
- A passing FAA knowledge test.
- Demonstrated knowledge, risk management, and skill during the practical test.

These are related requirements, but they are not interchangeable.

For example:

- Having enough flight hours does not automatically demonstrate proficiency.
- Passing the knowledge test does not replace flight training.
- Performing a maneuver once does not necessarily demonstrate consistent proficiency.
- Meeting a lesson standard does not necessarily mean every associated ACS element is already practical-test ready.

The course develops all of these pieces progressively.

### 2. The FAA Training and Certification Framework

At a basic level, the learner should understand the role of several major sources.

#### 14 CFR

The Code of Federal Regulations contains the rules.

For this course, Parts **61** and **91** are especially important.

Part 61 addresses pilot certification, training, endorsements, aeronautical experience, and testing requirements.

Part 91 addresses operating rules that govern much of general aviation flight.

#### Airman Certification Standards

The Private Pilot ACS describes the standards used during certification.

It integrates:

- Knowledge
- Risk Management
- Skill

The ACS is not intended to be studied only during the final week before a practical test. Flight Guide introduces and develops ACS-related knowledge, risks, and skills throughout training.

#### Aeronautical Information Manual

The AIM provides important information on the national airspace system, air traffic procedures, communications, airport operations, and recommended operating practices.

#### FAA Handbooks

FAA handbooks provide the foundational knowledge and instructional explanations used throughout training.

#### Advisory Circulars

Advisory circulars provide FAA guidance on specific subjects. AC 61-65K is particularly important to instructors because it addresses pilot and instructor certification, testing, and endorsements.

### 3. Flight Guide Lesson Architecture

Every training lesson begins with an **Overview**.

The Overview answers:

- What is this lesson?
- What are we trying to accomplish?
- What standards apply?
- What references support the lesson?
- Why does the lesson matter?

The operating training cycle is:

[[mnemonic: PREPARE → BRIEF → FLY → DEBRIEF | Prepare before training. Brief objectives, knowledge, procedures, and risks. Fly or apply the lesson when applicable. Debrief performance and determine the next step.]]

#### Prepare

Preparation occurs before meeting with the instructor.

It may include:

- FAA reading.
- Videos.
- Aircraft-specific study.
- Regulations.
- Study questions.
- Planning exercises.
- Review of previous material.

Preparation allows instructor time to be spent clarifying, applying, and evaluating instead of simply introducing every topic from zero.

#### Brief

The Brief is instructor-led ground instruction associated with the lesson.

It develops:

- Knowledge.
- Procedures.
- Risk management.
- Decision-making.
- Scenario application.
- Expected performance.

Some lessons in this syllabus are entirely ground-based. In those lessons, the Brief is the primary instructional event.

#### Fly

The Fly section is the flight application.

It is designed as a concise training card rather than a replacement for the POH, checklist, regulations, or instructor briefing.

Not every lesson contains a flight component.

#### Debrief

The Debrief closes the learning loop.

It should identify:

- What went well.
- What needs improvement.
- Whether the completion standard was met.
- What should be practiced.
- What preparation is required next.

A good debrief is specific. "Good flight" is not enough.

### 4. Course Stages

The Private Pilot syllabus is organized into six stages.

#### Stage 0 — Course Orientation

Establish how the course works and what is expected.

#### Stage I — Pre-Solo Foundations

Develop the knowledge, procedures, aircraft control, airport operations, regulations, judgment, and proficiency required before the learner operates the airplane alone.

This stage culminates in:

- Pre-solo knowledge review/testing.
- Pre-solo flight proficiency evaluation.
- Required endorsements.
- First solo when appropriate.

#### Stage II — Post-Solo Proficiency and Core Ground Knowledge

Build deeper aircraft knowledge and expand maneuver proficiency after the learner has demonstrated basic independent operating ability.

Subjects include:

- Aircraft systems.
- Performance.
- Weight and balance.
- Density altitude.
- Aeromedical factors.
- ADM and risk management.
- Regulations.
- Basic instrument flight.
- Emergency operations.

#### Stage III — Weather, Navigation and Cross-Country

Develop the knowledge and flight skills necessary to plan and conduct cross-country operations.

Subjects include:

- Weather theory.
- Weather products.
- VFR charts.
- Navigation systems.
- Pilotage.
- Dead reckoning.
- Fuel and performance planning.
- Diversions.
- Cross-country decision-making.

This stage culminates in solo cross-country readiness.

#### Stage IV — Night and Solo Cross-Country

Complete required night training and progressively develop independent cross-country experience.

#### Stage V — Certification Preparation

Integrate the entire course and prepare for certification.

This includes:

- Knowledge-test deficiency review.
- ACS integration.
- Practical-test preparation flights.
- Mock oral evaluation.
- Mock practical test.
- Final requirements and logbook audit.
- Instructor recommendation when all standards are satisfied.

### 5. Lesson Numbers Are Not Flight Numbers

A numbered lesson represents a training objective, not a promise that the objective will be completed in exactly one flight.

A learner may:

- Repeat a lesson.
- Combine portions of compatible lessons.
- Return to an earlier objective.
- Progress more quickly through familiar material.
- Require additional practice before moving forward.

The instructor should preserve the logical sequence of training while adapting the pace to the individual learner.

### 6. Flight Guide Proficiency Scale

The following terms are used throughout the syllabus.

#### Introduced

The learner has been exposed to the knowledge, risk, skill, or procedure and can participate with substantial instructor assistance.

#### Developing

The learner understands the basic concept and can perform or apply it with coaching, but consistency, accuracy, judgment, or workload management still needs improvement.

#### Proficient

The learner can perform or apply the task safely and consistently at the level expected for the current stage with limited instructor assistance.

#### ACS Ready

The learner consistently demonstrates the applicable ACS knowledge, risk management, and skill standards at practical-test readiness.

#### Needs Additional Training

The learner has not yet demonstrated the required lesson or stage completion standard and should receive additional instruction or practice before progression.

The purpose of these terms is to describe current performance clearly—not to label the learner.

### 7. Learner Responsibilities

The learner is expected to become increasingly responsible for the entire flight operation.

From the beginning, the learner should:

- Complete assigned preparation.
- Arrive on time and ready to learn.
- Bring required materials and documents.
- Ask questions when something is unclear.
- Admit when something is not understood.
- Identify personal limitations.
- Report illness, fatigue, medication use, stress, or other relevant fitness concerns.
- Participate actively in risk assessment.
- Use checklists and approved procedures.
- Accept feedback professionally.
- Review the debrief and complete the next assignment.
- Avoid practicing a maneuver or procedure independently unless authorized and prepared to do so.
- Never allow schedule pressure, money already spent, passengers, pride, or a desired milestone to override safety.

As training progresses, the learner should increasingly make the decisions rather than wait for the instructor to make them.

### 8. Instructor Responsibilities

The instructor is responsible for providing organized instruction and maintaining an environment in which effective learning can occur.

The instructor should:

- Define the objective and completion standard.
- Verify appropriate preparation.
- Teach required knowledge and procedures.
- Demonstrate proper technique when appropriate.
- Identify and manage instructional risk.
- Provide meaningful practice.
- Correct unsafe or improper habits early.
- Evaluate performance objectively.
- Provide useful feedback.
- Record required training.
- Provide endorsements only when applicable requirements are met.
- Model the standards and safety practices being taught.
- Adjust instruction to the learner without lowering required standards.

The instructor may take control of the aircraft whenever necessary for safety, demonstration, or instruction.

### 9. Training Records and Endorsements

Pilot training produces a record.

Depending on the training event, this may include:

- Logbook entries.
- Ground training entries.
- Flight training entries.
- Instructor signatures.
- Solo endorsements.
- Solo cross-country endorsements.
- Knowledge-test endorsements.
- Practical-test endorsements.
- Aeronautical experience records.

An endorsement is not merely administrative paperwork. It is an instructor certification that specified regulatory requirements have been met.

The learner should understand what an instructor is signing and should learn to maintain accurate records.

### 10. Solo Is a Milestone, Not the Objective

The first solo is an important event because the learner becomes the sole occupant and pilot responsible for the airplane.

It is not a race.

Solo occurs only after the applicable:

- Ground training.
- Flight training.
- Pre-solo knowledge testing.
- Demonstrated proficiency.
- Instructor review.
- Required endorsements.

are complete.

A learner should never compare solo timing with another student as a measure of pilot potential.

The correct question is:

**Am I prepared to operate safely and make appropriate decisions without the instructor in the airplane?**

### 11. Cross-Country Progression

Solo cross-country flight requires another level of preparation.

Before independent cross-country operations, the learner will develop:

- Weather interpretation.
- Route planning.
- Airspace knowledge.
- Navigation.
- Communications.
- Aircraft performance planning.
- Fuel planning.
- Diversion skills.
- Lost procedures.
- Risk assessment.
- Unfamiliar-airport operations.

The course therefore treats cross-country privileges as a separate training gate rather than an automatic extension of local solo privileges.

### 12. The Knowledge Test

The FAA knowledge test evaluates required aeronautical knowledge.

The learner should understand from the beginning that:

- Written-test preparation is part of the training program.
- Memorizing question-bank answers alone is not the course objective.
- Knowledge should be connected to actual flight decisions and operations.
- Any deficiencies identified by the knowledge test will later be reviewed as part of practical-test preparation.

### 13. The Practical Test

The practical test is the final certification evaluation.

It generally includes:

- An oral evaluation.
- A flight evaluation.

The applicant must arrive with the required eligibility, experience, endorsements, documents, and test results.

The practical test samples the applicant's ability to integrate knowledge, risk management, and flight skill. Training therefore aims beyond isolated maneuver performance.

### 14. Safety Culture From the First Lesson

Safety procedures are habits.

They work best when established before workload becomes high.

From the first lesson forward:

- Checklists are used deliberately.
- Distractions are controlled.
- Uncertainty is verbalized.
- Risks are discussed.
- Procedures are standardized.
- Unsafe shortcuts are corrected.
- The instructor and learner communicate clearly.
- Either person may stop or modify an activity for safety.

### 15. Personal Fitness and IMSAFE

Pilot decision-making begins before walking to the airplane.

[[mnemonic: IMSAFE | Illness · Medication · Stress · Alcohol · Fatigue · Emotion/Eating]]

The instructor should introduce IMSAFE as a routine personal check.

Lesson 17 develops aeromedical factors and ADM in detail. For now, the learner should develop one habit:

**If you are uncertain whether you are fit to fly, say so before the flight.**

The same principle applies to:

- Weather.
- Aircraft condition.
- Training readiness.
- Comfort with a maneuver.
- Confusion about instructions.
- Any other safety concern.

### 16. Scheduling, Continuity, and Preparation

Regular training generally supports better continuity because less time is spent relearning previously developed skills.

However, scheduling pressure must never become operational pressure.

A lesson may need to change because of:

- Weather.
- Aircraft availability.
- Maintenance.
- Instructor availability.
- Learner fitness.
- Learner preparation.
- Training progress.
- Airspace or airport conditions.

A cancelled flight can often become a productive ground lesson.

The goal is consistent progress, not simply keeping an airplane reservation.


## Risk Management

### External Pressure

A common training risk is allowing a desired date or milestone to become a reason to accept unnecessary risk.

Examples include:

- "I need to solo this week."
- "I already paid for the airplane."
- "My family came to watch."
- "I need to finish before vacation."
- "I only have one more flight scheduled before the checkride."

The schedule serves the training program.

The training standards do not serve the schedule.

### Under-Preparation

Arriving unprepared increases workload and reduces the time available for application and practice.

If preparation is incomplete, the instructor may:

- Complete the missing instruction on the ground.
- Modify the lesson.
- Delay a flight.
- Continue with only the portions for which the learner is adequately prepared.

### Ambiguous Aircraft Control

Unclear control transfer is unacceptable.

The exchange should be verbal and confirmed every time.

### Reluctance to Speak Up

A learner should never remain silent because they are worried about appearing inexperienced.

You are in training because you are learning.

Questions, uncertainty, and concerns should be communicated early.

### Instructor Intervention

During training, the instructor may:

- Coach verbally.
- Demonstrate.
- Guard the controls.
- Take the controls.

The learner should not resist an instructor taking control.

Once the instructor states **"I have the flight controls,"** the learner should acknowledge and release control.


## Skills

### Positive Exchange of Flight Controls

#### Purpose

Eliminate uncertainty regarding who is controlling the airplane.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Instructor explains and demonstrates the three-step verbal exchange.

[[mnemonic: YOU HAVE → I HAVE → YOU HAVE | Positive exchange of flight controls: the person transferring control states the transfer, the receiving pilot accepts it, and the transferring pilot confirms it.]]

#### Learner Practice

Practice both directions.

**Instructor to learner**

1. "You have the flight controls."
2. "I have the flight controls."
3. "You have the flight controls."

**Learner to instructor**

1. "You have the flight controls."
2. "I have the flight controls."
3. "You have the flight controls."

The wording reflects the perspective of the person speaking during each transfer.

#### Completion Standard

The learner correctly performs the exchange without prompting and explains why visual confirmation is also important.

### Pre-Lesson Personal Check

#### Purpose

Develop the habit of evaluating personal fitness before training.

#### Learner Practice

Use IMSAFE and identify any item that should be discussed with the instructor before flight.

#### Completion Standard

The learner can recall the IMSAFE categories and understands that uncertainty should trigger discussion rather than concealment.

### Lesson Flow

#### Purpose

Establish a repeatable training process.

#### Learner Practice

Explain:

1. What happens during Prepare.
2. What happens during Brief.
3. What happens during Fly.
4. What happens during Debrief.

#### Completion Standard

The learner can explain the purpose of each phase and knows where to find assigned material in Flight Guide.


## Scenario Application

### Scenario 1 — The First Solo Date

You have told friends and family that you expect to solo Saturday morning.

Saturday arrives with gusty winds near the limits of your current experience. Your instructor says the conditions are technically flyable but are not appropriate for your first solo.

#### Discuss

- What external pressure exists?
- Who makes the decision?
- Does delaying the solo represent failure?
- What could be accomplished instead?

### Scenario 2 — Unclear Control Transfer

During a demonstration, the instructor reaches for the controls but does not make a clear verbal transfer. You are unsure whether you are still flying.

#### Discuss

- What should you say immediately?
- Why is guessing unacceptable?
- How does the standard transfer procedure prevent this problem?

### Scenario 3 — Not Prepared

You arrive for a flight lesson but did not complete the assigned reading and cannot explain the procedure scheduled for that day.

#### Discuss

- Should you hide that fact?
- What options does the instructor have?
- Why might additional ground instruction be safer and more productive than continuing with the planned flight?

### Scenario 4 — Not Fit to Learn

You slept poorly, have a severe headache, and are concerned that cancelling will waste money and delay training.

#### Discuss

- Which IMSAFE categories may be affected?
- What should you tell the instructor?
- What alternatives exist to the planned flight?

- Basic cockpit familiarization.

The learner should bring:

- Flight Guide access.
- Logbook.
- Headset, if available.
- Appropriate pilot documents.
- Aircraft checklist, if assigned.
- Questions from the assigned preparation.


# Debrief

The Debrief closes the learning loop.

It should identify:

- What went well.
- What needs improvement.
- Whether the completion standard was met.
- What should be practiced.
- What preparation is required next.

A good debrief is specific. "Good flight" is not enough.

### 4. Course Stages

The Private Pilot syllabus is organized into six stages.

#### Stage 0 — Course Orientation

Establish how the course works and what is expected.

#### Stage I — Pre-Solo Foundations

Develop the knowledge, procedures, aircraft control, airport operations, regulations, judgment, and proficiency required before the learner operates the airplane alone.

This stage culminates in:

- Pre-solo knowledge review/testing.
- Pre-solo flight proficiency evaluation.
- Required endorsements.
- First solo when appropriate.

#### Stage II — Post-Solo Proficiency and Core Ground Knowledge

Build deeper aircraft knowledge and expand maneuver proficiency after the learner has demonstrated basic independent operating ability.

Subjects include:

- Aircraft systems.
- Performance.
- Weight and balance.
- Density altitude.
- Aeromedical factors.
- ADM and risk management.
- Regulations.
- Basic instrument flight.
- Emergency operations.

#### Stage III — Weather, Navigation and Cross-Country

Develop the knowledge and flight skills necessary to plan and conduct cross-country operations.

Subjects include:

- Weather theory.
- Weather products.
- VFR charts.
- Navigation systems.
- Pilotage.
- Dead reckoning.
- Fuel and performance planning.
- Diversions.
- Cross-country decision-making.

This stage culminates in solo cross-country readiness.

#### Stage IV — Night and Solo Cross-Country

Complete required night training and progressively develop independent cross-country experience.

#### Stage V — Certification Preparation

Integrate the entire course and prepare for certification.

This includes:

- Knowledge-test deficiency review.
- ACS integration.
- Practical-test preparation flights.
- Mock oral evaluation.
- Mock practical test.
- Final requirements and logbook audit.
- Instructor recommendation when all standards are satisfied.

### 5. Lesson Numbers Are Not Flight Numbers

A numbered lesson represents a training objective, not a promise that the objective will be completed in exactly one flight.

A learner may:

- Repeat a lesson.
- Combine portions of compatible lessons.
- Return to an earlier objective.
- Progress more quickly through familiar material.
- Require additional practice before moving forward.

The instructor should preserve the logical sequence of training while adapting the pace to the individual learner.

### 6. Flight Guide Proficiency Scale

The following terms are used throughout the syllabus.

#### Introduced

The learner has been exposed to the knowledge, risk, skill, or procedure and can participate with substantial instructor assistance.

#### Developing

The learner understands the basic concept and can perform or apply it with coaching, but consistency, accuracy, judgment, or workload management still needs improvement.

#### Proficient

The learner can perform or apply the task safely and consistently at the level expected for the current stage with limited instructor assistance.

#### ACS Ready

The learner consistently demonstrates the applicable ACS knowledge, risk management, and skill standards at practical-test readiness.

#### Needs Additional Training

The learner has not yet demonstrated the required lesson or stage completion standard and should receive additional instruction or practice before progression.

The purpose of these terms is to describe current performance clearly—not to label the learner.

### 7. Learner Responsibilities

The learner is expected to become increasingly responsible for the entire flight operation.

From the beginning, the learner should:

- Complete assigned preparation.
- Arrive on time and ready to learn.
- Bring required materials and documents.
- Ask questions when something is unclear.
- Admit when something is not understood.
- Identify personal limitations.
- Report illness, fatigue, medication use, stress, or other relevant fitness concerns.
- Participate actively in risk assessment.
- Use checklists and approved procedures.
- Accept feedback professionally.
- Review the debrief and complete the next assignment.
- Avoid practicing a maneuver or procedure independently unless authorized and prepared to do so.
- Never allow schedule pressure, money already spent, passengers, pride, or a desired milestone to override safety.

As training progresses, the learner should increasingly make the decisions rather than wait for the instructor to make them.

### 8. Instructor Responsibilities

The instructor is responsible for providing organized instruction and maintaining an environment in which effective learning can occur.

The instructor should:

- Define the objective and completion standard.
- Verify appropriate preparation.
- Teach required knowledge and procedures.
- Demonstrate proper technique when appropriate.
- Identify and manage instructional risk.
- Provide meaningful practice.
- Correct unsafe or improper habits early.
- Evaluate performance objectively.
- Provide useful feedback.
- Record required training.
- Provide endorsements only when applicable requirements are met.
- Model the standards and safety practices being taught.
- Adjust instruction to the learner without lowering required standards.

The instructor may take control of the aircraft whenever necessary for safety, demonstration, or instruction.

### 9. Training Records and Endorsements

Pilot training produces a record.

Depending on the training event, this may include:

- Logbook entries.
- Ground training entries.
- Flight training entries.
- Instructor signatures.
- Solo endorsements.
- Solo cross-country endorsements.
- Knowledge-test endorsements.
- Practical-test endorsements.
- Aeronautical experience records.

An endorsement is not merely administrative paperwork. It is an instructor certification that specified regulatory requirements have been met.

The learner should understand what an instructor is signing and should learn to maintain accurate records.

### 10. Solo Is a Milestone, Not the Objective

The first solo is an important event because the learner becomes the sole occupant and pilot responsible for the airplane.

It is not a race.

Solo occurs only after the applicable:

- Ground training.
- Flight training.
- Pre-solo knowledge testing.
- Demonstrated proficiency.
- Instructor review.
- Required endorsements.

are complete.

A learner should never compare solo timing with another student as a measure of pilot potential.

The correct question is:

**Am I prepared to operate safely and make appropriate decisions without the instructor in the airplane?**

### 11. Cross-Country Progression

Solo cross-country flight requires another level of preparation.

Before independent cross-country operations, the learner will develop:

- Weather interpretation.
- Route planning.
- Airspace knowledge.
- Navigation.
- Communications.
- Aircraft performance planning.
- Fuel planning.
- Diversion skills.
- Lost procedures.
- Risk assessment.
- Unfamiliar-airport operations.

The course therefore treats cross-country privileges as a separate training gate rather than an automatic extension of local solo privileges.

### 12. The Knowledge Test

The FAA knowledge test evaluates required aeronautical knowledge.

The learner should understand from the beginning that:

- Written-test preparation is part of the training program.
- Memorizing question-bank answers alone is not the course objective.
- Knowledge should be connected to actual flight decisions and operations.
- Any deficiencies identified by the knowledge test will later be reviewed as part of practical-test preparation.

### 13. The Practical Test

The practical test is the final certification evaluation.

It generally includes:

- An oral evaluation.
- A flight evaluation.

The applicant must arrive with the required eligibility, experience, endorsements, documents, and test results.

The practical test samples the applicant's ability to integrate knowledge, risk management, and flight skill. Training therefore aims beyond isolated maneuver performance.

### 14. Safety Culture From the First Lesson

Safety procedures are habits.

They work best when established before workload becomes high.

From the first lesson forward:

- Checklists are used deliberately.
- Distractions are controlled.
- Uncertainty is verbalized.
- Risks are discussed.
- Procedures are standardized.
- Unsafe shortcuts are corrected.
- The instructor and learner communicate clearly.
- Either person may stop or modify an activity for safety.

### 15. Positive Exchange of Flight Controls

There should never be doubt about who is flying the airplane.

The standard Flight Guide control-transfer procedure is:

[[mnemonic: YOU HAVE → I HAVE → YOU HAVE | Positive exchange of flight controls: the person transferring control states the transfer, the receiving pilot accepts it, and the transferring pilot confirms it.]]

Example:

**Instructor:** "You have the flight controls."

**Learner:** "I have the flight controls."

**Instructor:** "You have the flight controls."

The exchange should be accompanied by visual confirmation.

When control is returned, the same process is used in the opposite direction.

The pilot currently flying remains responsible for the controls until the transfer is positively acknowledged.

Never assume the other person has control.

### 16. Personal Fitness and IMSAFE

Pilot decision-making begins before walking to the airplane.

[[mnemonic: IMSAFE | Illness · Medication · Stress · Alcohol · Fatigue · Emotion/Eating]]

The instructor should introduce IMSAFE as a routine personal check.

Lesson 17 develops aeromedical factors and ADM in detail. For now, the learner should develop one habit:

**If you are uncertain whether you are fit to fly, say so before the flight.**

The same principle applies to:

- Weather.
- Aircraft condition.
- Training readiness.
- Comfort with a maneuver.
- Confusion about instructions.
- Any other safety concern.

### 17. Scheduling, Continuity, and Preparation

Regular training generally supports better continuity because less time is spent relearning previously developed skills.

However, scheduling pressure must never become operational pressure.

A lesson may need to change because of:

- Weather.
- Aircraft availability.
- Maintenance.
- Instructor availability.
- Learner fitness.
- Learner preparation.
- Training progress.
- Airspace or airport conditions.

A cancelled flight can often become a productive ground lesson.

The goal is consistent progress, not simply keeping an airplane reservation.

## Risk Management

### External Pressure

A common training risk is allowing a desired date or milestone to become a reason to accept unnecessary risk.

Examples include:

- "I need to solo this week."
- "I already paid for the airplane."
- "My family came to watch."
- "I need to finish before vacation."
- "I only have one more flight scheduled before the checkride."

The schedule serves the training program.

The training standards do not serve the schedule.

### Under-Preparation

Arriving unprepared increases workload and reduces the time available for application and practice.

If preparation is incomplete, the instructor may:

- Complete the missing instruction on the ground.
- Modify the lesson.
- Delay a flight.
- Continue with only the portions for which the learner is adequately prepared.

### Ambiguous Aircraft Control

Unclear control transfer is unacceptable.

The exchange should be verbal and confirmed every time.

### Reluctance to Speak Up

A learner should never remain silent because they are worried about appearing inexperienced.

You are in training because you are learning.

Questions, uncertainty, and concerns should be communicated early.

### Instructor Intervention

During training, the instructor may:

- Coach verbally.
- Demonstrate.
- Guard the controls.
- Take the controls.

The learner should not resist an instructor taking control.

Once the instructor states **"I have the flight controls,"** the learner should acknowledge and release control.

## Skills and Procedures

Lesson 0 is a ground lesson, but several procedures should be practiced verbally.

### Procedure — Positive Exchange of Flight Controls

#### Purpose

Eliminate uncertainty regarding who is controlling the airplane.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Instructor explains and demonstrates the three-step verbal exchange.

#### Learner Practice

Practice both directions:

**Instructor to learner**

1. "You have the flight controls."
2. "I have the flight controls."
3. "You have the flight controls."

**Learner to instructor**

1. "You have the flight controls."
2. "I have the flight controls."
3. "You have the flight controls."

The wording reflects the perspective of the person speaking during each transfer.

#### Completion Standard

The learner correctly performs the exchange without prompting and explains why visual confirmation is also important.

### Procedure — Pre-Lesson Personal Check

#### Purpose

Develop the habit of evaluating personal fitness before training.

#### Learner Practice

Use IMSAFE and identify any item that should be discussed with the instructor before flight.

#### Completion Standard

The learner can recall the IMSAFE categories and understands that uncertainty should trigger discussion rather than concealment.

### Procedure — Lesson Flow

#### Purpose

Establish a repeatable training process.

#### Learner Practice

Explain:

1. What happens during Prepare.
2. What happens during Brief.
3. What happens during Fly.
4. What happens during Debrief.

#### Completion Standard

The learner can explain the purpose of each phase and knows where to find assigned material in Flight Guide.

## Scenario Application

### Scenario 1 — The First Solo Date

You have told friends and family that you expect to solo Saturday morning.

Saturday arrives with gusty winds near the limits of your current experience. Your instructor says the conditions are technically flyable but are not appropriate for your first solo.

**Discuss:**

- What external pressure exists?
- Who makes the decision?
- Does delaying the solo represent failure?
- What could be accomplished instead?

### Scenario 2 — Unclear Control Transfer

During a demonstration, the instructor reaches for the controls but does not make a clear verbal transfer. You are unsure whether you are still flying.

**Discuss:**

- What should you say immediately?
- Why is guessing unacceptable?
- How does the standard transfer procedure prevent this problem?

### Scenario 3 — Not Prepared

You arrive for a flight lesson but did not complete the assigned reading and cannot explain the procedure scheduled for that day.

**Discuss:**

- Should you hide that fact?
- What options does the instructor have?
- Why might additional ground instruction be safer and more productive than continuing with the planned flight?

### Scenario 4 — Not Fit to Learn

You slept poorly, have a severe headache, and are concerned that cancelling will waste money and delay training.

**Discuss:**

- Which IMSAFE categories may be affected?
- What should you tell the instructor?
- What alternatives exist to the planned flight?
