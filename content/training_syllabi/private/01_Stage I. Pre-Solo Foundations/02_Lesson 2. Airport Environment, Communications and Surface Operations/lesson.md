# Lesson 2. Airport Environment, Communications and Surface Operations

**Lesson:** 2  
**Stage:** Stage I - Pre-Solo Foundations  
**Type:** GROUND + FLIGHT  
**Target Proficiency:** Developing

## Objective

Develop the learner's ability to plan, communicate, and safely conduct airport-surface operations while maintaining positive aircraft control, situational awareness, and disciplined runway-incursion-avoidance habits.

Using David Wayne Hooks Memorial Airport (KDWH) as the primary working environment, the learner should begin connecting the airport diagram, current airport information, clearances or intentions, signs, markings, lighting, wind, traffic, and actual aircraft position into one continuous surface picture.

## Completion Standards

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

- Orient the KDWH airport diagram to the airplane's actual position and intended route.
- Identify common runway, taxiway, ramp, movement-area, and hold-short features.
- Recognize the meaning and operational significance of common airport signs, markings, and lighting.
- Obtain and organize relevant airport information before movement.
- Brief a taxi route, including the first turn, runway crossings, hold-short points, hot spots, and likely decision points.
- Make or respond to appropriate towered and nontowered radio communications at an introductory/developing level.
- Read back and understand a taxi instruction rather than merely repeat it.
- Maintain positive aircraft control during taxi using appropriate speed, braking, outside scanning, and wind-control positioning.
- Stop and resolve uncertainty before entering, crossing, or approaching a runway when the clearance, route, sign, marking, or aircraft position is unclear.
- Explain the major human-factor contributors to runway incursions, including expectation bias, distraction, rushed operation, communication error, and loss of positional awareness.
- Reapply Lesson 1 basic airwork while demonstrating improved outside-reference flying, coordination, trim use, and workload management.

This lesson develops safe habits with instructor supervision. Practical-test-level independence is not expected yet.

## Why This Matters

The airport surface is an active operating environment. Safe taxiing requires more than steering the airplane from parking to the runway.

The pilot must continuously connect:

- the airport diagram,
- the clearance or intended route,
- signs and markings,
- wind,
- traffic,
- radio calls,
- runway status,
- and the airplane's actual position.

A runway incursion often begins as a small breakdown in that picture: an expected turn, a missed sign, a rushed radio call, a misunderstood clearance, or a pilot who continues moving while uncertain.

## Knowledge

### 1. Airport Orientation and Surface Areas

Before the airplane moves, the learner should be able to answer:

- Where are we?
- Where are we going?
- What is the first turn?
- What runways might we approach or cross?
- Where are the holding positions?
- Are there published hot spots?
- What frequency or communication method applies?
- What will make us stop?

Distinguish among:

- ramp or parking areas,
- movement and non-movement areas,
- taxiways,
- runways,
- runway safety areas,
- holding positions,
- and other protected surfaces.

The purpose is not vocabulary alone. Each feature should lead to an operational decision.

### 2. Airport Diagram Use

An airport diagram should be used **before movement**, not only after the pilot becomes uncertain.

A useful taxi briefing includes:

1. Current aircraft position.
2. Destination or assigned runway.
3. First turn.
4. Taxiway sequence.
5. Runway crossings.
6. Hold-short locations.
7. Hot spots or confusing intersections.
8. Current frequency.
9. Expected stopping point.
10. What to do if the clearance changes.

During taxi, periodically compare:

**Diagram → Clearance / Intention → Outside Picture → Actual Position**

[[highlight:
Key Point |
Never allow the airplane to continue moving simply because the route "looks familiar." Verify that the actual signs and markings agree with the route you believe you are following.
]]

### 3. Airport Information Before Movement

Surface planning may depend on:

- current wind,
- runway in use,
- ATIS or other airport information,
- NOTAMs,
- runway or taxiway closures,
- construction,
- airport frequencies,
- local procedures,
- and current traffic.

The learner should begin developing the habit of turning information into action rather than merely collecting it.

Example:

A closed taxiway is not just a NOTAM item. It changes the route brief.

### 4. Airport Signs

Teach the learner to connect **color → meaning → required action**.

#### Mandatory Instruction Signs

Typically red with white characters.

They identify locations where compliance, authorization, or special caution is required, including runway holding positions.

The learner should immediately ask:

**Am I required to stop here?**

#### Location Signs

Typically black with yellow characters/border.

They answer:

**Where am I now?**

#### Direction Signs

Typically yellow with black characters and arrows.

They answer:

**Which way is the named taxiway or destination?**

#### Destination Signs

Used to point toward destinations such as:

- runways,
- ramps,
- terminals,
- or other airport areas.

The learner should not simply name the sign. The learner should state the next airplane action.

### 5. Airport Markings

Important surface markings include:

- taxiway centerlines,
- enhanced taxiway centerlines,
- runway holding-position markings,
- runway designations,
- runway centerlines and threshold markings,
- movement/non-movement boundary markings,
- and other protected-area markings.

#### Runway Holding-Position Marking

The double-solid/double-dashed yellow marking is a critical runway boundary.

When approaching from the taxiway side, stop before the **solid lines** unless the operation is authorized and safe.

[[highlight:
Safety |
The hold-short line is a decision point. Do not cross it while still trying to decide what the clearance meant.
]]

### 6. Airport Lighting Relevant to Surface Operations

Introduce the lighting cues that support surface awareness, including as applicable:

- runway edge lighting,
- taxiway edge lighting,
- taxiway centerline lighting,
- runway guard lights,
- stop bars,
- runway status lighting,
- and airport beacon awareness.

The learner should understand what the cue means operationally rather than memorizing colors without context.

### 7. Wind Indicators and Taxi Control

Use available wind indicators and current weather information to determine wind direction.

Then connect the wind to:

- airplane orientation,
- control positioning,
- taxi speed,
- and steering/braking workload.

The objective is not rote control positioning. It is understanding that the control position should protect the airplane from the actual wind.

### 8. Towered Airport Communications

A useful initial-call structure is:

- facility being called,
- aircraft identification,
- position,
- request,
- and current information code when applicable.

For a taxi clearance:

1. Listen completely.
2. Identify the assigned runway and route.
3. Identify any hold-short instruction.
4. Read back the important instruction.
5. Locate the route on the airport diagram.
6. Clarify anything that does not make sense.
7. Move only after the route is understood.

The learner must understand the clearance, not merely repeat the words.

### 9. Nontowered Airport Communications

At a nontowered airport:

- use the published advisory frequency,
- make appropriate position and intention reports,
- listen for other traffic,
- verify the runway environment visually,
- and remember that radio communication does not create a clearance or guarantee that the runway is clear.

The learner remains responsible for seeing, avoiding, and making a safe runway-entry decision.

### 10. Progressive Taxi and Clarification

If the route is confusing or the airplane's position is uncertain, requesting assistance is appropriate.

Teach the learner to use plain, direct communication:

- request a repeat,
- request clarification,
- state the uncertainty,
- or request progressive taxi when needed.

There is no operational benefit to pretending to understand.

### 11. Runway Incursion Avoidance - Deep Dive

Runway-incursion prevention is a primary Lesson 2 subject.

The prevention chain begins **before the airplane moves**.

#### Before Taxi

- Review the airport diagram.
- Identify hot spots.
- Understand the likely route.
- Note runway crossings.
- Identify the expected holding position.
- Organize the cockpit before movement.

#### During Taxi

- Maintain a conservative speed.
- Keep the scan primarily outside.
- Keep the diagram available.
- Verify each turn with signs and markings.
- Avoid unnecessary heads-down tasks.
- Listen for changes to the clearance.
- Verbalize approaching runway boundaries.

#### Approaching a Runway

Look for:

- red mandatory instruction signs,
- runway holding-position markings,
- enhanced taxiway centerlines,
- runway guard lights,
- runway pavement markings,
- runway traffic,
- and the runway designation itself.

At a towered airport, do not enter or cross a runway without the required ATC authorization.

At a nontowered airport, verify the runway is clear, communicate as appropriate, and make a deliberate safe-entry decision.

#### Human Factors Behind Incursions

Common contributors include:

- expectation bias,
- distraction,
- task saturation,
- misunderstanding a clearance,
- accepting a blocked or incomplete transmission,
- rushing,
- unfamiliar airport geometry,
- following another airplane without independently verifying the route,
- and continuing while unsure of position.

#### When Unsure

1. Stop.
2. Maintain aircraft control.
3. Reorient using the diagram and outside signs/markings.
4. Communicate uncertainty if needed.
5. Proceed only after the route and runway status are understood.

[[highlight:
Safety |
A pilot should never cross a runway boundary simply because another airplane crossed, because the route is normally used, or because stopping feels inconvenient.
]]

## Risk Management

### Expectation Bias

#### Hazard

The pilot expects the usual route, runway, or instruction and begins acting on expectation rather than the actual clearance or airport environment.

#### Consequence

Wrong turn, missed hold-short point, runway incursion, or conflict with another aircraft or vehicle.

#### Recognition

The clearance, signs, markings, diagram, or actual airplane position do not match what the pilot expected.

#### Mitigation

Stop, compare the route with the diagram, identify the actual position, and clarify before continuing.

### Distraction and Heads-Down Time

#### Hazard

The pilot performs radio, checklist, avionics, or organizational tasks while the airplane continues moving.

#### Consequence

Missed signs, turns, traffic, vehicles, hold lines, or runway boundaries.

#### Recognition

The learner cannot immediately state the airplane's position and next surface action.

#### Mitigation

Slow or stop. Complete high-workload tasks while stationary whenever practical.

### Unclear or Blocked Communication

#### Hazard

The pilot accepts an incomplete or misunderstood transmission.

#### Consequence

Incorrect route, missed restriction, unauthorized runway entry, or loss of shared understanding with ATC.

#### Recognition

The learner cannot accurately state the assigned route or identify it on the diagram.

#### Mitigation

Ask for a repeat or clarification and remain stopped until the instruction is understood.

### Excessive Taxi Speed

#### Hazard

The airplane moves faster than the learner can comfortably scan, orient, communicate, and stop.

#### Consequence

Reduced time to recognize a sign, make a turn, avoid traffic, or stop before a boundary.

#### Recognition

Urgent braking, missed cues, wide turns, or inability to maintain an effective outside scan.

#### Mitigation

Reduce power early and maintain a speed that preserves stopping and decision-making margin.

### Wind-Control Error

#### Hazard

Flight controls are positioned without considering the actual wind direction and airplane orientation.

#### Consequence

Reduced control margin, unnecessary workload, or inappropriate control positioning in stronger winds.

#### Recognition

The learner uses a memorized control position without first identifying the wind.

#### Mitigation

Identify wind direction, compare it with airplane orientation, position controls deliberately, and reassess after turns.

### Runway Incursion

#### Hazard

The aircraft enters or crosses a runway or protected runway surface incorrectly or while the pilot is uncertain of authorization, position, or traffic.

#### Consequence

Collision risk, pilot deviation, loss of separation, or aircraft damage.

#### Recognition

Approaching runway signs, holding-position markings, enhanced centerline markings, guard lights, runway intersections, or a published hot spot.

#### Mitigation

Stop before the runway boundary, confirm runway/clearance status, scan, and enter or cross only when the operation is understood and safe.

## Skills

### Airport Diagram Orientation and Taxi-Route Briefing

#### Purpose

Turn airport information and a taxi instruction or intended route into a surface plan that can be verified while the airplane is moving.

#### Procedure

1. Identify the airplane's actual position.
2. Identify the intended runway or destination.
3. Trace the route.
4. Identify the first turn.
5. Identify every runway crossing.
6. Identify each hold-short point.
7. Identify published hot spots or confusing intersections.
8. Identify the current frequency.
9. Brief the expected stopping point.
10. State what will cause the airplane to stop and reorient.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate one normal route and one changed route.

Show how to reorient after deliberately introducing uncertainty.

#### Learner Practice

Brief at least:

- one departure taxi route,
- one return route,
- and one changed-clearance route.

#### Common Errors

- Starting to taxi before understanding the route.
- Briefing from memory rather than the current diagram.
- Missing runway crossings or hold-short points.
- Failing to update the plan after a changed clearance.
- Continuing after the outside picture no longer matches the planned route.

#### Completion Standard

The learner can orient the diagram, trace the route, identify runway risk points, and explain what to do if position or routing becomes uncertain.

### Taxi Control and Wind Positioning

#### Purpose

Maintain positive aircraft control while preserving enough attention to understand the airport environment.

#### Procedure

1. Complete the applicable checklist.
2. Confirm the immediate route before movement.
3. Clear the area.
4. Begin moving smoothly.
5. Perform the brake check.
6. Maintain a conservative taxi speed.
7. Keep the primary scan outside.
8. Use centerline and pavement references appropriately.
9. Apply wind-control positioning based on actual wind and airplane orientation.
10. Slow or stop before workload exceeds available attention.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate:

- speed control,
- brake check,
- centerline tracking,
- turns,
- wind-control changes after direction changes,
- and stopping before a high-workload task.

#### Learner Practice

Taxi with progressively less prompting while verbalizing:

- present position,
- next turn,
- approaching runway,
- and next planned stop.

#### Common Errors

- Taxiing too fast.
- Excessive braking instead of controlling speed early.
- Prolonged heads-down fixation.
- Failing to verify position after a turn.
- Rote wind-control positioning unrelated to actual wind.
- Continuing to taxi while confused.

#### Completion Standard

The learner maintains safe speed and direction, uses appropriate wind controls, keeps an effective outside scan, and stops when workload or uncertainty requires it.

### Towered Taxi Communication and Readback

#### Purpose

Create a shared understanding of the intended surface operation.

#### Procedure

1. Listen before transmitting.
2. Make the initial call with aircraft identification, position, request, and airport information as applicable.
3. Listen to the entire response.
4. Identify runway, route, and restrictions.
5. Read back the important instruction.
6. Trace or confirm the route on the diagram.
7. Ask for clarification when anything is uncertain.
8. Begin or continue taxi only after the instruction is understood.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate:

- a normal taxi request,
- a changed route,
- a hold-short instruction,
- and a request for progressive taxi.

#### Learner Practice

Practice realistic KDWH calls and clearances before engine start, then apply them during the flight when appropriate.

#### Common Errors

- Transmitting before listening.
- Speaking too quickly.
- Repeating words without understanding the route.
- Omitting an important hold-short restriction.
- Failing to compare the clearance to the diagram.
- Guessing after a blocked or incomplete transmission.

#### Completion Standard

The learner can make or respond to a surface communication clearly, identify the route and restrictions, and request clarification when necessary.

### Nontowered Surface Communication

#### Purpose

Use radio communication to improve shared awareness without confusing an advisory transmission with an ATC clearance.

#### Procedure

1. Confirm the published frequency.
2. Listen before transmitting.
3. Identify the airport, aircraft, position, and intention.
4. Listen for conflicting traffic.
5. Continue visual scanning.
6. Verify runway status before entry or crossing.
7. Make additional calls as appropriate to the operation.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Model concise, useful position and intention reports.

#### Learner Practice

Practice nontowered calls verbally or during an appropriate operation.

#### Common Errors

- Treating a radio call as permission to enter the runway.
- Failing to listen before transmitting.
- Overly long calls.
- Incomplete position or intention information.
- Relying on radio instead of visual traffic scanning.

#### Completion Standard

The learner explains the distinction between advisory communication and ATC clearance and can make a concise, useful surface call.

### Signs, Markings, and Lighting Recognition

#### Purpose

Connect airport visual cues to the correct airplane action.

#### Procedure

For each cue:

1. Identify it before passing it.
2. State what it means.
3. State the required airplane action.
4. Confirm that action against the diagram and current operation.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Use the diagram and actual airport surface to identify:

- location signs,
- direction signs,
- mandatory instruction signs,
- runway holding-position markings,
- enhanced taxiway centerlines,
- and available lighting cues.

#### Learner Practice

During taxi, call out relevant cues early enough to act on them.

#### Common Errors

- Naming a sign without understanding the required action.
- Confusing location and direction information.
- Looking for signs too late.
- Missing the solid side of a runway holding-position marking.
- Allowing the diagram to replace outside observation.

#### Completion Standard

The learner recognizes common cues and connects each cue to an appropriate taxi, stop, turn, or runway-entry decision.

### Hold Short, Runway Entry, and Crossing Discipline

#### Purpose

Develop an unmistakable stop-and-verify habit whenever the airplane approaches a runway boundary.

#### Procedure

1. Recognize the approaching runway environment.
2. Reduce taxi speed.
3. Identify the runway holding-position sign and marking.
4. Stop before the solid hold lines when required.
5. Confirm the assigned runway and clearance/status.
6. Scan the runway environment.
7. Enter or cross only when the operation is understood and safe.
8. Continue across deliberately without unnecessary delay or heads-down tasks.
9. Reestablish the taxi route after clearing the runway.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate a complete hold-short sequence and verbalize each decision.

#### Learner Practice

Perform each runway approach as a deliberate event rather than another taxiway intersection.

#### Common Errors

- Approaching the runway too fast.
- Looking for clearance before first recognizing the boundary.
- Crossing because another airplane crossed.
- Assuming a familiar route includes permission to cross.
- Failing to scan the runway environment.
- Becoming heads-down during the crossing.

#### Completion Standard

The learner recognizes the runway boundary early, stops when required, verifies the operation, and does not infer permission.

### Stop, Reorient, and Clarify

#### Purpose

Make uncertainty trigger a safe action rather than continued movement.

#### Procedure

1. Stop in a safe position.
2. Maintain brakes and aircraft control.
3. Identify the last known position.
4. Compare outside signs/markings with the diagram.
5. Restate the route or clearance.
6. Request clarification or progressive taxi if needed.
7. Resume only after the position and next action are understood.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Deliberately create one low-risk uncertainty scenario and model the response.

#### Learner Practice

Require at least one stop-and-reorient exercise.

#### Common Errors

- Continuing slowly instead of stopping.
- Trying to solve the route while still moving.
- Guessing the current taxiway.
- Being reluctant to request assistance.
- Resuming before the route is fully understood.

#### Completion Standard

The learner treats uncertainty as a reason to stop and can reestablish position and routing before continuing.

## Scenario Application

### Scenario 1 - Changed Taxi Clearance

**Situation:**

The expected taxi route is briefed before start. Ground then issues a different route that includes a runway hold-short instruction.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What must be read back?
- Where is the new route on the diagram?
- What changed from the original brief?
- Where are the runway crossings and holding points?
- What if part of the route cannot be located?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

The learner reads back the important instruction, traces the new route, identifies restrictions, and asks for clarification rather than following the expected route.

**Expected Performance:** Developing, with instructor prompting as needed.

### Scenario 2 - Position Uncertain

**Situation:**

After a turn, the learner is no longer certain the airplane is on the planned taxiway.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What is the safest immediate airplane action?
- Which outside cues should be used?
- How should the diagram be reoriented?
- When should the radio be used?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

The learner stops, maintains control, determines the actual position, and resumes only when the route is understood.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 3 - Runway-Incursion Threat

**Situation:**

The airplane approaches a runway holding position while a transmission is partially blocked.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What visual cues identify the runway boundary?
- Is the airplane authorized to enter or cross?
- What should the learner say if the instruction was not fully understood?
- What should the airplane do while the uncertainty is resolved?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

The learner stops before the runway boundary, does not infer authorization, and requests clarification.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 4 - Heads-Down Distraction

**Situation:**

While taxiing, the learner begins changing a radio or avionics setting and looks inside longer than expected.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- Should the airplane continue moving?
- What surface information could be missed?
- What task should be completed first?
- When is stopping the better option?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

The learner reduces speed or stops and restores outside situational awareness before continuing.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

## Preflight and Cockpit

### Surface Brief Before Start

#### Procedure

1. Complete the C172S preflight/checklist.
2. Review current airport information.
3. Identify current wind and likely runway.
4. Orient the KDWH airport diagram.
5. Identify the airplane's actual parking position.
6. Brief the likely first turn.
7. Identify runway crossings and holding positions.
8. Review published hot spots.
9. Organize frequencies and cockpit items before movement.
10. Brief: **If unsure, stop.**

#### Common Errors

- Starting before the surface plan is understood.
- Treating a familiar airport as requiring no diagram.
- Failing to identify runway crossings.
- Leaving unnecessary setup tasks for taxi.

#### Instructor Demonstration / Learner Practice

The learner should perform progressively more of the surface brief while the instructor questions assumptions and verifies that the learner can point to each item on the diagram.

## Engine Start and Taxi

### Engine Start

#### Procedure

Use the C172S checklist.

#### Common Errors

- Rushing because of expected radio or taxi workload.
- Beginning movement before completing essential setup.

### Initial Movement and Brake Check

#### Procedure

1. Clear the area.
2. Begin movement slowly.
3. Verify steering response.
4. Perform the brake check.
5. Establish a conservative taxi pace.

#### Common Errors

- Taxiing before fully clearing the area.
- Delaying the brake check.
- Allowing speed to build before establishing situational awareness.

### Wind-Control Positioning

#### Procedure

1. Identify wind direction.
2. Compare wind with airplane orientation.
3. Position controls appropriately.
4. Reassess after each significant turn.

#### Common Errors

- Rote control positioning.
- Failing to change control position after a turn.
- Focusing on the controls long enough to reduce outside awareness.

## Surface Operations

### Taxi Route Execution

#### Procedure

1. Maintain positive aircraft control.
2. Keep the primary scan outside.
3. Verbalize the current taxiway when practical.
4. Identify the next turn before reaching it.
5. Compare signs and markings to the diagram.
6. Confirm runway crossings and hold-short points.
7. Stop whenever the outside picture does not match the plan.

#### Common Errors

- Following another aircraft instead of independently verifying the route.
- Looking at the diagram too long while moving.
- Missing a sign because the learner is thinking about the radio.
- Continuing after position uncertainty develops.

### Communications

#### Procedure

1. Listen.
2. Make or receive the call.
3. Identify route/runway/restrictions.
4. Read back or self-announce appropriately.
5. Compare the communication with the diagram and outside environment.
6. Clarify anything uncertain.

#### Common Errors

- Repeating words without understanding.
- Failing to hear a changed route.
- Accepting a blocked transmission.
- Transmitting while another aircraft is already speaking.

### Runway Incursion Avoidance

#### Procedure

1. Identify an approaching runway early.
2. Reduce taxi speed.
3. Identify the red mandatory sign and runway holding-position marking.
4. Stop before the boundary when required.
5. Confirm runway/clearance status.
6. Scan the runway environment.
7. Enter or cross only when the operation is understood and safe.
8. After crossing, reestablish the taxi route immediately.

#### Common Errors

- Approaching the runway too fast.
- Crossing because the route is normally used.
- Assuming another aircraft's movement applies to your aircraft.
- Failing to verify the runway designation.
- Heads-down activity during a runway crossing.
- Failing to reorient after clearing the runway.

### Stop-and-Clarify Exercise

#### Procedure

At least once during the lesson, create or use a realistic low-risk point of uncertainty.

The learner should:

1. Stop.
2. Maintain control.
3. Identify the last known position.
4. Use signs, markings, and the diagram.
5. Communicate if necessary.
6. Resume only after the route is confirmed.

#### Common Errors

- Continuing slowly rather than stopping.
- Guessing.
- Resuming before the actual position is confirmed.

1. Complete the applicable C172S checklist.

3. Review wind.

6. Transition into the flight portion only when the learner is ready.

## Air Work Reinforcement

This lesson does not introduce a new airborne maneuver.

Repeat the **Lesson 1 four fundamentals** so the learner begins transferring basic aircraft-control skills while handling the additional workload of airport information and communications.

### Outside Reference / Lindbergh Reference Reinforcement

#### Procedure

1. Establish stable flight.
2. Identify the normal forward horizon reference.
3. Identify useful side/forward-side visual references.
4. Make a small pitch or bank change.
5. Observe the airplane-to-horizon relationship.
6. Return to the desired attitude.
7. Resume a normal outside scan.

#### Common Errors

- Fixating on one visual reference.
- Looking inside for confirmation before establishing the outside attitude.
- Allowing the reference exercise to replace the normal traffic scan.

#### Instructor Demonstration / Learner Practice

Require the learner to describe the outside picture before referring to instruments.

### Straight-and-Level Flight

#### Procedure

1. Select an outside reference.
2. Set the desired pitch attitude.
3. Level the wings.
4. Allow the airplane to stabilize.
5. Confirm performance.
6. Trim to relieve control pressure.
7. Maintain an outside-dominant scan.

#### Common Errors

Emphasize recurrence of Lesson 1 errors:

- Chasing instruments instead of maintaining the natural-horizon picture.
- Overcontrolling.
- Flying with continuous unnecessary control pressure instead of trimming.
- Allowing a wing to remain low.
- Inadequate outside traffic scan.

#### Instructor Demonstration / Learner Practice

Reduce prompting from Lesson 1. Ask the learner to identify and correct deviations using outside references first.

### Level Turns

#### Procedure

1. Clear the direction of turn.
2. Select the desired bank.
3. Roll smoothly with coordinated aileron and rudder.
4. Maintain pitch attitude.
5. Maintain the desired bank.
6. Lead the rollout.
7. Return to wings level and neutralize controls.

#### Common Errors

- Failing to clear.
- Altitude gain or loss.
- Poor coordination.
- Inconsistent bank.
- Excessive rudder.
- Fixating on instruments rather than the outside picture.

#### Instructor Demonstration / Learner Practice

Use shallow-to-moderate turns appropriate to learner proficiency and require coordinated rollout to an outside reference.

### Climbs

#### Procedure

1. Clear the area.
2. Apply appropriate power.
3. Establish climb attitude.
4. Coordinate with rudder.
5. Allow airspeed to stabilize.
6. Trim.
7. Maintain outside scan.
8. Lead the level-off.

#### Common Errors

- Chasing airspeed instead of holding climb attitude.
- Inadequate right rudder.
- Excessive pitch.
- One wing low.
- Poor coordination.
- Abrupt level-off control input.

#### Instructor Demonstration / Learner Practice

Ask the learner to establish the climb from outside attitude and then confirm with instruments.

### Descents

#### Procedure

1. Clear the area.
2. Adjust power as appropriate.
3. Establish descent attitude.
4. Allow airspeed to stabilize.
5. Coordinate.
6. Trim.
7. Maintain outside scan.
8. Lead the level-off.

#### Common Errors

- Chasing airspeed.
- Failing to establish the outside descent picture before instrument confirmation.
- Poor coordination.
- Inadequate pitch control during level-off.
- Fixation on instruments.

#### Instructor Demonstration / Learner Practice

Use the descent as preparation for returning to the airport while reinforcing outside-reference attitude control.

### Trim and Coordination

#### Procedure

Use:

**CONTROL → HOLD → TRIM**

1. Fly the desired attitude with the primary controls.
2. Hold the required pressure.
3. Trim to relieve the pressure.
4. Verify that the desired attitude/performance remains.

#### Common Errors

- Trying to fly the airplane with trim.
- Trimming before establishing the desired attitude.
- Using rudder alone for directional corrections.
- Accepting a slip or skid without correction.

#### Instructor Demonstration / Learner Practice

By the end of the flight, the learner should require less prompting to trim and coordinate than during Lesson 1.

### Approach to the Airport Environment

During this lesson, emphasize only the supporting awareness needed to return safely:

- identify the airport,
- identify the runway environment,
- monitor or make appropriate communications,

### After Landing / Runway Exit

#### Procedure

1. Maintain aircraft control.
2. Exit the runway when appropriate.
3. Cross the runway holding-position marking completely.
4. Stop or slow as needed before heads-down tasks.
5. Complete the applicable after-landing checklist when clear.
6. Reorient on the airport diagram.
7. Confirm the route to parking.
8. Resume taxi only after the route is understood.

#### Common Errors

- Beginning checklist work before fully clearing the runway.
- Turning onto the expected taxiway without confirming it.
- Losing positional awareness immediately after runway exit.
- Taxiing too quickly while reorganizing the cockpit.

Use the C172S checklist.

Report any discrepancy identified during the lesson.
