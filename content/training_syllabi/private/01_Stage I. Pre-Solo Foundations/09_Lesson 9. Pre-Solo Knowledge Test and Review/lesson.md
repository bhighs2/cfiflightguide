# Lesson 9. Pre-Solo Knowledge Test and Review

**Lesson:** 9  
**Stage:** Stage I — Pre-Solo Foundations  
**Type:** GROUND · EVALUATION  
**Target Proficiency:** Developing — ready for the pre-solo flight check

## Objective

Administer the pre-solo aeronautical knowledge test required by 14 CFR § 61.87(b), evaluate the learner's knowledge of the applicable regulations, KDWH airspace and procedures, and the C172S's flight characteristics and operational limitations, and review every missed or weak answer to full understanding. On satisfactory completion, sign the pre-solo aeronautical knowledge endorsement (AC 61-65K A.3).

## Completion Standards

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

- Complete the pre-solo knowledge test covering applicable Parts 61 and 91, KDWH airspace and procedures, and C172S characteristics and limitations.
- Demonstrate satisfactory aeronautical knowledge to the instructor's standard.
- Correct every incorrect answer to full understanding before any solo authorization.
- Explain the student-pilot limitations and the privileges and boundaries of solo flight.
- Recite the C172S V-speeds, limitations, and key emergency procedures.
- State KDWH airspace classification, tower/CTAF procedures, light-gun signals, and local operating procedures.
- Explain the weather minimums, go-around discipline, and collision- and wake-turbulence-avoidance that bear on a safe solo.

## Why This Matters

Solo flight is the point where the instructor steps out of the airplane. The knowledge test is the regulatory and practical gate that confirms the learner understands the rules, the airplane, and the airport well enough to be alone in it. § 61.87(b) is specific: the test must address the applicable regulations, the airspace rules and procedures for the airport where the solo will be flown, and the flight characteristics and operational limitations of the make and model — and the instructor must review every incorrect answer before authorizing solo.

This is not a formality. The questions on this test are the situations a solo pilot will face without anyone in the right seat. A gap found and fixed here, on the ground, is a gap that does not appear at 1,000 feet alone.

## Risk Management

### Knowledge Gaps Assumed Closed

#### Hazard

The learner passes the test but holds an unexamined misunderstanding that surfaces alone.

#### Consequence

A solo pilot acting on a wrong assumption about a rule, a limitation, or a procedure.

#### Recognition

An answer given correctly on the test but explained incorrectly when asked "why."

#### Mitigation

Review beyond the letter of the answer — ask "why" for every item, correct or not. The test measures recall; the review measures understanding.

### Test Anxiety Masking Readiness

#### Hazard

Test pressure causes a prepared learner to underperform, hiding real readiness.

#### Consequence

An unnecessary delay in solo, or a learner pushed to re-test before a genuine gap is addressed.

#### Recognition

Blank answers on material the learner has demonstrated in flight or discussion.

#### Mitigation

Distinguish a knowledge gap from test anxiety. Re-ask orally. Confirm understanding, not just the written answer.

### Rushing to Solo

#### Hazard

Schedule pressure or eagerness pushes the endorsement before knowledge is genuinely satisfactory.

#### Consequence

A solo pilot who is not ready, with the attendant safety and regulatory exposure.

#### Recognition

A sense of "just sign it" when incorrect answers remain unexplained.

#### Mitigation

The endorsement is signed only when every gap is closed. If the review runs long, the flight check moves — the standard does not.

## Administering the Test

### Purpose

Satisfy § 61.87(b) by administering the knowledge test, evaluating the result, and reviewing every incorrect answer before authorizing solo.

### Procedure

1. Set the conditions — open-book or closed-book, and the time — as the instructor's standard requires.
2. Administer the pre-solo knowledge test. Adapt any item written for another airport (e.g., references to another field's SOP or base) to the KDWH equivalent.
3. Grade the test and identify every incorrect or weak answer.
4. Review each missed item to full understanding — the learner explains the correct answer and why it is correct.
5. Confirm no gap remains across regulations, the C172S, and KDWH.
6. On satisfactory completion, sign the AC 61-65K A.3 pre-solo aeronautical knowledge endorsement in the learner's logbook.

### Common Errors

- Accepting a passing score without reviewing incorrect answers to understanding.
- Leaving an airport-specific item unadapted to KDWH, so the wrong procedure is learned.
- Signing the endorsement while gaps remain.
- Treating the test as the FAA Private Pilot knowledge test — it is separate and in addition to it.

### Completion Standard

The learner completes the test, every incorrect answer is reviewed to full understanding, and the instructor is satisfied that the knowledge standard of § 61.87(b) is met. The A.3 endorsement is signed.

## Scenario Application

Use these to probe the judgment behind the knowledge — the "why" a solo pilot needs.

### Scenario 1 — The Radio Quits

**Situation:**

On a solo flight in the pattern at KDWH, your radio fails during tower hours.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What do you do immediately, and what do you watch for?
- What are the light-gun signals you could receive, and what does each mean?
- What does the tower expect you to do?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Squawk 7600, remain outside or exit the Class D as appropriate, watch the tower for light-gun signals, and know the steady/flashing green and red meanings in flight and on the ground. Land when safe and practical.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 2 — Weather Dropping

**Situation:**

Mid-solo in the local area, the ceiling and visibility begin dropping toward the Class D VFR minimums.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What are the Class D minimums, and what is the difference between legal and prudent?
- What is your personal minimums line, and what do you do as the weather approaches it?
- Who is responsible for the decision — you or the instructor who signed you off?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Class D minimums are 3 SM and 1,000 feet. Legal is not the same as prudent. Return and land before minimums are approached; the solo pilot in command makes the call.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 3 — The Passenger Request

**Situation:**

A friend asks to ride along on your first solo, just around the pattern.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- May a student pilot carry a passenger on a solo flight?
- What other student-pilot limitations apply to solo?
- How do you say no?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

No — a student pilot may not carry passengers. Recite the other limitations: no compensation, solo only within the endorsed area and limits, no night or cross-country without specific endorsement. Decline clearly.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 4 — High and Fast on Final, Solo

**Situation:**

Alone in the pattern, you turn final high and fast.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What are your options, and which is the disciplined one?
- What does the go-around protect against?
- Why is there no one to talk you through it now?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Go around. The go-around is always available and is the disciplined answer to an unstabilized approach. Solo means the decision is yours alone — make it early.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.
