# Lesson 7. Emergencies, Engine-Failure Approaches and Slips

**Lesson:** 7  
**Stage:** Stage I — Pre-Solo Foundations  
**Type:** GROUND + FLIGHT  
**Target Proficiency:** Developing

## Objective

Develop the learner's ability to respond to an engine failure by immediately establishing best glide, selecting a suitable field, and flying an accurate approach to it, while managing the emergency checklist without losing control of the airplane. The learner will also learn the forward slip as an energy-management tool for a high or fast approach, understand the difference between a forward slip and a sideslip, and build the calm, aviate-first mindset that an actual emergency demands.

## Completion Standards

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

- State the immediate actions on an engine failure: establish best glide, select the best available field, and run the restart checklist (ABCs).
- Explain why altitude is options, and why the turn back to the runway at low altitude is usually not survivable.
- Recognize abnormal engine indications and respond without denial or delay.
- Fly a simulated engine-failure approach: establish and hold best glide, select a field, plan and fly a ground track to it, and manage the checklist while flying the airplane first.
- Perform a forward slip to a landing, controlling airspeed with elevator and dissipating altitude without gaining speed.
- Distinguish a forward slip from a sideslip, and explain why slipping into the crosswind aids the approach.
- Apply the basic safety concepts of an emergency landing: attitude and sink-rate control, terrain selection, configuration, and approach.
- Explain firm-but-uniform deceleration in poor terrain — spreading structural breakup over a short distance to reduce peak deceleration of the cabin.
- Apply aviate, navigate, communicate as the emergency priority order.

## Why This Matters

An engine failure is the emergency every pilot rehearses and hopes never to use. The outcome is decided in the first seconds: the pilot who immediately establishes best glide and commits to a field within gliding distance has options; the pilot who denies the problem, or who attempts a low-altitude turn back to the runway, often has none. The skills that decide it — an accurate ground track to a field, energy management on final, and the calm to fly the airplane first — are exactly the skills built in Lessons 3, 5, and 6, now applied under pressure.

The forward slip is the partner skill: the tool that lets a pilot who is high and fast on a forced-landing final get down without getting faster. It is uncomfortable, unfamiliar, and — in a real engine-out — can be the difference between reaching the field and landing in the trees short of it.

## Knowledge

### 1. Emergency Landings and the Basic Safety Concepts

An emergency landing is any landing made under greater-than-normal urgency. The basic safety concepts apply regardless of the cause:

- **Attitude and sink-rate control** — keep the airplane under control and touch down at the minimum safe sink rate.
- **Terrain selection** — a controlled landing in poor terrain is usually more survivable than an uncontrolled landing on a good field.
- **Airplane configuration** — configure for the situation; positive control on final has priority over all checklist tasks.
- **Approach** — plan and fly an approach that reaches the selected touchdown area.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=350 | end=353]]

### 2. Terrain Selection, the Approach, and the Impossible Turn

Terrain is chosen from what is within gliding distance: the best available field, road, or open area — not the ideal one. On the approach, positive control has priority; the airplane is configured only as far as control allows.

An engine failure immediately after takeoff is the most demanding case. At low altitude there is **no safe turn back to the runway** in most situations — the turn costs altitude and raises stall risk close to the ground. The correct response is best glide and a landing straight ahead, with only small turns to a field. This is the "impossible turn," and it is why the takeoff brief always includes the plan for a failure on climbout.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=353 | end=356]]

### 3. Awareness — Emergency Descents, Fires, and a Door in Flight

These are ground-knowledge items in this lesson, not flown procedures:

- **Emergency descent** — a rapid, controlled descent for an uncontrollable fire or a sudden loss of pressurization.
- **Engine fire** — shut off fuel and air to the engine and land immediately.
- **Electrical fire** — master off, individual electrical switches off, then isolate the source.
- **Door opening in flight** — fly the airplane; the aerodynamic effect is usually minimal, and the distraction is the real hazard.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=357 | end=366]]

### 4. The Simulated Engine-Failure Approach

The core maneuver of the lesson. On a simulated engine failure:

1. **Establish best glide** immediately — the C172S best-glide speed from the POH.
2. **Select the best field** within gliding distance and commit to it.
3. **Run the restart checklist** only once the airplane is flying and the field is chosen.
4. **Fly the ground track** to the field and plan the approach.
5. **Configure** as the approach allows, keeping positive control.
6. **Touch down under control** at minimum speed.

The restart attempt never comes before flying the airplane and choosing the field.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/POH-Cessna-172S.pdf | start=55 | end=58]]

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=353 | end=356]]

### 5. Intentional Slips — Forward Slip vs Sideslip

A slip is a cross-controlled condition used to dissipate altitude without increasing airspeed, or to correct for a crosswind. There are two types, and they are **not** the same:

- **Forward slip** — used to lose altitude without gaining speed. The flightpath continues toward the landing point while the nose is yawed off the direction of travel. Bank controls the sink rate; elevator controls airspeed; power is normally at idle.
- **Sideslip** — used to align the airplane with a crosswind runway. The longitudinal axis stays aligned with the flightpath while the airplane slips sideways toward the low wing.

In a slip, the airplane flies sideways through the air, drag increases sharply, and the airplane can descend steeply without gaining speed. Some airspeed indicators read erroneously in a slip — recognize a properly flown slip by attitude, sound, and feel.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=183 | end=186]]

### 6. The Forward Slip to a Landing

The forward slip is the energy-management tool for a high or fast final — especially a forced-landing approach. The pilot plans the descent so a forward slip may be used on final; flaps usually remain retracted.

1. Establish the approach with power at idle.
2. Lower one wing with aileron and apply sufficient opposite rudder to yaw the nose off the flightpath while keeping the flightpath toward the aiming point.
3. Control airspeed with elevator; control sink rate with bank.
4. Slip **into** the wind when a crosswind is present — it makes holding the flightpath easier.
5. At the appropriate time, smoothly level the wings and release rudder pressure while readjusting to the normal glide attitude, and transition to a normal landing.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=183 | end=186]]

## Risk Management

### Denial and Delayed Response

#### Hazard

The pilot refuses to accept an actual engine problem, or delays the response hoping it will resolve.

#### Consequence

Altitude and options are spent on denial instead of glide; the field is no longer reachable by the time the pilot commits.

#### Recognition

Abnormal engine indications — roughness, power loss, unusual gauge readings — met with "wait and see" instead of action.

#### Mitigation

Treat abnormal indications as real until proven otherwise. Establish best glide and identify a field first, then troubleshoot. Altitude is options.

### Failure to Establish Best Glide

#### Hazard

The pilot does not establish and hold the best-glide speed immediately after the failure.

#### Consequence

Reduced glide range and time; the selected field may be out of reach; stall risk rises if the pilot stretches the glide.

#### Recognition

Airspeed decaying below or climbing above best glide; the airplane not stabilized in the glide.

#### Mitigation

Best glide first, every time. Trim for it. It is the single action that buys the most time and distance.

### Poor Field Selection and Stretching the Glide

#### Hazard

The pilot selects an unreachable or unsuitable field, or tries to stretch a glide by raising the nose.

#### Consequence

Landing short in hostile terrain, or a stall/spin on the final approach — the accident Lessons 5 and 6 exist to prevent.

#### Recognition

The field rising in the windshield (below the glide path), airspeed decaying as the pilot pulls, or a field that was never within gliding distance.

#### Mitigation

Apply Lesson 6 field selection and ground-track skill. Pick the best field within reach and commit. Respect the Lesson 5 stall margin — never stretch a glide; a controlled landing short is more survivable than an uncontrolled stall.

### Forward Slip Mishandled

#### Hazard

A forward slip flown with poor airspeed control, slipped into the wrong direction relative to a crosswind, or transitioned out of too late.

#### Consequence

Excess airspeed, a balloon, loss of glidepath control, or a hard/side-loaded landing.

#### Recognition

Airspeed rising in the slip, the flightpath drifting off the aiming point, or the slip still in at the flare.

#### Mitigation

Airspeed with elevator, sink with bank, slip into the wind, and transition out smoothly at the appropriate point. Discontinue the slip by leveling the wings and releasing rudder together while readjusting pitch.

### Task Saturation in the Emergency

#### Hazard

The pilot fixates on the restart checklist or the radio and stops flying the airplane.

#### Consequence

Loss of control or a poorly flown approach while the checklist is being read.

#### Recognition

Altitude or airspeed wandering while the pilot is heads-down; the approach geometry deteriorating.

#### Mitigation

Aviate, navigate, communicate — in that order. Fly the airplane and secure the field first; the checklist and the radio wait their turn.

## Skills and Procedures

### Simulated Engine-Failure Approach and Landing

#### Purpose

Respond to an engine failure by establishing best glide, selecting a field, and flying an accurate approach to it while managing the emergency checklist without losing control.

#### Procedure

1. On the simulated failure, **establish best glide** and trim.
2. **Select the best field** within gliding distance and commit to it.
3. **Run the restart checklist** only once the airplane is flying and the field is chosen.
4. **Fly the ground track** to the field and plan the approach (high key, low key, base, final as briefed).
5. **Configure** as the approach allows, keeping positive control.
6. **Manage energy** on final — use a forward slip if high.
7. **Touch down under control** at minimum speed, or go around / climb out when the instructor terminates the maneuver at a safe altitude.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate one simulated engine failure from a known altitude, verbalizing best glide, field selection, the checklist, and the approach.

#### Learner Practice

The learner flies repeated simulated engine failures from varying altitudes and positions, holding best glide, selecting fields, and managing the checklist while flying the airplane first.

#### Common Errors

- Delayed response or denial of the simulated failure.
- Failure to establish or hold best glide.
- Poor field selection — unreachable or unsuitable.
- Running the restart checklist before the airplane is flying and the field is chosen.
- Stretching the glide by raising the nose; approaching the stall.
- Poor approach geometry — too high, too low, or off the field.
- Task saturation — losing control while reading the checklist.
- Failure to terminate into a safe climb when directed.

#### Completion Standard

The learner responds promptly, establishes best glide, selects a suitable field, flies a developing approach to it, and manages the checklist without losing control — with coaching.

### Forward Slip to a Landing

#### Purpose

Dissipate altitude without increasing airspeed on a high or fast final, using a cross-controlled forward slip, then transition to a normal landing.

#### Procedure

1. Plan the approach so a forward slip may be used on final; flaps usually retracted; power idle.
2. Lower one wing with aileron and apply sufficient opposite rudder to yaw the nose off the flightpath while keeping the flightpath toward the aiming point.
3. Control airspeed with elevator and sink rate with bank.
4. Slip into the wind when a crosswind is present.
5. At the appropriate point, smoothly level the wings and release rudder pressure while readjusting to the normal glide attitude.
6. Transition to a normal landing — round out and touch down in a landing attitude.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate one forward slip to a landing, verbalizing the entry, airspeed control, the into-the-wind direction, and the smooth transition out.

#### Learner Practice

The learner performs forward slips to a landing with coaching on airspeed control, direction, and the transition.

#### Common Errors

Common errors with forward slips to a landing are:

1. Incorrect pitch adjustments that result in poor airspeed control.
2. Reacting to erroneous airspeed indications.
3. Using excess power while trying to lose altitude.
4. A slip in the same direction as any crosswind.
5. Poor glidepath control.
6. Late transition to a sideslip during landing with crosswinds.
7. Landing without the longitudinal axis parallel to runway.
8. Landing off the centerline.

#### Completion Standard

The learner performs a developing forward slip to a landing — controlling airspeed with elevator, sinking with bank, slipping into the wind, and transitioning smoothly to a normal landing — with coaching.

### Emergency Awareness Review

#### Purpose

Ground-knowledge awareness of the emergency descent, engine and electrical fires, and a door opening in flight. Not flown in this lesson.

#### Procedure

1. State when an emergency descent is used and its purpose.
2. State the immediate actions for an engine fire and an electrical fire.
3. State the response to a door opening in flight — fly the airplane first.

#### Common Errors

- Treating awareness items as flown procedures for this lesson.
- Fixating on a door or a fire indication and losing control of the airplane.
- Reaching for the checklist before the airplane is under control.

#### Completion Standard

The learner can state the purpose and immediate actions for each awareness item and confirms they are not flown in this lesson.

## Scenario Application

### Scenario 1 — Roughness on Downwind

**Situation:**

On downwind at KDWH, the engine begins running rough. Power is decreasing but the engine is still producing some thrust.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- Is this a forced landing or a precautionary landing, and what is the difference?
- Do you troubleshoot on downwind or land now?
- What is the priority order?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Recognize a partial-power situation as a precautionary landing — land now while power is available, rather than troubleshooting until the engine quits at a bad point. Aviate, navigate, communicate.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 2 — Engine Failure on Climbout

**Situation:**

At 400 feet AGL on climbout, the engine fails completely.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What is the only realistic option at this altitude?
- Why is the turn back to the runway not survivable here?
- What does your takeoff brief say about this?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Lower the nose, establish best glide, and land straight ahead with only small turns to a field. Never attempt the low-altitude turn back. Reference the impossible-turn video.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 3 — High and Fast on the Forced Landing

**Situation:**

You have selected your field and flown a good ground track, but you arrive on final high and fast.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- How do you use a forward slip to manage this energy?
- Which wing do you lower if there is a crosswind, and why?
- When do you transition out of the slip?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Establish a forward slip into the wind, control airspeed with elevator and sink with bank, and transition smoothly to a normal landing at the appropriate point.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 4 — Simulated Failure Over the Practice Area

**Situation:**

At 3,000 feet over the practice area, the instructor reduces power to idle: "Simulated engine failure."

**Discuss / Decide:**

- Talk through the ABCs in order.
- Where is your field, and what is your ground track to it?
- When do you run the restart checklist?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Best glide first, best field, then checklist — while flying the airplane. Fly the ground track to the field and manage the approach.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

# Flight Card

## Flight Setup

### Lesson Target

**Target Proficiency: Developing**

Primary lesson target:

**Simulated engine-failure approach and landing, and the forward slip to a landing.**

Secondary airborne target:

**Calm, control-first emergency response — best glide, best field, then checklist — while flying an accurate ground track to the field.**

Boundary:

**Forward slip to a landing only. No sideslip/wing-low crosswind landing (Lesson 8), no emergency descent, fires, or door flown — awareness only.**

### Scope and Flexibility

This is an instructional plan, not a requirement to complete every repetition.

If wind, traffic, terrain suitability, or learner workload require it:

- reduce the number of simulated failures,
- terminate each approach into a safe climb at the briefed altitude rather than to touchdown,
- favor a single well-flown approach and a single well-flown slip over many rushed ones,
- and re-brief the ABCs and best glide before continuing.

Do not expand into sideslip landings, emergency descents, or actual power-off landings to a field.

## Preflight and Cockpit

### Emergency Briefing Before Start

#### Procedure

1. Complete the C172S preflight/checklist.
2. Review the C172S best-glide speed from POH Section 3 — do not guess.
3. Brief the ABCs and the aviate-navigate-communicate priority.
4. Brief the impossible turn and the takeoff-failure plan for today's runway.
5. Brief the simulated-failure termination: instructor calls the recovery to a climb at a safe altitude.
6. Brief the forward slip: entry, airspeed with elevator, sink with bank, into the wind, smooth transition out.

#### Common Errors

- Best-glide speed guessed rather than looked up.
- No takeoff-failure plan briefed.
- Unclear on how the simulated failure will be terminated.

## Engine Start, Taxi, and Takeoff

Apply Lessons 2 and 3. Short version only: checklist, brake check, wind control, radio discipline, hold short and verify, normal takeoff and climb at POH speeds.

Include the takeoff-failure brief for the runway in use.

## En Route to Practice Area

#### Procedure

1. Climb to the working altitude.
2. Level, trim, and set up over an area with suitable forced-landing fields.
3. Confirm wind and the available fields below.

#### Common Errors

- Practicing over terrain with no suitable fields.
- No wind read before the simulated failure.

---

## Simulated Engine-Failure Approach

#### Procedure

1. Instructor reduces power: "Simulated engine failure."
2. Establish best glide and trim.
3. Select the best field and commit.
4. Run the restart checklist once flying and field chosen.
5. Fly the ground track and plan the approach.
6. Configure as control allows; manage energy with a forward slip if high.
7. Terminate into a safe climb when the instructor calls it, or as briefed.

#### Common Errors

- Denial or delay on the simulated failure.
- Not holding best glide.
- Checklist before airplane and field.
- Stretching the glide toward the stall.
- Poor approach geometry; not terminating into a climb when directed.

---

## Forward Slip to a Landing

#### Procedure

1. Plan the approach so a forward slip may be used on final; flaps retracted; power idle.
2. Lower one wing with aileron; opposite rudder to yaw the nose off while holding the flightpath to the aiming point.
3. Airspeed with elevator; sink with bank; slip into the wind.
4. Smoothly level the wings and release rudder at the appropriate point; readjust to the glide attitude.
5. Round out and land in a landing attitude.

#### Common Errors

Common errors with forward slips to a landing are:

1. Incorrect pitch adjustments that result in poor airspeed control.
2. Reacting to erroneous airspeed indications.
3. Using excess power while trying to lose altitude.
4. A slip in the same direction as any crosswind.
5. Poor glidepath control.
6. Late transition to a sideslip during landing with crosswinds.
7. Landing without the longitudinal axis parallel to runway.
8. Landing off the centerline.

---

## Return, Shutdown and Securing

#### Procedure

1. Cruise back; rejoin the pattern per Lesson 3 and tower per Lesson 4.
2. Land, exit, fully clear, then checklist, and reorient.
3. Taxi to parking; C172S shutdown, secure, postflight, log discrepancies.

#### Common Errors

- Heads-down before clear of the runway.
- Losing position after exit.

---

## Flight Completion Criteria

The flight portion is satisfactory for this stage when the learner demonstrates developing ability to:

- respond promptly to the simulated failure and establish best glide,
- select a suitable field and fly an accurate ground track to it,
- manage the restart checklist without losing control of the airplane,
- fly a developing emergency approach and terminate it into a safe climb as briefed,
- perform a forward slip to a landing with proper airspeed control, direction, and transition,
- and apply aviate, navigate, communicate under pressure.

The lesson may be repeated when the engine-failure response, best-glide discipline, field selection, or the forward slip are not yet safe to continue.
