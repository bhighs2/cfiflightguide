# Lesson 5. Aerodynamics, Slow Flight and Stalls

**Lesson:** 5  
**Stage:** Stage I — Pre-Solo Foundations  
**Type:** GROUND + FLIGHT  
**Target Proficiency:** Developing

## Objective

Develop the learner's understanding of the aerodynamics that govern the slow-flight and stall regime — angle of attack, load factor, and the relationship between bank angle and stall speed — and build the ability to maneuver safely at high angles of attack and to recognize and recover from power-off and power-on stalls. The learner will understand that a wing stalls by exceeding its critical angle of attack, not by reaching a particular airspeed, and will practice slow flight and both stall types at a safe altitude with instructor coaching.

## Completion Standards

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

- Explain that a stall is an angle-of-attack event, possible at any airspeed, attitude, or power setting.
- Describe how load factor and bank angle increase stall speed, using the Vg diagram.
- Establish and maneuver in slow flight in the cruise and landing configurations, maintaining altitude and heading with coordinated controls.
- Recognize the first indications of an approaching stall — stall warning, buffet, decayed control effectiveness — and state the correct immediate response.
- Recover from a power-off stall by reducing angle of attack first, then adding power and returning to the desired flightpath with minimum altitude loss.
- Recover from a power-on stall by reducing angle of attack, managing left-turning tendency, and establishing a climb.
- Demonstrate altitude and clearing discipline: clearing turns before maneuvering and recovery completed no lower than 1,500 feet AGL.
- Explain what a spin is, why the C172S is not spun in the normal category, and recite the PARE recovery concept.
- Manage the startle response and fly the airplane first before analyzing or talking.

## Why This Matters

Loss of control in flight (LOC-I) is the leading cause of fatal general aviation accidents, and the most common pathway is an unintended stall — often at low altitude in the traffic pattern, where recovery is not possible. Every slow-landing approach, every go-around, and every steep turn operates closer to the critical angle of attack than cruise flight. A pilot who understands angle of attack, recognizes the earliest stall cues, and responds with the correct recovery sequence has a margin that a pilot who merely memorizes airspeeds does not.

This lesson also reframes two maneuvers the learner has already flown. The normal landing is, aerodynamically, a controlled descent into slow flight. The go-around — especially with nose-up landing trim — is a power-on stall setup if mishandled, which is exactly what the instructor demonstrated in Lesson 3.

## Knowledge

### 1. Angle of Attack — The Real Cause of the Stall

A stall is an aerodynamic condition in which the smooth airflow over the wing separates, causing a loss of lift. It happens when the **angle of attack** — the angle between the wing's chord line and the relative wind — exceeds the **critical angle of attack**.

The critical angle of attack can be exceeded at **any airspeed, any attitude, and any power setting**. Airspeed is a clue, not the cause. This is the single most important idea in the lesson.

[[highlight:
AOA, Not Airspeed |
Because AOA is the cause, the recovery is always the same first step: **reduce the angle of attack.** Everything else — power, configuration, returning to the flightpath — follows that.
]]

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=106 | end=114]]

[[pdf: /knowledge/Pilots_Handbook_of_Aeronautical_Knowledge.pdf | start=98 | end=119]]

### 2. Load Factor and the Vg Diagram

In a bank, the wing must produce more lift to support the airplane, because part of the lift now turns the airplane. That extra lift demand is **load factor**, measured in Gs. A level 60° bank produces 2 G.

More required lift means a higher AOA at any given speed — so the critical AOA is reached at a **higher airspeed**. The stall speed rises with load factor.

<figure class="lesson-reference-figure">
    <img src="/knowledge/Vg_Diagram.png" alt="Vg diagram showing the relationship between airspeed, load factor, and the stall region">
    <figcaption>Vg diagram — the curved stall boundary rises with load factor. The airplane has no single stall speed; it stalls at a higher speed the harder it is loaded or banked.</figcaption>
</figure>

Operational meaning:

- A steep turn close to the ground brings the stall closer even though the airspeed "looks fine."
- An abrupt pull — in a botched recovery or a tight turn — can stall the airplane at an unexpectedly high speed. This is the **accelerated stall**.
- A wing already at the edge in slow flight has very little AOA margin left for maneuvering.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Pilots_Handbook_of_Aeronautical_Knowledge.pdf | start=119 | end=135]]

### 3. Slow Flight

The objective of maneuvering in slow flight is to develop the ability to fly at low airspeeds and high angles of attack — the regime of the final approach and the flare.

Slow flight is characterized by:

- high angle of attack,
- high power to maintain altitude (well below L/Dmax, slower requires more power),
- decayed control effectiveness — the controls feel "mushy,"
- and the need for right rudder to counter left-turning tendency at high power and low speed.

It is practiced in two elements:

1. **Performing** slow flight — establishing and stabilizing the maneuver in the desired configuration.
2. **Maneuvering** in slow flight — maintaining altitude and heading through straight flight and gentle turns.

Slow flight is typically practiced in the landing configuration (flaps extended). Any further increase in AOA, increase in load factor, or reduction in power produces a stall warning. If the stall warning sounds, slightly reduce AOA to silence it.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=114 | end=117]]

### 4. Stall Recognition

Stalls are practiced to two levels:

- **Impending stall** — the AOA has produced a stall warning but has not yet reached the critical AOA. Indications: stall horn, buffet, stick shaker.
- **Full stall** — the critical AOA is exceeded. Indications: an uncommanded nose-down pitch that cannot be readily arrested, possibly with an uncommanded roll.

The cues arrive in a rough order: decaying control effectiveness, stall warning (horn), buffet, then the break. Recognize the **earliest** cue. Do not wait for the nose to drop.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=117 | end=122]]

### 5. Stall Recovery Fundamentals

The recovery sequence is always the same in priority:

1. **Reduce the angle of attack** — relax back-elevator / apply forward elevator until the stall warning stops and the wing is flying again. This is the only action that un-stalls the wing.
2. **Add power** smoothly as AOA is reduced, to minimize altitude loss and accelerate.
3. **Level the wings** with coordinated rudder and aileron — but only after the AOA is reduced.
4. **Return to the desired flightpath** — retrim and reconfigure as appropriate.

Common fatal errors: trying to level the wings or add power **before** reducing AOA, and pulling again too soon into a **secondary stall**.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=117 | end=122]]

### 6. Power-Off Stall — The Approach-to-Landing Stall

This maneuver simulates stalling on final approach.

**Setup:** clearing turns, landing configuration (flaps as directed), power to idle, maintain altitude with increasing back-elevator as speed decays — as in the flare.

**The stall:** as speed bleeds off, recognize the stall warning and buffet; allow the stall to develop to the directed level (impending or full).

**Recovery:** reduce AOA, add power smoothly, level wings, retract flaps incrementally, and establish a climb or the assigned flightpath with minimum altitude loss.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=122 | end=124]]

### 7. Power-On Stall — The Departure / Go-Around Stall

This maneuver simulates stalling on takeoff, in a climb, or on a go-around — the situation behind the Lesson 3 trim-stall demonstration.

**Setup:** clearing turns, slow to lift-off/departure speed, then apply takeoff power while raising the nose to a pitch attitude that will produce a stall.

**The stall:** high pitch, full power, strong left-turning tendency requiring right rudder; recognize the warning and the break.

**Recovery:** promptly reduce AOA (forward elevator), keep the ball centered with rudder, and lower the nose to a climb attitude, establishing a climb at VY.

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=122 | end=124]]

### 8. Awareness — Accelerated, Secondary, Cross-Control Stalls, and Spins

These are taught for **awareness and avoidance only**. None are practiced in this lesson.

- **Accelerated stall:** a stall entered above the normal stall speed because of an abrupt increase in load factor — a hard pull or steep turn. It is why the Vg diagram matters.
- **Secondary stall:** a stall that follows a botched recovery when the pilot pulls again before the wing is fully flying. Recover, let speed build, then climb.
- **Elevator-trim stall:** the pitch-up that follows full power with nose-up (landing) trim — the Lesson 3 demonstration. Anticipate it; apply forward pressure and retrim.
- **Cross-control stall:** a stall entered from a **skidding** turn — classically the overshot base-to-final turn where the pilot adds inside rudder and opposite aileron to avoid banking steeper. The inside wing stalls first and the airplane can roll toward the dead engine side. This is the accident this whole lesson exists to prevent, and it is why we never "help" the turn with rudder.
- **Spin:** an aggravated stall in which one wing is more deeply stalled than the other, producing **autorotation**. A stalled, uncoordinated airplane can spin; a coordinated one generally will not. The C172S is approved for spins only in the utility category and is **not intentionally spun** in normal training. Spin recovery is a **CFI-level** maneuver.

[[mnemonic:
PARE — Spin Recovery |
**P**ower idle · **A**ilerons neutral · **R**udder opposite the rotation · **E**levator forward to break the stall. This is recovery knowledge for awareness — spins are not practiced.
]]

## Risk Management

### Loss of Control in Flight (LOC-I)

#### Hazard

The airplane exceeds its critical angle of attack unintentionally — the leading cause of fatal general aviation accidents, with maneuvering as the most common phase.

#### Consequence

Stall, spin, and loss of control with insufficient altitude to recover.

#### Recognition

Decaying control effectiveness, stall warning, buffet, or an uncommanded nose-down pitch.

#### Mitigation

Recognize the earliest cue and reduce angle of attack immediately. Train the recovery until it is a reflex, not a recollection.

### The Pattern Stall/Spin

#### Hazard

An overshot base-to-final turn, corrected with a skidding, cross-controlled turn at low altitude and low airspeed.

#### Consequence

A cross-control stall at pattern altitude with no room to recover — the classic fatal training accident.

#### Recognition

Nose swinging toward the runway while overshooting, heavy inside rudder, bank held shallow with opposite aileron, speed decaying.

#### Mitigation

Never "help" the turn with rudder. Keep the ball centered. If the approach cannot be salvaged with a coordinated turn, go around. Avoidance — not recovery — is the only reliable defense at this altitude.

### Inadequate Recovery Altitude

#### Hazard

Beginning or continuing a stall maneuver too low, so recovery cannot be completed above a safe floor.

#### Consequence

Ground contact during the recovery or the dive that follows it.

#### Recognition

The altimeter approaching the briefed floor during the maneuver.

#### Mitigation

Brief the recovery floor (terrain + 1,500 feet AGL) before every maneuver. Terminate and climb if the floor is approaching. Recovery is complete no lower than 1,500 feet AGL.

### Failure to Clear the Area

#### Hazard

Maneuvering — especially clearing turns, slow flight, and stalls — into unseen traffic.

#### Consequence

Midair collision during a high-workload, head-down maneuver.

#### Recognition

Other traffic in or near the practice area; a clearing turn that reveals a conflict.

#### Mitigation

Two clearing turns before every maneuver, scanning the area the maneuver will take you into. Maneuver over a designated practice area away from the traffic pattern.

### Startle and Surprise Response

#### Hazard

The first real stall break produces a startle response that freezes or misdirects the pilot.

#### Consequence

No recovery input, or the wrong input — pulling back harder — at the moment correct action matters most.

#### Recognition

The learner freezing, tensing, or making an abrupt, incorrect control input at the break.

#### Mitigation

Brief the startle response in advance. Train the sequence — fly the airplane, reduce AOA, then analyze, then talk. The instructor manages the first exposures and debriefs the response openly.

## Skills and Procedures

### Maneuvering During Slow Flight

#### Purpose

Develop the ability to fly at low airspeeds and high angles of attack — the regime of the final approach and the flare — while maintaining altitude, heading, and coordination.

#### Procedure

1. Clearing turns; establish the practice area and recovery floor.
2. Reduce power gradually and add back-elevator to hold altitude as airspeed decays.
3. As the speed approaches the target slow-flight speed, adjust power to maintain altitude — high AOA, high power.
4. Trim, and use right rudder to stay coordinated.
5. For landing configuration, extend flaps incrementally, anticipating the AOA and trim changes.
6. Maneuver: maintain altitude and heading in straight flight and gentle turns, dividing attention between control and orientation.
7. If the stall warning sounds, slightly reduce AOA to silence it.
8. Recover by adding power, reducing AOA, retracting flaps, and accelerating to cruise.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate the entry and stabilized slow flight in both configurations, verbalizing power, pitch, trim, and right-rudder coordination.

#### Learner Practice

The learner establishes and maneuvers in slow flight in cruise and landing configurations, with coaching on power management and coordination.

#### Common Errors

Common errors in the performance of slow flight are:

- Failure to adequately clear the area.
- Inadequate back-elevator pressure as power is reduced, resulting in altitude loss.
- Excessive back-elevator pressure as power is reduced, resulting in a climb followed by rapid reduction in airspeed.
- Insufficient right rudder to compensate for left yaw.
- Fixation on the flight instruments.
- Failure to anticipate changes in AOA as flaps are extended or retracted.
- Inadequate power management.
- Inability to adequately divide attention between airplane control and orientation.
- Failure to properly trim the airplane.
- Failure to respond to a stall warning.

#### Completion Standard

The learner establishes and maneuvers in slow flight in both configurations, holds altitude and heading, stays coordinated, and respects the stall warning — with coaching.

### Power-Off Stall

#### Purpose

Recognize and recover from a stall in the approach-to-landing configuration — the power-off stall.

#### Procedure

1. Clearing turns; recovery floor briefed.
2. Establish the landing configuration; reduce power to idle.
3. Hold altitude with increasing back-elevator as the speed decays toward the stall — the flare picture.
4. Recognize the stall warning and buffet; allow the stall to develop to the directed level (impending or full).
5. Recover: reduce AOA first, add power smoothly, level the wings, retract flaps incrementally, and climb with minimum altitude loss.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate one power-off stall, verbalizing the entry, the first cues, and the reduce-AOA-first recovery.

#### Learner Practice

The learner performs power-off stalls to the impending and full levels, recovering with reduce-AOA-first technique and minimum altitude loss.

#### Common Errors

Common errors in the performance of intentional stalls are:

1. Failure to adequately clear the area.
2. Over-reliance on the airspeed indicator and slip-skid indicator while excluding other cues after recovery.
3. Inadvertent accelerated stall by pulling too fast on the controls during a power-off or power-on stall entry.
4. Inability to recognize an impending stall condition.
5. Failure to take timely action to prevent a full stall during the conduct of impending stalls.
6. Failure to maintain a constant bank angle during turning stalls.
7. Failure to maintain proper coordination with the rudder throughout the stall and recovery.
8. Recovering before reaching the critical AOA when practicing the full stall maneuver.
9. Not disconnecting the wing leveler or autopilot, if equipped, prior to reducing AOA.
10. Recovery is attempted without recognizing the importance of pitch control and AOA.
11. Not maintaining a nose down control input until the stall warning is eliminated.
12. Pilot attempts to level the wings before reducing AOA.
13. Pilot attempts to recover with power before reducing AOA.
14. Failure to roll wings level after AOA reduction and stall warning is eliminated.
15. Inadvertent secondary stall during recovery.
16. Excessive forward-elevator pressure during recovery resulting in low or negative G load.
17. Excessive airspeed buildup during recovery.
18. Losing situational awareness and failing to return to desired flightpath or follow ATC instructions.

#### Completion Standard

The learner recognizes the earliest power-off stall cues and recovers with reduce-AOA-first technique, minimum altitude loss, and coordination — with coaching.

### Power-On Stall

#### Purpose

Recognize and recover from a stall in the departure or go-around configuration — the power-on stall — the situation behind the Lesson 3 trim-stall demonstration.

#### Procedure

1. Clearing turns; recovery floor briefed.
2. Slow to lift-off/departure speed, then apply takeoff power while raising the nose to a pitch attitude that will produce the stall.
3. Hold the pitch attitude and maintain coordination with right rudder as the stall approaches.
4. Recognize the warning and the break.
5. Recover: promptly reduce AOA with forward elevator, keep the ball centered, lower the nose, and establish a climb at VY.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate one power-on stall, emphasizing the strong left-turning tendency at full power and the prompt forward-elevator recovery.

#### Learner Practice

The learner performs power-on stalls, controlling left-turning tendency and recovering to a VY climb.

#### Common Errors

Common errors in the performance of intentional stalls are:

1. Failure to adequately clear the area.
2. Over-reliance on the airspeed indicator and slip-skid indicator while excluding other cues after recovery.
3. Inadvertent accelerated stall by pulling too fast on the controls during a power-off or power-on stall entry.
4. Inability to recognize an impending stall condition.
5. Failure to take timely action to prevent a full stall during the conduct of impending stalls.
6. Failure to maintain a constant bank angle during turning stalls.
7. Failure to maintain proper coordination with the rudder throughout the stall and recovery.
8. Recovering before reaching the critical AOA when practicing the full stall maneuver.
9. Not disconnecting the wing leveler or autopilot, if equipped, prior to reducing AOA.
10. Recovery is attempted without recognizing the importance of pitch control and AOA.
11. Not maintaining a nose down control input until the stall warning is eliminated.
12. Pilot attempts to level the wings before reducing AOA.
13. Pilot attempts to recover with power before reducing AOA.
14. Failure to roll wings level after AOA reduction and stall warning is eliminated.
15. Inadvertent secondary stall during recovery.
16. Excessive forward-elevator pressure during recovery resulting in low or negative G load.
17. Excessive airspeed buildup during recovery.
18. Losing situational awareness and failing to return to desired flightpath or follow ATC instructions.

#### Completion Standard

The learner recognizes the power-on stall, controls left-turning tendency, and recovers to a coordinated climb with reduce-AOA-first technique — with coaching.

### Spin and Cross-Control Awareness

#### Purpose

Awareness and avoidance only: understand the spin and the cross-control stall so they are never entered. Neither is practiced.

#### Procedure

1. Define a spin as an aggravated stall with autorotation — one wing more deeply stalled than the other.
2. State that the C172S is approved for spins only in the utility category and is not intentionally spun in normal training.
3. Recite the PARE recovery: Power idle, Ailerons neutral, Rudder opposite, Elevator forward.
4. Walk through the cross-control stall — the skidded base-to-final turn — and state why it is avoided, not recovered.

#### Common Errors

- Confusing a stall with a spin.
- Attempting spin recovery with aileron first instead of reducing AOA.
- Believing the cross-controlled turn "saves" an overshot final.
- Treating spin recovery as a private-pilot maneuver rather than awareness knowledge.

#### Completion Standard

The learner can explain the spin and cross-control hazards, recite PARE, and state that neither is practiced in this lesson.

## Scenario Application

### Scenario 1 — The Overshot Final

**Situation:**

On the turn from base to final you overshoot the centerline. You are low and slow, and the instinct is to "help" the turn with inside rudder while holding opposite aileron to keep the bank shallow.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What is happening aerodynamically in a skidding, cross-controlled turn at low airspeed?
- Which wing stalls first, and why is there no recovery here?
- What is the correct response?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Recognize the cross-control stall setup. Keep the ball centered, accept a coordinated turn, and if the approach cannot be salvaged, go around — the Lesson 3 answer.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 2 — The Go-Around Pull

**Situation:**

You decide to go around and add full power. With nose-up landing trim, the nose pitches up strongly and you pull harder to hold the climb.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What stall situation does this recreate?
- Why does full power plus nose-up trim plus back pressure drive AOA toward the critical angle?
- What is the recovery sequence?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Connect this to the Lesson 3 trim-stall demonstration. Apply the power-on recovery — forward pressure to reduce AOA, right rudder for coordination, then a proper climb attitude.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 3 — The Buffet in the Turn

**Situation:**

In slow flight you begin a gentle turn and feel the stall horn chirp and a light buffet.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What did the turn add, and how did it change the stall speed?
- What happens if you pull harder?
- What is the correct action?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Recognize that the turn added load factor and raised stall speed — near an accelerated stall. Slightly reduce AOA to silence the warning and add power to maintain altitude.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 4 — Planning the Recovery Floor

**Situation:**

You brief slow flight over the practice area at 3,000 feet MSL. The terrain below is 1,200 feet MSL.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- Where is the recovery floor in feet MSL?
- When do you terminate the maneuver?
- Is the floor a target or a hard limit?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Compute the floor — 1,500 feet AGL over 1,200 feet MSL terrain is 2,700 feet MSL — and treat it as a hard limit. Terminate and climb if the maneuver drags the airplane toward it.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

## Review and Evaluation

### Review Questions

1. What causes a stall, and at what airspeeds can it occur?
2. What does the Vg diagram show about bank angle and stall speed?
3. What is the first recovery action in any stall, and why does it come before power?
4. How do the power-off and power-on stalls differ in the situation each simulates?
5. Why is a skidding base-to-final turn so dangerous?
6. What is a spin, and what is the PARE recovery sequence?
7. What is the recovery floor, and why are clearing turns required before every maneuver?

[[answer:
1. Exceeding the wing's critical angle of attack. Because AOA — not airspeed — is the cause, a stall can occur at any airspeed, any attitude, and any power setting.
2. Stall speed rises with load factor. A 60°-bank level turn (2 G) raises stall speed by roughly 40 percent.
3. Reduce the angle of attack. The stall is an excess-AOA condition; power alone does not un-stall the wing, and adding power before reducing AOA can deepen the stall and cost altitude.
4. Power-off simulates the approach-to-landing stall; power-on simulates the departure or go-around stall.
5. It is a cross-control stall — the inside wing stalls first, at pattern altitude, with no room to recover.
6. A spin is an aggravated, autorotating stall. PARE: Power idle, Ailerons neutral, Rudder opposite the rotation, Elevator forward. Awareness only — not practiced.
7. Recovery is complete no lower than 1,500 feet AGL; clearing turns protect against maneuvering into unseen traffic.
]]

- Explains the stall as an angle-of-attack event, not an airspeed event.
- Uses the Vg diagram to connect bank angle, load factor, and stall speed.
- Performs clearing turns and respects the 1,500-foot-AGL recovery floor.
- Establishes and maneuvers in slow flight in both configurations.
- Recognizes the earliest stall cues — horn and buffet — before the break.
- Recovers reduce-AOA-first on both stall types, without leveling wings or adding power first.
- Maintains coordination, especially against left-turning tendency at full power.
- Verbalizes the spin-avoidance and PARE recovery concepts.
- Does not treat spin or cross-control recovery as maneuvers to be practiced.

## Flight Setup

### Lesson Target

**Target Proficiency: Developing**

**Slow flight in the cruise and landing configurations, and recognition and recovery from power-off and power-on stalls, at a safe altitude.**

Secondary airborne target:

**Keep Lesson 1 control quality — outside reference, coordination, trim — while maneuvering at high angle of attack.**

### Scope and Flexibility

This is an instructional plan, not a requirement to complete every maneuver.

If weather, turbulence, traffic, learner workload, or the startle response require it:

- reduce the number of stall repetitions,
- favor the impending-stall level over the full stall,
- repeat clearing turns and the floor brief before every maneuver,
- and terminate and climb if the recovery floor is approached.

Do not expand into spins, accelerated stalls, cross-control stalls, or secondary-stall practice. Those are awareness items only.

## Preflight and Cockpit

### Maneuver Briefing Before Start

#### Procedure

1. Complete the C172S preflight/checklist.
2. Confirm the practice area and the recovery floor in feet MSL (terrain + 1,500 feet AGL).
3. Brief the clearing-turn procedure before every maneuver.
4. Brief the recovery sequence for both stalls: reduce AOA → power → wings level → flightpath.
5. Brief the startle response: fly the airplane first, then analyze, then talk.
6. Confirm the configurations to be used and that no spins, accelerated stalls, or cross-control stalls will be practiced.

#### Common Errors

- No recovery floor briefed or computed.
- Unclear on the recovery order — power or wings before AOA.
- No plan for the startle response.

## Engine Start, Taxi, and Takeoff

Apply Lessons 2 and 3. Short version only:

1. Checklist / area clear / brake check / wind control.
2. Radio listen → understand → read back; hold short and verify.
3. Normal takeoff and climb at POH speeds, tracking upwind.

Do not reteach surface operations or the takeoff.

---

## En Route to Practice Area

#### Procedure

1. Climb to the working altitude.
2. Level and trim.
3. Reconfirm the recovery floor against the altimeter.

#### Common Errors

- Starting maneuvers without confirming the floor on the altimeter.

---

## Clearing Turns

#### Procedure

1. Two clearing turns before every maneuver.
2. Scan the area into which the maneuver will take you.
3. Confirm the recovery floor.

#### Common Errors

- Skipping clearing turns under workload.
- Scanning only where the airplane is, not where the maneuver will go.

---

## Slow Flight — Cruise Configuration

#### Procedure

1. Reduce power gradually; add back-elevator to hold altitude.
2. As speed decays to the target slow-flight speed, add power to maintain altitude.
3. Trim; right rudder for coordination.
4. Maneuver in straight flight and gentle turns, holding altitude and heading.
5. Silence any stall warning by slightly reducing AOA.

#### Common Errors

- Altitude loss on entry.
- Fixation on the flight instruments.
- Insufficient right rudder.
- Ignoring the stall warning.
- Poor power management; failure to trim.

---

## Slow Flight — Landing Configuration

#### Procedure

1. From slow flight, extend flaps incrementally, anticipating AOA and trim changes.
2. Maintain altitude and heading; note the higher power required.
3. Recover: add power, reduce AOA, retract flaps, accelerate to cruise.

#### Common Errors

- Abrupt flap changes.
- Not anticipating the AOA change as flaps extend or retract.
- Altitude loss during configuration change.

---

## Power-Off Stall

#### Procedure

1. Clearing turns; landing configuration; power to idle.
2. Hold altitude as speed decays — the flare picture.
3. Recognize the warning and buffet; develop the stall to the directed level.
4. Recover: reduce AOA → power → wings level → flaps up incrementally → climb.

#### Common Errors

- Not clearing the area.
- Missing the impending-stall cue; waiting for the break.
- Power or wings-level before reducing AOA.
- Secondary stall from pulling again too soon.
- Excessive airspeed buildup in the recovery dive.

---

## Power-On Stall

#### Procedure

1. Clearing turns; slow to lift-off/departure speed.
2. Apply takeoff power and raise the nose to the stall attitude.
3. Hold pitch; right rudder against left-turning tendency; recognize the break.
4. Recover: forward elevator to reduce AOA → ball centered → nose down → climb at VY.

#### Common Errors

- Pulling too fast on entry (inadvertent accelerated stall).
- Ball not centered against left-turning tendency.
- Recovering before reaching the critical AOA on a full stall.
- Power before AOA.
- Excessive forward pressure producing low or negative G.

---

## Return, Shutdown and Securing

#### Procedure

1. Cruise back; rejoin the pattern per Lesson 3 and tower per Lesson 4.
2. Land, exit, fully clear, then checklist, and reorient.
3. Taxi to parking; C172S shutdown, secure, postflight, log discrepancies.

#### Common Errors

- Heads-down before clear of the runway.
- Losing position after exit.

---

## Flight Completion Criteria

The flight portion is satisfactory for this stage when the learner demonstrates developing ability to:

- perform clearing turns and respect the 1,500-foot-AGL recovery floor,
- establish and maneuver in slow flight in cruise and landing configurations,
- recognize the earliest stall cues — horn and buffet — without relying on the airspeed indicator,
- recover from a power-off stall with reduce-AOA-first technique and minimum altitude loss,
- recover from a power-on stall while controlling left-turning tendency,
- and verbalize the spin-avoidance and PARE recovery concepts.

The lesson may be repeated when recognition, recovery order, coordination, or altitude discipline are not yet safe to continue.
