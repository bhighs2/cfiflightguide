# Lesson 3. Traffic Pattern, Takeoffs, Landings and Go-Arounds

**Lesson:** 3  
**Stage:** Stage I - Pre-Solo Foundations  
**Type:** GROUND + FLIGHT  
**Target Proficiency:** Developing

## Objective

Develop the learner's ability to fly a standard airport traffic pattern, perform a normal takeoff and climb, fly a normal approach and landing, and execute a practiced go-around in the Cessna 172S at David Wayne Hooks Memorial Airport (KDWH).

The learner should begin connecting wind, runway alignment, configuration, airspeed, ground track, traffic, radio, and a go-around decision into one continuous pattern picture. Surface operations and communications from Lesson 2 are applied, not reteached.

This lesson develops those skills with instructor supervision. Practical-test-level independence is not expected yet.

## Completion Standards

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

- Explain the standard traffic-pattern legs, typical pattern altitude concept, and why ground track—not heading—defines each leg.
- Use crab / wind-drift correction to hold pattern ground track on upwind, crosswind, downwind, and base.
- Brief and fly a normal takeoff and climb using C172S POH airspeeds, runway alignment, and outside visual references.
- Explain and, if conditions allow, practice a rejected-takeoff abort decision before the airplane is committed to flight.
- Fly a developing normal approach and landing: configured, on speed from the POH, aimed, rounded out, and touched down in a proper landing attitude with instructor coaching.
- Recognize an unstabilized approach and execute at least one practiced go-around.
- Apply Lesson 2 surface, hold-short, and radio habits while transitioning between the runway and the pattern.
- Describe wake-turbulence and windshear awareness as they apply to takeoff, landing, and go-around.
- Explain why a go-around can produce a strong pitch-up if the airplane is trimmed nose-up, and observe an instructor elevator-trim-stall demonstration when conditions allow.

The learner is **not expected** to demonstrate ACS numeric tolerances, independent crosswind landings, slips, stall recoveries as a learner task, short- or soft-field technique, night operations, LAHSO, or power-off 180s.

## Why This Matters

Takeoff, pattern, landing, and go-around are the highest-workload, highest-consequence sequence in early training. Most pre-solo accidents involve loss of control in the pattern, an unstable approach continued to landing, or a delayed go-around.

The traffic pattern is not a drawing to memorize. It is a moving three-dimensional plan that must stay aligned with the runway, corrected for wind, sequenced with traffic, and abandoned when the approach is no longer safe.

## Knowledge

### 1. Traffic Pattern Structure

The standard airport traffic pattern is a rectangular ground track around the landing runway.

Typical legs:

1. **Upwind / departure** — aligned with the runway after takeoff.
2. **Crosswind** — approximately 90° to the runway, used to reach downwind distance.
3. **Downwind** — parallel to the landing runway, opposite the landing direction.
4. **Base** — approximately 90° from downwind toward final.
5. **Final** — aligned with the landing runway.

Closed traffic at KDWH means remaining in that circuit rather than departing the area.

Teach:

- pattern altitude as a target to plan and capture, using local/airport guidance and instructor briefing;
- remain on the departure / upwind leg until within **300 ft of traffic pattern altitude**, then turn crosswind;
- approximate distance from the runway so base and final are not rushed;
- descending only when the plan calls for it, not because the airplane “feels high” without a reference;
- continuous traffic scan;
- and that ATC may alter the pattern with instructions that must be understood before they are flown.

[[highlight:
Key Point |
Fly the ground track the runway needs. Crab into the wind so each leg stays rectangular.
]]

### 2. Wind Drift and Crab in the Pattern

Wind correction in this lesson is **crab to hold ground track**.

- On downwind with a wind from the runway side, crab toward the runway or the pattern will balloon outward.
- On base, inadequate drift correction causes overshoot or undershoot of final.
- On final, crab to keep the ground track on centerline.

Do **not** teach the wing-low / sideslip landing or a crosswind landing as a learner task. That is Lesson 8.

A light crosswind takeoff may be introduced conceptually: aileron into the wind, rudder for centerline. Do not turn the lesson into crosswind-landing practice.

### 3. Right-of-Way and Pattern Traffic

An aircraft on final approach to land generally has the right-of-way over other aircraft in flight or on the surface. Do not cut in front of an aircraft already on final.

See-and-avoid remains continuous. Radio calls help shared awareness; they do not replace looking.

Airspace classification and weather minimums remain Lesson 4.

### 4. Normal Takeoff and Climb

Before lining up:

- complete the C172S before-takeoff checklist;
- verify runway, wind, traffic, and clearance/status using Lesson 2 discipline;
- brief abort: if it is not normal before committed to flight, close the throttle and stop straight ahead if conditions allow.

Takeoff process:

1. Align on centerline.
2. Verify heading and runway.
3. Apply takeoff power smoothly.
4. Check engine instruments.
5. Maintain centerline with rudder; anticipate left-turning tendency.
6. Use outside visual references for attitude and tracking, not the airspeed indicator alone.
7. Rotate to the proper lift-off attitude at the POH speed.
8. Accelerate to and climb at the POH **VY** unless a brief **VX** obstacle discussion is needed.
9. Trim.
10. Keep the climb aligned with the runway / upwind ground track.

**VX** is best angle of climb. **VY** is best rate of climb. Use the C172S POH values. Do not invent numbers.

Ground effect reduces induced drag near the surface. Do not yank the airplane off in ground effect and try to climb steeply before a safe climb speed exists.

### 5. Rejected Takeoff

A rejected takeoff is a normal safety decision.

Consider aborting for:

- insufficient power or abnormal engine indications,
- directional-control problem,
- obstruction on the runway,
- or any condition that makes continuing unsafe before the airplane is committed to flight.

If aborting:

1. Close the throttle.
2. Maintain directional control.
3. Stop straight ahead if runway remaining allows.
4. Do not try to leapfrog a problem into the air.

Practice the abort only when runway, traffic, and conditions make it safe. If conditions do not allow a full abort demonstration, still brief it.

### 6. Flaps, Configuration, and Stabilized Approach

Use flaps as the C172S POH and checklist provide for a normal landing.

A developing stabilized-approach idea for this lesson:

- on intended ground track;
- on intended glidepath to the aiming point;
- at POH approach speed for the configuration;
- configured for landing in a timely way;
- sink and power under control;
- and ready to go around.

If those are not present, go around.

Do not use elevator alone to stretch a landing. Pitch and power work together. Trim so the airplane is not being muscled to the aiming point.

### 7. Normal Approach and Landing

Typical closed-traffic landing flow, adjusted to KDWH, wind, and instructor briefing:

1. Complete the landing checklist in a timely manner — not on short final as a surprise.
2. On downwind, establish spacing and configuration as briefed.
3. Abeam the aiming point / as briefed, begin descent.
4. Turn base with wind-drift correction. Do not skid to “make the runway.”
5. Turn final aligned with centerline, crabbing as needed for ground track.
6. Aim at the selected aiming point.
7. Round out over the runway — not a dive at the numbers and a late yank.
8. Touch down in the proper landing attitude on the main wheels.
9. Hold sufficient back-elevator after touchdown.
10. Maintain directional control on rollout. Do not lock the brakes.

First landings today: **full-stop or stop-and-go preferred**. Touch-and-go is optional only if the landing, rollout, and configuration change are under control.

Focus during roundout: looking too close causes a high roundout; looking too far causes a low roundout.

### 8. Go-Around / Rejected Landing

A go-around is a **normal** maneuver. Practice it. Do not treat it as a failure.

Reasons include:

- ATC instruction,
- traffic or a hazard on the runway,
- unstable approach,
- wake turbulence or windshear,
- overtaking another airplane,
- or any condition that makes continuing hazardous.

Go-around process:

1. Decide.
2. Apply maximum allowable power smoothly but without delay.
3. Pitch to the appropriate climb attitude. Expect a pitch-up if trimmed nose-up.
4. Configure per POH/checklist (flaps in stages as appropriate; do not dump everything at once near the ground).
5. Establish a climb; do not try to zoom out of ground effect before the airplane will climb.
6. Compensate for torque / P-factor.
7. Climb, then communicate when the airplane is under control.
8. Rejoin the pattern as instructed or as appropriate.

[[highlight:
Safety |
Never delay a go-around to “see if it will work out.” Delay is a listed common error and a common accident chain.
]]

### 9. Wake Turbulence and Windshear Awareness

Take off and land beyond a preceding large aircraft’s rotation or touchdown point when applicable, and note wind drift of the vortices.

If windshear or a sudden airspeed/performance change appears on takeoff or final, the response may be reject, go around, or a maximum-performance climb as conditions require. Do not continue a landing into unrecognized windshear.

### 10. Faulty Approaches — Go Around, Do Not Salvage

AFH faulty-approach material is used here as **recognition**. Typical problems include floating, ballooning, bouncing, high roundout, low roundout, and loss of control after touchdown.

The instructional answer at this stage is almost always: **go around** if the approach or landing is not developing normally, rather than adding a new recovery maneuver.

Intentional slips are **not** the answer in this lesson.

### 11. Instructor Elevator Trim Stall — Awareness Only

This is an **instructor demonstration**, not a learner stall task and not Lesson 5.

Purpose: show why a go-around from a landing configuration with nose-up trim can produce a strong pitch-up, and why immediate forward pressure, then retrim and reconfiguration, are required.

If conditions allow, demonstrate at a safe altitude after pattern work, with a clear area, specified configuration, and instructor on the controls. The learner observes and later explains. The learner does not practice this as a stall task today.

## Risk Management

### Unstable Approach Continued to Landing

#### Hazard

The airplane is not on path, on speed, configured, or aligned, but the approach is continued.

#### Consequence

Hard landing, long landing, runway excursion, bounce, or loss of control.

#### Recognition

Large power or pitch changes, high/low/fast/slow, not tracking centerline, late configuration, or a rushed base-to-final.

#### Mitigation

Go around. Rebuild the pattern.

### Skidding Base-to-Final Turn

#### Hazard

Overshooting final and using inside rudder / outside aileron to yank the nose to the runway.

#### Consequence

Uncoordinated descending turn, stall/spin risk, collision with terrain.

#### Recognition

Nose swinging toward the runway while the airplane is still overshooting; heavy inside rudder; bank increasing.

#### Mitigation

Do not skid. If the turn is wrong, go around. Stall aerodynamics and stall recovery remain Lesson 5; the pattern decision belongs here.

### Delayed or Indecisive Go-Around

#### Hazard

The pilot recognizes a problem but waits.

#### Consequence

Landing in a worse energy state, runway overrun, or loss of control close to the ground.

#### Recognition

“Maybe it will work” on short final; late large corrections; traffic still on the runway.

#### Mitigation

Decide early. Power, pitch, configure, climb.

### Takeoff Abnormality Not Rejected

#### Hazard

Continuing a takeoff with abnormal power, control, or runway environment.

#### Consequence

Engine problem in initial climb, runway excursion, or collision.

#### Recognition

Engine instruments, failure to accelerate, directional-control problem, or obstruction.

#### Mitigation

Brief the abort. Reject while it is still a ground problem if conditions allow.

### Wake Turbulence / Windshear

#### Hazard

Vortex encounter or sudden wind/performance change during takeoff, landing, or go-around.

#### Consequence

Roll, sink, or airspeed loss close to the ground.

#### Recognition

Preceding large aircraft, light/quartering wind, reported windshear, or sudden airspeed/vertical-speed change.

#### Mitigation

Spacing, path selection, reject or go around, and do not linger on the same path as a heavier aircraft.

### Task Saturation in the Pattern

#### Hazard

Radio, configuration, traffic, and airplane control compete, and the scan collapses inside.

#### Consequence

Altitude/airspace busts, unstabilized approach, missed traffic, or runway-incursion after landing.

#### Mitigation

Prioritize: fly the airplane first, then navigate the pattern, then communicate. Use Lesson 2 radio habits. Full-stop or stop-and-go reduces configuration rush on early landings.

### Pitch-Up on Go-Around from Landing Trim

#### Hazard

Adding power with nose-up trim without immediate forward pressure.

#### Consequence

Excessive pitch, stall, or secondary stall during recovery.

#### Recognition

Yoke force increasing nose-up as power is added; airspeed decaying; stall warning.

#### Mitigation

Expect it. Forward pressure, then retrim. Instructor trim-stall demo is awareness for this hazard.

## Skills

### Traffic Pattern

#### Purpose

Produce a repeatable, wind-corrected rectangular ground track that sets up a stabilized final.

#### Procedure

1. Climb on upwind / departure, aligned with the runway ground track.
2. Remain on departure until within **300 ft of traffic pattern altitude**. Then turn **crosswind**, clearing first. Crab so the XWind ground track is rectangular, not blown downwind.
3. Turn downwind; crab as needed to parallel the runway at a usable distance.
4. Complete the landing checklist in a timely manner.
5. Abeam the aiming point / as briefed, configure and descend.
6. Turn base with drift correction. Do not skid.
7. Turn final to the centerline. Crab for ground track.
8. If the pattern will not produce a stabilized final, go around.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate one complete pattern, verbalizing ground track, crab, configuration, and the go-around decision point.

#### Learner Practice

Fly multiple closed-traffic circuits with coaching. Prefer full-stop or stop-and-go on first landings.

#### Common Errors

- Flying heading instead of ground track.
- Pattern too wide or too tight.
- Descending too early or too late.
- Inadequate wind-drift correction on base.
- Overshooting or undershooting the turn to final.
- Skidding base to final.
- Fixating inside and losing traffic scan.
- Treating the radio as more important than flying the airplane.

#### Completion Standard

The learner can describe and fly a developing rectangular pattern with crab for wind and an early go-around decision if the final is not working.

### Normal Takeoff and Climb

#### Purpose

Leave the runway aligned, under control, at POH speeds, with a planned abort if it is not normal.

#### Procedure

1. Verify runway, wind, traffic, clearance/status.
2. Brief abort.
3. Align on centerline.
4. Apply takeoff power smoothly.
5. Check engine instruments.
6. Maintain centerline; anticipate left-turning tendency.
7. Use outside references for attitude and tracking.
8. Rotate to the proper lift-off attitude at the POH speed.
9. Climb at POH **VY** unless otherwise briefed.
10. Trim. Keep wings level and track aligned.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate one takeoff with abort brief, instrument check, and outside-reference lift-off.

#### Learner Practice

Learner takeoffs with reduced prompting as control allows.

#### Common Errors

Common errors in the performance of normal takeoffs and departure climbs are:

- Failure to review AFM/POH and performance charts prior to takeoff.
- Failure to adequately clear the area prior to taxiing into position on the active runway.
- Abrupt use of the throttle.
- Failure to check engine instruments for signs of malfunction after applying takeoff power.
- Failure to anticipate the airplane’s left turning tendency on initial acceleration.
- Overcorrecting for left turning tendency.
- Relying solely on the airspeed indicator rather than developing an understanding of visual references and tracking clues of airplane airspeed and controllability during acceleration and lift-off.
- Failure to attain proper lift-off attitude.
- Inadequate compensation for torque/P-factor during initial climb resulting in a sideslip.
- Over-control of elevators during initial climb-out and lack of elevator trimming.
- Limiting scan to areas directly ahead of the airplane (pitch attitude and direction), causing a wing (usually the left) to drop immediately after lift-off.
- Failure to attain/maintain best rate-of-climb airspeed (VY) or desired climb airspeed.
- Failure to employ the principles of attitude flying during climb-out, resulting in “chasing” the airspeed indicator.

#### Completion Standard

The learner performs a developing normal takeoff: smooth power, instrument check, centerline, proper attitude, and POH climb speed with coaching.

### Rejected Takeoff

#### Purpose

Make an abnormal takeoff a ground problem while it still can be.

#### Procedure

1. Brief before lining up.
2. If aborting: throttle closed.
3. Maintain directional control.
4. Stop straight ahead if conditions allow.
5. Do not continue into the air to “see what happens.”

#### Instructor Demonstration / Learner Practice

If runway remaining, traffic, and conditions allow, practice or demonstrate an abort early in the roll. If not, complete a thorough verbal abort brief and instructor simulation as appropriate.

#### Common Errors

- No abort brief.
- Continuing with abnormal indications.
- Abrupt swerve during abort.
- Indecision after the problem is already obvious.

#### Completion Standard

The learner can brief the abort and, if practiced, close the throttle and keep the airplane straight.

### Normal Approach and Landing

#### Purpose

Arrive at the aiming point on path and on POH speed, then round out, touch down in a landing attitude, and keep control on rollout.

#### Procedure

1. Complete the landing checklist in a timely manner.
2. Establish downwind spacing and configuration.
3. Descend as briefed.
4. Correct wind drift on base. Do not skid to final.
5. Align on final; crab for ground track. No slip instruction.
6. Aim. Use pitch and power. Trim.
7. Round out. Touch down on the mains in a proper landing attitude.
8. Hold sufficient back-elevator after touchdown.
9. Directional control first; braking only as needed.
10. Exit, then checklist, using Lesson 2 runway-clear discipline.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate one approach and landing, including aiming point, roundout look, and rollout.

#### Learner Practice

Multiple approaches. Full-stop or stop-and-go preferred. Touch-and-go only if appropriate.

#### Common Errors

Common errors in the performance of normal approaches and landings are:

1. Failure to complete the landing checklist in a timely manner.
2. Inadequate wind drift correction on the base leg.
3. An overshooting, undershooting, too steep, or too shallow a turn onto final approach.
4. A skidding turn from base leg to final approach as a result of overshooting/inadequate wind drift correction.
5. Poor coordination during turn from base to final approach.
6. Unstable approach.
7. Failure to adequately compensate for flap extension.
8. Poor trim technique on final approach.
9. Attempting to maintain altitude or reach the runway using elevator alone.
10. Focusing too close to the airplane resulting in a too high round out.
11. Focusing too far from the airplane resulting in a too low round out.
12. Touching down prior to attaining proper landing attitude.
13. Failure to hold sufficient back-elevator pressure after touchdown.
14. Excessive braking after touchdown.
15. Loss of aircraft control during touchdown and rollout.

#### Completion Standard

The learner flies a developing approach to a supervised landing or a timely go-around. ACS landing tolerances are not required.

### Go-Around / Rejected Landing

#### Purpose

Make rejecting a landing a trained, prompt, normal maneuver.

#### Procedure

1. Recognize the need.
2. Maximum allowable power without delay, not a punch that yaws the airplane.
3. Pitch for climb; immediate forward pressure if trimmed nose-up.
4. Configure per POH/checklist.
5. Climb; do not try to pull out of ground effect prematurely.
6. Compensate for torque / P-factor.
7. When under control, communicate and rejoin as appropriate.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate at least one go-around from a realistic approach.

#### Learner Practice

**At least one practiced go-around** is required for this lesson unless safety uniquely prevents it. Prefer a planned go-around plus any unplanned one that conditions create.

#### Common Errors

Common errors in the performance of go-arounds (rejected landings) are:

1. Failure to recognize a condition that warrants a rejected landing.
2. Indecision.
3. Delay in initiating a go-around.
4. Failure to apply maximum allowable power in a timely manner.
5. Abrupt power application.
6. Improper pitch attitude.
7. Failure to configure the airplane appropriately.
8. Attempting to climb out of ground effect prematurely.
9. Failure to adequately compensate for torque/P factor.
10. Loss of aircraft control.

#### Completion Standard

The learner initiates a timely go-around, controls pitch, configures, and climbs with coaching.

### Instructor Elevator Trim Stall Demonstration

#### Purpose

Awareness only: show the pitch-up that can follow a power application from landing trim.

#### Procedure

Instructor on the controls, at a safe altitude, if conditions allow:

1. Clear the area.
2. Establish the specified demonstration configuration.
3. Set realistic thrust/torque and nose-up trim as briefed for the demo.
4. Add power and immediately control pitch with forward pressure.
5. Recover, retrim, reconfigure, and maintain control.
6. Debrief the go-around connection.

The learner does **not** perform this as a stall task.

#### Common Errors

Instructor watches for, and debriefs:

- Failure to clear the area.
- Failing specified configuration.
- Not setting realistic thrust/torque/nose-up trim.
- Improper pitch/heading/bank.
- Failure to recognize first stall indications.
- Allowing pitch to increase too high without immediate forward pressure when power is added.
- Reducing power during recovery.
- Failing to maintain control while retrimming/retracting flaps.
- Secondary stall from pulling too soon.

#### Completion Standard

If demonstrated, the learner can explain why go-around pitch control and retrim matter. This is not a Lesson 5 stall completion item.

## Scenario Application

### Scenario 1 - Unstable Final

**Situation:**

On short final the airplane is high, fast, and not tracking centerline. The aiming point is sliding under the nose.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- Is this approach stabilized?
- What last-second corrections are unsafe?
- What is the airplane’s next maneuver?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Go around. Do not salvage.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 2 - Traffic on the Runway

**Situation:**

On final, another airplane is still on the runway.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- Who has the right-of-way problem now?
- Do you continue because you already configured?
- When do you go around, and what is the first control action?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Go around without delay; fly the airplane first; then communicate.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 3 - Wind Drift on Base

**Situation:**

A crosswind is drifting the airplane through the extended centerline on base.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What happens if you skid the nose to the runway?
- How do you use crab on base and final in this lesson?
- When is the correct answer a go-around rather than a tighter turn?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Correct with coordinated crab / ground-track control, or go around. No slip. No skid.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 4 - Abnormal Takeoff Roll

**Situation:**

After applying takeoff power, engine indications are abnormal or acceleration is clearly wrong.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- Are you committed to flight?
- What is the abort procedure?
- What would make you continue?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Reject the takeoff if not committed to flight; keep it straight; stop.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 5 - Changed Pattern Instruction

**Situation:**

ATC extends downwind or calls a short approach while the learner is already task-saturated.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What is understood versus merely heard?
- Can this still produce a stabilized final?
- If not, what do you do?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Understand before acting. If the approach will not be stable, go around or request an alternative. Apply Lesson 2 radio discipline.

**Expected Performance:** Developing, with instructor prompting.

## Closed Traffic / Pattern

#### Procedure

1. Upwind / departure aligned with runway ground track.
2. Remain on departure until within **300 ft of TPA**. Then turn **XWind**; clear first.
3. XWind: ~90° to the runway; crab so the ground track is rectangular, not blown downwind.
4. Downwind: parallel the runway with crab; timely landing checklist.
5. Descent as briefed.
6. Base with drift correction; coordinated turn.
7. Final aligned; crab for track, not a slip.
8. If not stabilized, go around.

#### Common Errors

- Flying heading instead of ground track.
- Turning XWind before within 300 ft of TPA.
- Pattern too wide or too tight.
- XWind / downwind blown by the wind (no crab).
- Late checklist.
- Descending too early or too late.
- Inadequate base drift correction.
- Overshoot/undershoot or skid to final.
- Inside fixation.

#### Instructor Demonstration / Learner Practice

Instructor demonstrates the first pattern as needed. Learner then flies subsequent patterns with coaching.
