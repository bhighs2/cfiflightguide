# Lesson 6. Wind Correction and Ground Reference Maneuvers

**Lesson:** 6  
**Stage:** Stage I — Pre-Solo Foundations  
**Type:** GROUND + FLIGHT  
**Target Proficiency:** Developing

## Objective

Develop the learner's ability to control the airplane's ground track in wind by applying deliberate wind correction — crabbing into the wind and varying bank angle through turns — while flying the rectangular course, turns around a point, and S-turns across a road. The learner will fly each maneuver at a consistent altitude (600–1,000 feet AGL) and airspeed, dividing attention between airplane control and ground references, and will understand the rectangular course as the direct link between wind correction and the traffic pattern.

## Completion Standards

By the end of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

- Explain how wind drifts the airplane off its intended ground track and how crab and bank correct for it.
- Select a suitable ground reference and a safe maneuvering area, clear of people, property, and obstructions.
- Enter each maneuver from a downwind position, establishing the steepest bank first.
- Fly a rectangular course, maintaining a constant-altitude, constant-airspeed rectangular ground track with appropriate crab on each leg.
- Fly turns around a point, holding a constant radius over the ground by continuously varying bank angle for wind.
- Fly S-turns across a road, reversing a constant-radius turn directly over the reference line.
- Maintain the selected altitude and airspeed throughout, coordinating aileron and rudder without slips or skids.
- Apply Lesson 5 awareness: avoid excessive bank and skidding at low altitude, and respect the stall/spin margin.

## Why This Matters

Ground reference maneuvers are where wind stops being an abstraction and becomes something the pilot must actively manage. Every traffic pattern, every approach, and every forced landing is a ground-reference problem: the runway or field is fixed to the earth, and the wind is trying to push the airplane off the track that reaches it. A pilot who can hold a precise ground track at low altitude — correcting smoothly and continuously for wind — is a pilot who can fly an accurate pattern and, later, put the airplane on a field in an emergency.

The rectangular course is, quite literally, the traffic pattern flown around a field instead of a runway. Mastering it here pays directly in Lesson 7's engine-failure approach and in every pattern the learner will ever fly.

[[highlight:
Track, Not Heading |
The wind is moving the air mass the airplane flies in. Heading is where the nose points; ground track is where the airplane actually goes over the earth. Ground reference maneuvers are flown entirely by track — crab into the wind so the track over the ground is the shape you intend.
]]

## Knowledge

### 1. Purpose and the Low-Altitude Environment

Ground reference maneuvers are the principal maneuvers that combine the four fundamentals while placing the airplane in relation to specific references on the ground. They develop division of attention, precise airplane control, and wind-drift correction — and they place the airplane in a low-altitude environment with associated hazards: terrain, obstacles, other traffic, and reduced margin for error.

Ground reference maneuvers are flown between **600 and 1,000 feet AGL**. **Consistency is key: choose an altitude and an airspeed, and stick with them.**

**Source:**

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=147 | end=151]]

### 2. Wind Drift and Ground Track Control

Wind moves the air mass the airplane flies in, so the airplane's **track over the ground** differs from its **heading** whenever there is a crosswind component.

- **Crab** — a wind correction angle into the wind — holds a straight ground track. The stronger the crosswind, the greater the crab.
- In a **turn**, the wind effect changes continuously as the heading changes, so bank angle must change continuously to hold a constant-radius track over the ground.
- **Groundspeed** is highest downwind and lowest upwind. To hold a constant radius, bank is **steepest downwind** and **shallowest upwind**.

### 3. The Rectangular Course

The rectangular course is a rectangular ground track flown around a field or reference area — **it is the traffic pattern flown around a field.** The learner applies crab on each leg exactly as in the pattern.

- Enter on a downwind, at the chosen altitude and airspeed.
- Crab into the wind on each leg to hold the rectangular track.
- Time the turns so the corners are square, adjusting bank and roll rate for drift.
- Maintain altitude and airspeed throughout.

### 4. Turns Around a Point

Turns around a point are a 360° constant-radius turn around a single ground reference. Because the airplane is flying through a moving air mass, the pilot **constantly adjusts bank angle** to hold the radius.

- Enter downwind; establish the steepest bank first.
- Steepest bank on the downwind side, shallowest on the upwind side.
- Keep the reference point at a constant distance — neither spiraling in nor drifting out.
- Maintain altitude, airspeed, and coordination.

### 5. S-Turns Across a Road

S-turns are a series of constant-radius half-circles of equal size on opposite sides of a straight reference line, reversing direction **directly over the line**.

- Enter perpendicular to the road, downwind, so the first (downwind) bank is steepest.
- Reverse the turn with wings level exactly over the road.
- Each half-circle is a constant radius, symmetrical about the line.
- Maintain altitude, airspeed, and coordination.

## Risk Management

### Low-Altitude Maneuvering

#### Hazard

Maneuvering between 600 and 1,000 feet AGL places the airplane close to terrain, obstacles, and other traffic, with little margin for error.

#### Consequence

Collision with terrain, obstacles, or wires; forced landing in an unsuitable area.

#### Recognition

Rising workload, a sinking or climbing altitude, or a ground track drifting toward hazards.

#### Mitigation

Choose a suitable area and reference, hold the briefed altitude and airspeed, keep the scan outside-dominant, and terminate the maneuver if altitude or track is not being held.

### Stall/Spin at Low Altitude

#### Hazard

Excessive bank or a skidding turn at low altitude raises load factor and stall speed or produces a cross-control stall — with no altitude to recover.

#### Consequence

Stall/spin close to the ground; loss of control; collision with terrain.

#### Recognition

Bank steepening beyond what the maneuver needs, the ball out of center, speed decaying, or the horn/buffet.

#### Mitigation

Apply Lesson 5 awareness: never exceed the bank the maneuver requires, keep the ball centered, respect the stall margin, and go around / recover level if the maneuver is not working. Never "help" a turn with rudder.

### Unsuitable Area or Reference

#### Hazard

Selecting a reference line, point, or course over people, property, roads with traffic, or obstructed terrain.

#### Consequence

Hazard to persons and property on the ground; no safe forced-landing option; legal exposure.

#### Recognition

Houses, power lines, vehicles, livestock, or congested areas beneath or near the maneuver.

#### Mitigation

Select a reference over open, unpopulated terrain with a suitable forced-landing option nearby. If the only available reference is unsuitable, do not fly the maneuver there.

### Fixation on the Ground Reference

#### Hazard

Staring at the reference and losing altitude control, airspeed control, and traffic scan.

#### Consequence

Altitude/airspeed deviation, disorientation, or collision with unseen traffic.

#### Recognition

Altitude or airspeed wandering; a scan that has collapsed onto the reference point.

#### Mitigation

Divide attention deliberately — reference, altitude, airspeed, scan, repeat. The reference is a cue, not a fixation point.

### Wind Misjudgment

#### Hazard

Misreading wind direction or strength, so the wrong correction is applied and the ground track drifts.

#### Consequence

Drifting into the reference, off the course, or toward hazards; an uncoordinated, inaccurate maneuver.

#### Recognition

The ground track not matching the intended shape; the circle widening on one side; corners not squaring.

#### Mitigation

Assess wind before entering (smoke, water, wind lines, ATIS, drift on the way out). Reassess during the maneuver and correct early and smoothly.

## Skills and Procedures

### Rectangular Course

#### Purpose

Fly a rectangular ground track around a reference, applying crab on each leg — the traffic pattern flown around a field.

#### Procedure

1. Select a suitable rectangular reference over open terrain; assess wind.
2. Establish the briefed altitude (600–1,000 ft AGL) and airspeed; trim.
3. Enter on a downwind.
4. Crab into the wind on each leg to hold the rectangular track.
5. Time the turns so the corners are square; adjust roll rate for drift.
6. Maintain altitude, airspeed, and coordination throughout.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate one circuit, verbalizing wind assessment, crab on each leg, and the timed, square turns.

#### Learner Practice

The learner flies left and right rectangular courses with coaching on wind correction and timing.

#### Common Errors

Common errors in the performance of rectangular courses are:

1. Failure to adequately clear the surrounding area for safety hazards, initially and throughout the maneuver.
2. Failure to establish a constant, level altitude prior to entering the maneuver.
3. Failure to maintain altitude during the maneuver.
4. Failure to properly assess wind direction.
5. Failure to establish the appropriate wind correction angle.
6. Failure to apply coordinated aileron and rudder pressure, resulting in slips and skids.
7. Failure to manipulate the flight controls in a smooth and continuous manner.
8. Failure to properly divide attention between airplane control and orientation with ground references.
9. Failure to execute turns with accurate timing.

#### Completion Standard

The learner flies a developing rectangular course — constant altitude and airspeed, crab on each leg, square corners, coordinated — with coaching.

### Turns Around a Point

#### Purpose

Hold a constant-radius circle around a single ground reference by continuously varying bank for wind.

#### Procedure

1. Select a suitable point over open terrain; assess wind.
2. Establish the briefed altitude and airspeed; trim.
3. Enter downwind; establish the steepest bank first.
4. Vary bank continuously — steepest downwind, shallowest upwind — to hold a constant radius.
5. Keep the point at a constant distance; do not spiral in or drift out.
6. Maintain altitude, airspeed, and coordination.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate one circle, verbalizing the changing bank and the constant radius.

#### Learner Practice

The learner flies turns around a point in both directions with coaching on bank modulation.

#### Common Errors

Common errors in the performance of turns around a point are:

1. Failure to adequately clear the surrounding area for safety hazards, initially and throughout the maneuver.
2. Failure to establish a constant, level altitude prior to entering the maneuver.
3. Failure to maintain altitude during the maneuver.
4. Failure to properly assess wind direction.
5. Failure to properly execute constant-radius turns.
6. Failure to manipulate the flight controls in a smooth and continuous manner.
7. Failure to establish the appropriate wind correction angle.
8. Failure to apply coordinated aileron and rudder pressure, resulting in slips or skids.

#### Completion Standard

The learner holds a developing constant-radius turn around a point, varying bank for wind, holding altitude and airspeed, coordinated — with coaching.

### S-Turns Across a Road

#### Purpose

Fly constant-radius half-circles of equal size on opposite sides of a straight reference, reversing directly over the line.

#### Procedure

1. Select a suitable straight road over open terrain; assess wind.
2. Establish the briefed altitude and airspeed; trim.
3. Enter perpendicular to the road, downwind, so the first (downwind) bank is steepest.
4. Reverse the turn with wings level exactly over the road.
5. Hold constant-radius, symmetrical half-circles on each side.
6. Maintain altitude, airspeed, and coordination.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate two reversals, verbalizing the wings-level crossing and the symmetrical radii.

#### Learner Practice

The learner flies S-turns with coaching on the reversal timing and radius symmetry.

#### Common Errors

Common errors in the performance of S-turns across a road are:

1. Failure to adequately clear surrounding area for safety hazards, initially and throughout the maneuver.
2. Failure to establish a constant, level altitude prior to entering the maneuver.
3. Failure to maintain altitude during the maneuver.
4. Failure to properly assess wind direction.
5. Failure to properly execute constant-radius turns.
6. Failure to manipulate the flight controls in a smooth and continuous manner when transitioning into turns.
7. Failure to establish the appropriate wind correction angle.
8. Failure to apply coordinated aileron and rudder pressure, resulting in slips or skids.

#### Completion Standard

The learner flies developing S-turns — constant-radius, symmetrical half-circles reversed over the line, holding altitude and airspeed, coordinated — with coaching.

## Scenario Application

### Scenario 1 — Blown Toward the Course

**Situation:**

On the downwind leg of the rectangular course, the wind is drifting you toward the reference area, narrowing the rectangle.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- Which way do you crab on this leg, and why?
- How is this leg exactly like a traffic-pattern downwind?
- What happens if you fly heading instead of track?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Crab away from the course enough to hold the rectangular track, and recognize the rectangular course as the pattern flown around a field. Track, not heading.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 2 — The Widening Circle

**Situation:**

In turns around a point, you finish the circle wider on the downwind side than you started.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- What does a widening downwind side tell you about your bank?
- Where should the bank have been steepest?
- What correction fixes the next circle?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Recognize that the bank was too shallow on the downwind (high-groundspeed) side. Increase bank on the downwind side of the next circle to hold a constant radius.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 3 — The Asymmetric S

**Situation:**

In S-turns, you cross the road upwind with the bank still too steep, and the upwind half-circle comes out tight while the downwind half comes out wide.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- Where in the S-turn should the bank be steepest and shallowest?
- What does crossing the road wings-level enforce?
- How do you make the two halves symmetrical?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Steepest bank downwind, shallowest upwind, wings level over the road. Match the radii by modulating bank, not by rushing the reversal.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

### Scenario 4 — The Only Straight Road

**Situation:**

The only straight road nearby has houses along it, power lines crossing it, and steady vehicle traffic.

**Discuss / Decide:**

- Is this a suitable reference for S-turns?
- What are the hazards to people and property?
- What do you do if no suitable reference is available?

**Desired Learner Outcome:**

Decline the maneuver at that reference. Select open, unpopulated terrain with a forced-landing option nearby, or do not fly the maneuver.

**Expected Performance:** Developing.

# Flight Card

## Flight Setup

### Lesson Target

**Target Proficiency: Developing**

Primary lesson target:

**Wind correction and ground-track control through the rectangular course, turns around a point, and S-turns across a road, at a consistent altitude (600–1,000 ft AGL) and airspeed.**

Secondary airborne target:

**Keep Lesson 1 control quality — outside references, coordination, trim — while dividing attention between the airplane and the ground references.**

Boundary:

**No eights. Rectangular course, turns around a point, and S-turns only.**

### Scope and Flexibility

This is an instructional plan, not a requirement to complete every maneuver.

If wind, turbulence, traffic, terrain, or learner workload require it:

- reduce the number of repetitions,
- fly a single direction or a single maneuver well rather than all three poorly,
- re-brief wind assessment and the altitude/airspeed discipline,
- and climb away from the low-altitude environment if control or awareness is slipping.

Do not expand into eights, steep turns, slips, or engine-out work.

---

## Preflight and Cockpit

### Maneuver Briefing Before Start

#### Procedure

1. Complete the C172S preflight/checklist.
2. Select the practice area and suitable references over open, unpopulated terrain.
3. Plan the maneuver altitude (600–1,000 ft AGL) and a consistent airspeed; brief holding both.
4. Assess the wind and plan the downwind entry.
5. Brief each maneuver: rectangular course, turns around a point, S-turns.
6. Brief the boundary: no eights, no excessive bank, no skidding, respect the stall margin.

#### Common Errors

- No altitude/airspeed plan — maneuvering inconsistently.
- Reference over people, property, or obstructions.
- No wind assessment before entry.

---

## Engine Start, Taxi, and Takeoff

Apply Lessons 2 and 3. Short version only: checklist, brake check, wind control, radio discipline, hold short and verify, normal takeoff and climb at POH speeds.

Do not reteach surface operations or the takeoff.

---

## En Route to Practice Area

#### Procedure

1. Climb to the working altitude.
2. Level, trim, and set the briefed airspeed.
3. Assess wind by smoke, water, wind lines, and drift.
4. Confirm the reference and area are suitable.

#### Common Errors

- Arriving without a wind read.
- Discovering the reference is unsuitable only after setting up.

---

## Rectangular Course

#### Procedure

1. Enter downwind at the briefed altitude and airspeed.
2. Crab into the wind on each leg to hold the rectangular track.
3. Time the turns so the corners are square; adjust roll rate for drift.
4. Maintain altitude, airspeed, and coordination.

#### Common Errors

Common errors in the performance of rectangular courses are:

1. Failure to adequately clear the surrounding area for safety hazards, initially and throughout the maneuver.
2. Failure to establish a constant, level altitude prior to entering the maneuver.
3. Failure to maintain altitude during the maneuver.
4. Failure to properly assess wind direction.
5. Failure to establish the appropriate wind correction angle.
6. Failure to apply coordinated aileron and rudder pressure, resulting in slips and skids.
7. Failure to manipulate the flight controls in a smooth and continuous manner.
8. Failure to properly divide attention between airplane control and orientation with ground references.
9. Failure to execute turns with accurate timing.

---

## Turns Around a Point

#### Procedure

1. Enter downwind; establish the steepest bank first.
2. Vary bank continuously — steepest downwind, shallowest upwind — to hold a constant radius.
3. Keep the point at a constant distance.
4. Maintain altitude, airspeed, and coordination.

#### Common Errors

Common errors in the performance of turns around a point are:

1. Failure to adequately clear the surrounding area for safety hazards, initially and throughout the maneuver.
2. Failure to establish a constant, level altitude prior to entering the maneuver.
3. Failure to maintain altitude during the maneuver.
4. Failure to properly assess wind direction.
5. Failure to properly execute constant-radius turns.
6. Failure to manipulate the flight controls in a smooth and continuous manner.
7. Failure to establish the appropriate wind correction angle.
8. Failure to apply coordinated aileron and rudder pressure, resulting in slips or skids.

---

## S-Turns Across a Road

#### Procedure

1. Enter perpendicular to the road, downwind, so the first bank is steepest.
2. Reverse the turn with wings level exactly over the road.
3. Hold constant-radius, symmetrical half-circles on each side.
4. Maintain altitude, airspeed, and coordination.

#### Common Errors

Common errors in the performance of S-turns across a road are:

1. Failure to adequately clear surrounding area for safety hazards, initially and throughout the maneuver.
2. Failure to establish a constant, level altitude prior to entering the maneuver.
3. Failure to maintain altitude during the maneuver.
4. Failure to properly assess wind direction.
5. Failure to properly execute constant-radius turns.
6. Failure to manipulate the flight controls in a smooth and continuous manner when transitioning into turns.
7. Failure to establish the appropriate wind correction angle.
8. Failure to apply coordinated aileron and rudder pressure, resulting in slips or skids.

---

## Return, Shutdown and Securing

#### Procedure

1. Climb away from the practice area and cruise back.
2. Rejoin the pattern per Lesson 3 and tower per Lesson 4.
3. Land, exit, fully clear, then checklist, and reorient.
4. Taxi to parking; C172S shutdown, secure, postflight, log discrepancies.

#### Common Errors

- Heads-down before clear of the runway.
- Losing position after exit.

---

## Flight Completion Criteria

The flight portion is satisfactory for this stage when the learner demonstrates developing ability to:

- select a suitable reference and area and assess wind before entering,
- enter from downwind and hold the briefed altitude and airspeed consistently,
- fly a rectangular course with crab on each leg and square corners,
- hold a constant radius in turns around a point by varying bank for wind,
- fly symmetrical S-turns, reversing wings-level over the line,
- and coordinate aileron and rudder without slips or skids while dividing attention outside.

The lesson may be repeated when altitude/airspeed consistency, wind correction, or coordination are not yet safe to continue.
