# Lesson 1. Aircraft Familiarization, Preflight and Basic Control

## Lesson Information

- **Stage:** Stage I — Pre-Solo Foundations
- **Lesson Type:** GROUND + FLIGHT
- **Estimated Ground:** 1.0–1.5 hours
- **Estimated Flight:** 1.0–1.3 hours
- **Prerequisites:** Lesson 0 complete; access to the Cessna 172S POH and aircraft checklist
- **Target Proficiency:** Introduced

## Objective

Introduce the learner to the Cessna 172S, establish a systematic approach to determining whether the airplane is ready for flight, develop safe cockpit and checklist habits, and begin hands-on aircraft control using outside visual references.

The learner should leave the lesson able to identify the airplane's major components and flight controls, participate meaningfully in a supervised preflight inspection, understand the purpose of the major preflight and before-takeoff procedures, and make smooth basic control inputs in straight-and-level flight, turns, climbs, descents, and trim with instructor guidance.

## Completion Standards

At the completion of this lesson, the learner should be able to:

- Identify the major external components of the Cessna 172S and describe their basic functions.
- Identify the primary and secondary flight controls and describe the primary motion produced by each.
- Locate and use the applicable Cessna 172S POH and aircraft checklist.
- Explain the purpose of the required aircraft documents at an introductory level.
- Conduct a systematic preflight inspection with instructor guidance and checklist use.
- Recognize that fuel quantity, fuel quality, oil, control surfaces, landing gear, tires, brakes, engine, propeller, openings, antennas, and general condition require deliberate assessment before flight.
- Organize the cockpit and identify the major controls, switches, instruments, and systems needed for the lesson.
- Demonstrate the positive exchange of flight controls without prompting.
- Participate in engine start, basic taxi, and before-takeoff procedures using the aircraft checklist and instructor supervision.
- Maintain basic aircraft control using outside visual references during straight-and-level flight, shallow-to-moderate turns, climbs, and descents with instructor coaching.
- Use trim to relieve sustained control pressure after establishing the desired attitude.
- Participate in after-landing, shutdown, securing, and postflight procedures using the checklist.
- Identify the principal risks associated with preflight inspection, propeller operations, cockpit distractions, taxiing, checklist use, control transfer, overcontrol, and excessive inside fixation.

The learner is **not expected to demonstrate practical-test-level precision** during this introductory lesson. Safe habits, correct concepts, deliberate procedures, and basic aircraft control are the priorities.

    - **Task A — Pilot Qualifications** — introductory document and eligibility awareness.
    - **Task B — Airworthiness Requirements** — aircraft documents, inspections, discrepancies, and airworthiness awareness.

## Why This Matters

Every flight begins before the engine starts.

The pilot first determines whether the airplane appears suitable for flight, organizes the cockpit, uses the correct procedures, controls risk on the ground, and establishes disciplined habits before becoming airborne.

Those habits matter because later training adds weather, communications, navigation, traffic, passengers, abnormal situations, and increasingly complex maneuvers. The learner who develops deliberate habits now has more mental capacity available when workload increases later.

## Instructional Approach

This lesson uses a combination of:

- **Preparation** — assigned reading, videos, and aircraft-specific review before the lesson.
- **Presentation** — instructor explanation, aircraft familiarization, guided discussion, and demonstration.
- **Application** — learner participation in preflight, cockpit procedures, taxi, basic aircraft control, and scenario-based decisions.
- **Review and Evaluation** — review questions, instructor evaluation, debrief, learner reflection, and assignment of the next lesson.

The lesson should be adapted to the learner's experience, pace, and demonstrated understanding. If workload becomes excessive or prerequisite knowledge or skill is inadequate, the instructor should reduce scope, repeat a task, or defer an item rather than force completion of every planned element in one period.

### 2. Aircraft Documents and Basic Airworthiness

#### ARROW

[[mnemonic:
ARROW |
Airworthiness Certificate · Registration Certificate · Radio Station License when required · Operating Limitations · Weight and Balance
]]

ARROW is a useful **training mnemonic**, but the learner should progressively understand what each item actually means, where it is found, and when it is required.

#### A — Airworthiness Certificate

The Airworthiness Certificate is carried aboard the aircraft and is displayed at the **cabin or cockpit entrance** so that it is visible and legible to passengers or crew.

In many training airplanes, the Airworthiness Certificate and Registration Certificate are kept together in a clear document pocket near the cabin or cockpit entrance.

</figure>

[[highlight:
Document Location |
Do not simply memorize the letter **A** in ARROW. On the actual training airplane, physically locate the Airworthiness Certificate and know where to verify it before flight.
]]

#### R — Registration Certificate and Registration Marks

Two different items should not be confused:

- The aircraft's **registration marks** — the N-number — are displayed on the **outside of the aircraft**.
- The **paper Registration Certificate** is carried **aboard the aircraft**.

The paper Registration Certificate is commonly stored with the Airworthiness Certificate in a clear document pocket inside the airplane, although the exact location varies by aircraft and operator.

[[highlight:
Key Distinction |
The **N-number is visible outside**. The **Registration Certificate is a document carried onboard**.

Do not describe the paper Registration Certificate itself as the item displayed on the exterior of the airplane.
]]

#### R — Radio Station License When Required

A radio station license is not normally required for routine domestic U.S. operations, but it may be required for international operations.

The learner should understand why ARROW includes the item without assuming it is required for every training flight.

#### O — Operating Limitations

Operating limitations may be expressed through the approved flight manual/POH, markings, placards, and other approved limitations applicable to the airplane.

During Lesson 1, the learner should know **where to find the limitations**, not attempt to memorize the entire limitations section.

#### W — Weight and Balance Information

The learner should know where the operator maintains the current aircraft weight-and-balance information and understand that aircraft loading and center-of-gravity calculations will be developed in detail later in the syllabus.

Detailed weight-and-balance and performance training occurs in **Lesson 14 — Aircraft Performance, Weight and Balance, and Density Altitude**.

#### Airworthiness Awareness

At this stage, the learner should understand that a pilot does not determine airworthiness by looking at one item alone.

The preflight decision considers, as applicable:

- Required documents.
- Required inspections and maintenance status.
- Known discrepancies.
- Required equipment.
- Operating limitations.
- The actual physical condition observed during preflight.

Detailed inspections, maintenance records, Airworthiness Directives, preventive maintenance, and inoperative-equipment rules are developed later in the syllabus.

### 3. Flight Controls

#### Primary Flight Controls

[[mnemonic:
AILERON → ROLL · ELEVATOR → PITCH · RUDDER → YAW |
The three primary flight controls produce the airplane's primary roll, pitch, and yaw responses.
]]

The learner should connect three things:

1. The cockpit control movement.
2. The movement of the corresponding control surface.
3. The airplane's response.

#### Ailerons

Ailerons are used primarily to control **roll**. Moving the control wheel left or right commands a rolling response through opposite movement of the ailerons.

#### Elevator

The elevator is used primarily to control **pitch**. Fore-and-aft movement of the control wheel changes elevator position and changes the airplane's pitch attitude.

#### Rudder

The rudder is used primarily to control **yaw** through the rudder pedals.

The learner should not think of the rudder as a steering wheel for flight. Its use is coordinated with the other controls as training progresses.

#### Flaps

Flaps are secondary flight controls. Lesson 1 introduces their location, cockpit control, indication, and basic purpose. Detailed flap use is developed during takeoff and landing training.

#### Trim

Trim is used to relieve sustained control pressure after the pilot establishes the desired attitude.

[[mnemonic:
CONTROL → HOLD → TRIM |
Use the primary flight control to establish the desired attitude, hold the required pressure, then trim to relieve that pressure.
]]

[[highlight:
Key Point |
**Trim relieves control pressure. Trim does not replace the flight controls.**

First establish the desired attitude with the primary flight controls. Hold the attitude. Then trim until the sustained control pressure is relieved.
]]

### 4. Preflight Assessment

#### Purpose

A preflight inspection is an assessment of the airplane's apparent condition and suitability for the planned operation.

It should be systematic enough to reduce omission while remaining thoughtful enough that the pilot actually observes each item rather than merely touches it.

[[highlight:
Preflight Principle |
A preflight inspection is **not a ritual and not a race**.

The objective is to determine whether the airplane appears safe and suitable for the planned flight.

If something looks, sounds, smells, or feels wrong, stop and investigate it.
]]

#### Use the Checklist

The learner should follow the operator's Cessna 172S checklist and understand the reason for each major item.

[[highlight:
Checklist Discipline |
The goal is not to prove how much you can remember.

Use the checklist to verify that important items have not been missed. As familiarity increases, flows may improve efficiency—but the checklist remains the verification tool.
]]

#### Cabin and Initial Condition

Before walking around the airplane, assess applicable cabin items and establish that the airplane is configured safely for inspection according to the checklist.

The learner should begin recognizing:

- Required documents and checklist location.
- Control security devices.
- Switch positions.
- Fuel selector position as applicable.
- Loose or unsecured objects.
- General cabin condition.

#### Exterior Inspection

Use a consistent path around the airplane.

The learner should understand the purpose of examining:

- Control surfaces and hinges.
- Wings and empennage.
- Fuel quantity and fuel quality.
- Fuel caps and drains.
- Oil quantity and evidence of leakage.
- Tires and landing gear.
- Brakes.
- Engine cowling.
- Propeller and spinner.
- Pitot/static openings as applicable.
- Air inlets and openings.
- Antennas and lights.
- General structural condition.

#### Fuel and Oil

The learner should physically observe how fuel quantity and fuel quality are evaluated in the training airplane and how oil quantity is checked.

Fuel-system design and operating details are developed in **Lesson 13 — Aircraft Systems, Instruments and Powerplant**. Lesson 1 establishes the habit of **verifying rather than assuming**.

#### Engine and Propeller

Inspect the visible engine-compartment area and propeller in accordance with the checklist and instructor guidance.

### 5. Flight Deck Familiarization

#### Seating and Restraints

Before engine start, the learner should be able to adjust the seat, confirm positive seat engagement, use the restraint system, and establish a position that permits full control movement and a consistent outside sight picture.

#### Controls and Switches

Identify the controls needed during this lesson, including as applicable:

- Control wheel.
- Rudder pedals and brakes.
- Throttle.
- Mixture control.
- Fuel selector.
- Flap control and indication.
- Elevator trim.
- Ignition/start control.
- Master/electrical controls.
- Exterior-light controls.
- Cabin heat and ventilation controls.
- Circuit-breaker location.
- Avionics controls needed for the flight.

Detailed systems knowledge is intentionally deferred to later lessons.

#### Flight Instruments

The learner should identify the primary instruments or displays used to observe:

- Airspeed.
- Attitude.
- Altitude.
- Heading.
- Vertical movement.
- Engine indications.

The first flight should not become an instrument-identification exercise. The learner needs enough familiarity to understand instructor references while keeping primary attention outside.

#### Loose Objects and Cockpit Organization

Secure loose objects before flight. Organize charts, devices, checklists, headsets, and other equipment so they can be used without obstructing controls or creating unnecessary distraction.

#### Positive Exchange of Flight Controls

Use the standard three-step exchange established in Lesson 0:

1. **"You have the flight controls."**
2. **"I have the flight controls."**
3. **"You have the flight controls."**

Visual confirmation accompanies the verbal exchange.

Engine start is performed using the applicable aircraft checklist and POH procedure.

#### Before Starting

Verify, as applicable:

### 8. Basic Flight Control

#### The Four Fundamentals

Flying an airplane starts with four foundational maneuvers:

1. **Straight-and-level flight**
2. **Turns**
3. **Climbs**
4. **Descents**

Nearly every maneuver learned later is a variation, refinement, or combination of these four fundamentals.

The FAA identifies recurring errors for these maneuvers in the *Airplane Flying Handbook*. Those errors are incorporated directly into the **Skills** cards so the instructor can teach the maneuver, observe the likely errors, and correct them while the learner is still developing the basic control habit.

[[highlight:
Key Point |
Do not think of straight-and-level flight, turns, climbs, and descents as four maneuvers you learn once and leave behind.

They are the **basic building blocks of aircraft control**. Traffic patterns, approaches, navigation, emergencies, instrument flight, and advanced maneuvers all depend on these same fundamentals.
]]

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=62 | end=82]]

#### Effect and Use of the Flight Controls

The learner should connect control input to airplane response rather than memorize isolated definitions.

- **Ailerons** primarily control roll.
- **Elevator** primarily controls pitch.
- **Rudder** primarily controls yaw and supports coordination.
- **Power** affects airplane performance and interacts with pitch and trim.

The controls are not used independently in normal flight. A change in one condition often requires coordinated changes in other controls.

#### Attitude Flying

Attitude flying means controlling the airplane primarily by establishing and maintaining a desired relationship between the airplane and the natural horizon.

The learner should begin recognizing:

- Pitch attitude from the position of the nose relative to the horizon.
- Bank attitude from the relationship of the wings to the horizon.
- Changes in performance that follow changes in attitude and power.
- The need to make small corrections rather than continuously chase indications.

[[highlight:
Look Outside |
During visual flight, the airplane is primarily controlled by reference to the **natural horizon and outside visual picture**.

Instruments confirm and refine what you see. Avoid becoming fixated inside the cockpit.
]]

#### Integrated Flight Instruction

The learner should be introduced to both outside visual references and appropriate instrument cross-check from the beginning.

The outside picture remains primary during VFR maneuvering, while the instruments are used to confirm performance and help the learner understand the relationship between sight picture and aircraft indications.

The objective is not to create instrument fixation. It is to build a correct connection between **what the airplane looks and feels like** and **what the instruments report**.

#### The Lindbergh Reference

The FAA's foundational concept is **attitude flying using outside visual references**. The Lindbergh Reference is a practical instructor technique for applying that concept when the airplane's nose blocks or reduces the forward view.

The learner should begin developing more than one useful outside reference.

From the normal seated position, the learner can look toward the left-front or side portion of the windshield and observe the relationship between the airplane and the natural horizon or other external references.

That relationship can help the learner recognize changes in:

- Pitch attitude.
- Bank.
- Yaw or directional movement.
- Drift.
- Later in training, height and alignment cues during landing.

The exact useful sight picture depends on the airplane, seat position, eye position, and maneuver. The goal is not to memorize one universal point on the windshield. The goal is to learn **where useful visual information exists when the forward view becomes limited**.

[[highlight:
Visual Flying |
The Lindbergh Reference is a **teaching technique**, not an FAA-required term or maneuver.

Use it to reinforce the FAA concepts of **attitude flying, outside visual references, and aircraft feel**.

Learn the picture rather than staring at an instrument.
]]

#### Small, Smooth Inputs

New learners often move the controls farther than necessary, see a response, then make an equally large correction in the opposite direction.

Use this sequence:

1. Make a small input.
2. Allow the airplane to respond.
3. Observe the result.
4. Make a smaller correction if needed.

[[highlight:
Control Technique |
Use small, smooth control inputs.

Do not chase every small deviation. Establish the desired attitude, allow the airplane to respond, and correct deliberately.
]]

#### Attitude, Power, Performance, and Trim

[[mnemonic:
ATTITUDE + POWER = PERFORMANCE |
Set the airplane's attitude and power, observe the resulting performance, then make small corrections.
]]

This relationship is introduced in Lesson 1 and refined throughout the course.

Pitch and power establish the general flight condition. The pilot then observes the resulting airspeed, altitude trend, vertical movement, and outside picture and makes deliberate corrections.

Trim is used only after the desired attitude has been established.

[[mnemonic:
CONTROL → HOLD → TRIM |
Use the primary flight control to establish the desired attitude, hold the required pressure, then trim to relieve that pressure.
]]

#### Straight-and-Level Flight

Straight-and-level flight is the foundation from which most other maneuvering begins and ends.

The learner should begin to recognize and control:

- A stable outside pitch picture.
- Wings-level attitude using the horizon.
- Heading trend.
- Altitude trend.
- Coordination.
- Appropriate power.
- Trim.

**Instructor demonstration:** Establish straight-and-level flight and point out the outside sight picture before directing attention briefly to supporting instruments.

**Learner application:** Maintain approximately straight-and-level flight using the horizon as the primary reference and make small corrections as needed.

**Initial emphasis:** Smoothness, outside reference, and recognition of trends are more important than tight numerical tolerances.

#### Level Turns

A level turn combines roll, pitch, yaw control, coordination, and visual awareness.

Introduce:

- Clearing the area before turning.
- Selecting a reference or desired heading.
- Smooth coordinated roll into bank.
- Maintaining the desired pitch attitude as bank is established.
- Stabilizing the bank angle.
- Maintaining coordination.
- Anticipating rollout before the desired heading.
- Smooth coordinated rollout.

The learner should understand that establishing bank changes the direction of the lift vector and that pitch/control pressure may need adjustment to maintain altitude as the turn develops. Detailed aerodynamic treatment comes later; Lesson 1 focuses on the observable control relationship.

**Instructor demonstration:** Demonstrate shallow-to-moderate level turns in both directions using outside references.

**Learner application:** Practice smooth roll-in, stable bank, and rollout with coaching.

#### Climbs and Climbing Turns

A climb requires the learner to coordinate attitude, power, airspeed, directional control, and trim.

Introduce the sequence appropriate to the Cessna 172S and training conditions, emphasizing:

- Establishing the appropriate climb attitude.
- Using the applicable power setting/procedure.
- Recognizing the new outside sight picture.
- Confirming airspeed and climb performance.
- Maintaining coordination and directional control.
- Trimming after the climb attitude is established.
- Beginning the level-off before the desired altitude so the airplane can transition smoothly to level flight.

Climbing turns may be demonstrated or introduced if learner workload permits, but are not required to complete Lesson 1.

#### Descents and Descending Turns

A descent requires coordinated management of power, attitude, airspeed, direction, and trim.

Introduce:

- Selecting an appropriate power setting or descent configuration.
- Establishing the desired descent attitude.
- Recognizing the outside sight picture.
- Confirming airspeed and descent performance.
- Maintaining coordination.
- Trimming after the condition is established.
- Beginning the level-off early enough for a smooth transition back to level flight.

Descending turns may be introduced if learner workload permits, but are not required to complete Lesson 1.

#### Glides — Awareness Only

The FAA Basic Flight Maneuvers chapter also introduces glides. In Lesson 1, the learner should understand only the basic idea that a glide is a descent with little or no engine power in which pitch is used to control the appropriate airspeed.

Detailed glide performance, best-glide procedures, emergency descent planning, and power-off approach application are developed later in the syllabus.

## Risk Management

### Preflight Distraction and Interruption

A preflight can be interrupted by conversation, fueling activity, passengers, phones, other aircraft, or a desire to hurry.

#### Mitigation

- Use a consistent inspection flow.
- Use the checklist.
- If interrupted, deliberately establish where the inspection should resume.
- Repeat an item or section when uncertain whether it was completed.


### Incomplete or Ritualized Inspection

A pilot can physically walk around the airplane without meaningfully inspecting it.

#### Mitigation

For each major item, know what condition is being assessed and what would cause the inspection to stop for further evaluation.


### Fuel Quantity and Quality

Assuming fuel status rather than verifying it creates unnecessary risk.

#### Mitigation

- Verify fuel quantity using the operator's approved method.
- Sample fuel as required by the checklist and operating procedure.
- Evaluate the sample rather than merely draining it.
- Resolve contamination or uncertainty before flight.


### Propeller Hazard

The propeller area is hazardous before, during, and after engine operation.

#### Mitigation

- Treat the propeller as capable of moving.
- Keep people and equipment clear.
- Clear the area before start.
- Avoid unnecessary movement near the propeller arc.


### Loose Objects and Flight Deck Distraction

An unsecured object can interfere with controls or cause distraction at a high-workload moment.

#### Mitigation

Secure the cockpit before engine start and maintain an organized flight deck.


### Checklist Omission

Familiarity can create confidence that a checklist is no longer necessary.

#### Mitigation

Use the checklist as a verification tool and resist rushing because a procedure feels routine.


### Excessive Taxi Speed

High taxi speed reduces time available to recognize and correct errors and increases braking demand.

#### Mitigation

Use smooth power, maintain a conservative speed, and keep primary attention outside.


### Ambiguous Control Transfer

Two pilots believing the other is flying—or both pilots attempting to fly—can immediately create an unsafe condition.

#### Mitigation

Use the positive three-step control exchange every time.


### Overcontrol

Large control movements followed by large opposite corrections can create pilot-induced oscillation and increasing workload.

#### Mitigation

Use small inputs, allow time for the airplane to respond, and trim sustained pressure.


### Inside Fixation

A new learner may stare at instruments, avionics, or a single display while losing the outside attitude picture and traffic awareness.

#### Mitigation

Prioritize the horizon and outside environment. Use instruments for short confirmation checks and return attention outside.


## Skills

### Conduct a Supervised Preflight Inspection

#### Purpose

Develop a repeatable method for assessing the airplane's apparent condition before flight.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate a systematic inspection using the aircraft checklist while explaining **what is being assessed and why**.

#### Learner Practice

The learner performs or participates in the inspection and verbalizes the purpose of major items.

#### Common Errors

- Treating the walkaround as a memorized ritual rather than an inspection.
- Skipping an item after an interruption.
- Touching or pointing at an item without actually assessing its condition.
- Assuming fuel quantity or quality instead of verifying it.
- Failing to notice a missing cap, cover, tiedown, chock, control lock, or obvious discrepancy.
- Continuing when uncertain instead of stopping and asking.

#### Completion Standard

The learner follows the checklist and inspection path with instructor guidance, identifies the major items being assessed, and stops when uncertain rather than assuming an item is satisfactory.


### Organize and Configure the Flight Deck

#### Purpose

Create a safe, usable cockpit before engine operation.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate seat adjustment, restraint use, control clearance, checklist placement, headset organization, loose-object control, and identification of lesson-critical controls.

#### Learner Practice

The learner establishes a usable seating position, secures the cockpit, and identifies the controls needed for the lesson.

#### Common Errors

- Failing to verify the seat is positively locked.
- Leaving loose objects unsecured.
- Placing checklists, tablets, or bags where they can interfere with controls.
- Becoming absorbed in avionics setup before the cockpit is physically secure.
- Attempting to manipulate an unfamiliar control without first identifying it.

#### Completion Standard

The learner is secure, can reach the controls, understands where key controls are located, and does not obstruct or interfere with flight-control movement.


### Perform Positive Exchange of Flight Controls

#### Purpose

Ensure that both pilots always know who is flying.

#### Learner Practice

Practice transfers in both directions using the three-step verbal exchange and visual confirmation.

#### Common Errors

- Releasing the controls before the receiving pilot acknowledges the transfer.
- Using vague language such as "your airplane?" without a confirmed response.
- Assuming the instructor has control because the instructor touches the controls.
- Continuing to manipulate the controls after control has been transferred.

#### Completion Standard

The learner performs the exchange correctly without prompting.


### Participate in Engine Start

#### Purpose

Introduce safe checklist-driven engine operation.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate the aircraft-specific procedure using the Cessna 172S checklist and explain propeller-area safety and post-start indication checks.

#### Learner Practice

The learner participates as directed and identifies the major safety considerations.

#### Common Errors

- Beginning the start sequence before the cockpit and outside area are ready.
- Failing to visually clear the propeller area.
- Rushing or skipping checklist items.
- Allowing excessive engine RPM immediately after start.
- Failing to verify expected engine indications after start.
- Ignoring an abnormal indication because the engine is already running.

#### Completion Standard

The learner uses the checklist with instructor guidance, maintains propeller-area awareness, and recognizes that abnormal indications require attention.


### Basic Taxi Control

#### Purpose

Introduce safe ground directional and speed control.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate smooth power use, steering, braking, clearing, and conservative taxi speed.

#### Learner Practice

The learner taxis in an appropriate low-workload area with close instructor supervision.

#### Common Errors

- Taxiing faster than conditions require.
- Using excessive power and then excessive braking.
- Riding the brakes continuously.
- Looking inside instead of along the taxi path.
- Steering with the control wheel instead of understanding pedal/nosewheel control.
- Allowing the airplane to move before the path is clear.

#### Completion Standard

The learner begins maintaining directional control and safe speed without abrupt power or brake application. Full ACS taxi proficiency is not expected in this lesson.

Introduce a deliberate readiness check before departure.

Use the aircraft checklist and explain the purpose of major categories rather than encouraging rote memorization.

The learner follows the checklist and identifies the purpose of major checks with assistance.

#### Common Errors

- Rushing because another airplane is waiting.
- Performing the check from memory without verification.
- Moving controls mechanically without verifying correct movement and response.
- Continuing toward takeoff with an unresolved question or abnormal indication.

#### Completion Standard

The learner understands that takeoff does not begin until the airplane and crew are appropriately configured and ready.


### Straight-and-Level Flight

#### Purpose

Develop the first stable outside visual reference and basic feel for the controls.

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=67 | end=70]]

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate the outside pitch picture, wings-level reference, small corrections, instrument confirmation, and trim.

#### Learner Practice

The learner maintains approximately straight-and-level flight using outside references with instructor coaching.

#### Common Errors

FAA-highlighted common errors include:

1. Using inconsistent or inappropriate pitch-and-bank reference points from flight to flight.
2. Forgetting the location of previously selected outside reference points.
3. Attempting to establish or correct airplane attitude using flight instruments rather than the natural horizon.
4. **Chasing the flight instruments** instead of adhering to attitude-flying principles.
5. Mechanically pushing or pulling on the flight controls rather than applying accurate, smooth pressure.
6. Failing to scan outside for traffic, weather, and terrain.
7. Maintaining a tight palm grip on the controls, reducing feel and sensitivity.
8. Overcontrolling the airplane.
9. Habitually flying with one wing low or attempting to maintain directional control with rudder alone.
10. Failing to make timely, measured control inputs after a deviation from straight-and-level flight.
11. Giving inadequate attention to sensory cues and airplane reference points used to develop aircraft feel.

#### Completion Standard

The learner demonstrates basic control awareness, responds to coaching, and avoids persistent large or abrupt control inputs. Practical-test precision is not expected.


### Level Turns

#### Purpose

Introduce coordinated changes in heading and bank.

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=71 | end=78]]

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate clearing, smooth roll-in, stable bank, outside pitch reference, coordination, and smooth rollout.

#### Learner Practice

The learner makes shallow-to-moderate banked turns in both directions with instructor coaching.

#### Common Errors

FAA-highlighted common errors include:

1. Failing to adequately clear in the direction of turn for aircraft traffic.
2. Gaining or losing altitude during the turn.
3. Failing to hold the desired bank angle constant.
4. Attempting to execute the turn solely by instrument reference.
5. Leaning away from the direction of the turn while seated.
6. Failing to sense slips or skids without relying on the flight instruments.
7. Attempting to maintain a constant bank angle by referencing only the airplane's nose.
8. Making flat, skidding turns to avoid banking the airplane.
9. Holding excessive rudder in the direction of turn.
10. Developing proficiency in turns in only one direction.
11. Failing to coordinate the controls.

#### Completion Standard

The learner demonstrates the correct control relationship and begins recognizing bank and pitch visually.


### Climbs

#### Purpose

Introduce the relationship between pitch, power, airspeed, outside references, and trim during a climb.

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=78 | end=81]]

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate the aircraft-specific setup and the resulting outside picture.

#### Learner Practice

The learner establishes and maintains a basic climb with assistance.

#### Common Errors

FAA-highlighted common errors include:

1. Attempting to establish climb pitch attitude primarily by referencing the airspeed indicator and **chasing the airspeed**.
2. Applying elevator pressure too aggressively, resulting in an excessive climb angle.
3. Using inadequate or inappropriate rudder pressure during climbing turns.
4. Allowing the airplane to yaw during climbs, often because of inadequate right-rudder pressure.
5. Fixating on the airplane's nose during straight climbs and allowing one wing to remain low.
6. Initiating a climbing turn without coordinated use of the flight controls, resulting in little or no turn and a climb with one wing low.
7. Using improper coordination and slipping in a manner that reduces climb performance.
8. Failing to maintain constant pitch and bank attitude during climbing turns.
9. Attempting to exceed the airplane's climb capability.
10. Using excessive forward elevator pressure during level-off, resulting in altitude loss or excessive low-G loading.

#### Completion Standard

The learner uses smooth inputs, maintains awareness of the outside picture, and begins using trim appropriately.


### Descents

#### Purpose

Introduce the relationship between power, pitch, airspeed, outside references, and trim during descent.

[[pdf: /knowledge/Airplane_Flying_Handbook.pdf | start=81 | end=82]]

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate the aircraft-specific setup and visual picture.

#### Learner Practice

The learner establishes and maintains a basic descent with assistance.

#### Common Errors

FAA-highlighted common errors include:

1. Failing to adequately clear for aircraft traffic in the direction of turn or descent.
2. Using inadequate elevator back pressure during glide entry, resulting in an overly steep glide.
3. Failing to slow the airplane to approximately glide speed before lowering the pitch attitude when establishing a glide.
4. Attempting to establish or maintain a normal glide solely by reference to flight instruments.
5. Failing to sense changes in airspeed through sound and aircraft feel.
6. Failing to stabilize the glide and instead **chasing the airspeed indicator**.
7. Attempting to **stretch the glide** by applying excessive back-elevator pressure.
8. Skidding or slipping during gliding turns and failing to recognize the difference in rudder forces with and without power.
9. Failing to lower pitch attitude during gliding-turn entry, resulting in decreasing airspeed.
10. Using excessive rudder pressure during recovery from gliding turns.
11. Using inadequate pitch control during recovery from a straight glide.
12. Cross-controlling during gliding turns near the ground.
13. Failing to maintain a constant bank angle during gliding turns.

For Lesson 1, emphasize the errors applicable to the basic descent actually being practiced. The more glide-specific items are **awareness items** and are developed further during emergency and power-off training.

#### Completion Standard

The learner uses smooth inputs, maintains outside awareness, and begins using trim appropriately.


### Trim the Airplane

#### Purpose

Reduce sustained control pressure and workload.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Establish the desired attitude, hold the necessary pressure, and then trim the pressure away.

#### Learner Practice

The learner repeats the sequence in straight-and-level flight and, as appropriate, during climbs and descents.

#### Common Errors

- Attempting to fly the airplane primarily with trim.
- Trimming before the desired attitude is established.
- Making large trim changes and then chasing the resulting pitch change.
- Continuing to hold unnecessary control pressure after trimming.

#### Completion Standard

The learner does not attempt to use trim as the primary pitch control and can explain `CONTROL → HOLD → TRIM`.


### Shutdown, Secure and Postflight

#### Purpose

Establish that pilot responsibility continues after landing.

#### Instructor Demonstration

Demonstrate after-landing, parking, shutdown, securing, and discrepancy-reporting procedures using the checklist.

#### Learner Practice

The learner participates in the checklist and securing process.

#### Common Errors

- Treating landing as the end of the flight and mentally disengaging.
- Rushing the shutdown checklist.
- Leaving switches, controls, or equipment improperly configured.
- Failing to secure the airplane against movement or weather as applicable.
- Failing to report an unusual indication or discrepancy noticed during the flight.

#### Completion Standard

The learner understands the procedural flow and recognizes the need to report discrepancies or abnormal observations.

## Basic Air Work

### The Four Fundamentals

This flight introduces the four basic maneuver families that form the foundation for later training:

- Straight-and-level flight.
- Turns.
- Climbs.
- Descents.

The goal is **recognition and basic control**, not practical-test precision.

### Outside Reference Exercise — Lindbergh Reference

#### Procedure

1. Establish a stable flight condition.
2. Identify the normal forward horizon reference.
3. Shift attention toward the left-front or side windshield.
4. Identify a repeatable relationship between the airplane and the horizon or another distant outside reference.
5. Make a very small pitch change and observe how the side reference changes.
6. Return to the original attitude.
7. Make a very small bank change and observe how the side reference changes.
8. If appropriate, make a small coordinated yaw/directional change and observe the visual effect.
9. Return to the normal outside scan.

#### Common Error

Fixating on a single reference point instead of maintaining an outside scan and using the reference as one additional attitude cue.

#### Objective

Build recognition of pitch, roll, and yaw through outside visual cues. Precision is not the goal.

[[highlight:
Instructor Emphasis |
Use the Lindbergh Reference as an additional outside cue, not as the learner's only visual reference.

The learner should continue scanning outside and should not become fixated on one point along the windshield.
]]

### Straight-and-Level Flight

#### Procedure

1. Look outside and establish the desired pitch attitude using the natural horizon.
2. Establish a wings-level attitude using outside references.
3. Allow the airplane to stabilize.
4. Confirm heading, altitude trend, and coordination with the instruments.
5. Make small, measured corrections.
6. When the desired condition is established, **CONTROL → HOLD → TRIM**.

#### Common Error

FAA-highlighted common errors include:

1. Using inconsistent or inappropriate pitch-and-bank reference points from flight to flight.
2. Forgetting the location of previously selected outside reference points.
3. Attempting to establish or correct airplane attitude using flight instruments rather than the natural horizon.
4. **Chasing the flight instruments** instead of adhering to attitude-flying principles.
5. Mechanically pushing or pulling on the flight controls rather than applying accurate, smooth pressure.
6. Failing to scan outside for traffic, weather, and terrain.
7. Maintaining a tight palm grip on the controls, reducing feel and sensitivity.
8. Overcontrolling the airplane.
9. Habitually flying with one wing low or attempting to maintain directional control with rudder alone.
10. Failing to make timely, measured control inputs after a deviation from straight-and-level flight.
11. Giving inadequate attention to sensory cues and airplane reference points used to develop aircraft feel.

#### Instructor Demonstration / Learner Practice

Emphasize:

- Natural horizon / outside pitch picture.
- Wings-level visual reference.
- Light grip and small corrections.
- Instrument confirmation rather than fixation.
- Coordination.
- Trim after the desired condition is established.

### Level Turns

#### Procedure

1. Clear in the direction of the turn.
2. Select the desired outside reference or heading.
3. Smoothly coordinate aileron and rudder to establish the desired bank.
4. Maintain the outside pitch picture and apply the pressure needed to hold altitude.
5. Stabilize the bank and maintain coordination.
6. Anticipate the rollout.
7. Smoothly coordinate aileron and rudder back to wings level.
8. Reestablish straight-and-level flight.

#### Common Error

FAA-highlighted common errors include:

1. Failing to adequately clear in the direction of turn for aircraft traffic.
2. Gaining or losing altitude during the turn.
3. Failing to hold the desired bank angle constant.
4. Attempting to execute the turn solely by instrument reference.
5. Leaning away from the direction of the turn while seated.
6. Failing to sense slips or skids without relying on the flight instruments.
7. Attempting to maintain a constant bank angle by referencing only the airplane's nose.
8. Making flat, skidding turns to avoid banking the airplane.
9. Holding excessive rudder in the direction of turn.
10. Developing proficiency in turns in only one direction.
11. Failing to coordinate the controls.

Emphasize:

- Clear before turning.
- Smooth coordinated roll-in.
- Maintain the outside pitch picture.
- Stable shallow-to-moderate bank.
- Smooth rollout.

### Climbs

#### Procedure

1. Clear the area and establish the aircraft-specific climb procedure using the POH/checklist.
2. Establish the appropriate power and climb attitude.
3. Maintain directional control and coordination.
4. Use the outside pitch picture first, then confirm airspeed and climb performance.
5. When the climb attitude is established, trim to relieve control pressure.
6. Anticipate the target altitude and begin a smooth level-off.
7. Establish the appropriate level-flight attitude and power, allow the airplane to accelerate as appropriate, then retrim.

#### Common Error

FAA-highlighted common errors include:

1. Attempting to establish climb pitch attitude primarily by referencing the airspeed indicator and **chasing the airspeed**.
2. Applying elevator pressure too aggressively, resulting in an excessive climb angle.
3. Using inadequate or inappropriate rudder pressure during climbing turns.
4. Allowing the airplane to yaw during climbs, often because of inadequate right-rudder pressure.
5. Fixating on the airplane's nose during straight climbs and allowing one wing to remain low.
6. Initiating a climbing turn without coordinated use of the flight controls, resulting in little or no turn and a climb with one wing low.
7. Using improper coordination and slipping in a manner that reduces climb performance.
8. Failing to maintain constant pitch and bank attitude during climbing turns.
9. Attempting to exceed the airplane's climb capability.
10. Using excessive forward elevator pressure during level-off, resulting in altitude loss or excessive low-G loading.

Emphasize:

- Aircraft-specific procedure and instructor demonstration.
- Attitude and power.
- Outside picture first.
- Airspeed/performance confirmation.
- Coordination.
- Trim after the climb attitude is established.
- Anticipate and smoothly complete the level-off.

### Descents

#### Procedure

1. Clear the area and select the aircraft-specific descent procedure or configuration.
2. Establish the appropriate power setting.
3. Establish the desired descent attitude using outside references.
4. Maintain directional control and coordination.
5. Confirm airspeed and descent performance without chasing the instruments.
6. Trim after the descent condition is stabilized.
7. Anticipate the target altitude and begin a smooth level-off.
8. Reestablish the appropriate level-flight attitude and power, then retrim.

#### Common Error

FAA-highlighted common errors include:

1. Failing to adequately clear for aircraft traffic in the direction of turn or descent.
2. Using inadequate elevator back pressure during glide entry, resulting in an overly steep glide.
3. Failing to slow the airplane to approximately glide speed before lowering the pitch attitude when establishing a glide.
4. Attempting to establish or maintain a normal glide solely by reference to flight instruments.
5. Failing to sense changes in airspeed through sound and aircraft feel.
6. Failing to stabilize the glide and instead **chasing the airspeed indicator**.
7. Attempting to **stretch the glide** by applying excessive back-elevator pressure.
8. Skidding or slipping during gliding turns and failing to recognize the difference in rudder forces with and without power.
9. Failing to lower pitch attitude during gliding-turn entry, resulting in decreasing airspeed.
10. Using excessive rudder pressure during recovery from gliding turns.
11. Using inadequate pitch control during recovery from a straight glide.
12. Cross-controlling during gliding turns near the ground.
13. Failing to maintain a constant bank angle during gliding turns.

For Lesson 1, emphasize the errors applicable to the basic descent actually being practiced. The more glide-specific items are awareness items and are developed further during emergency and power-off training.

Emphasize:

- Aircraft-specific procedure and instructor demonstration.
- Power and attitude relationship.
- Outside picture first.
- Airspeed/performance confirmation.
- Coordination.
- Trim after the descent condition is established.
- Anticipate and smoothly complete the level-off.

### Trim

#### Procedure

1. Use the primary flight controls to establish the desired attitude.
2. Hold the required control pressure.
3. Adjust trim until the control pressure is relieved.
4. Verify that the airplane remains in the desired condition.
5. Make small corrections with the primary controls first, then retrim if necessary.

#### Common Error

Attempting to fly the airplane with trim or using trim to force the airplane into an attitude instead of using trim only to relieve established control pressure.

[[mnemonic:
CONTROL → HOLD → TRIM |
Establish the attitude with the flight controls, hold the required pressure, then trim the pressure away.
]]

[[mnemonic:
ATTITUDE + POWER = PERFORMANCE |
Set attitude and power, observe the result, then make small corrections.
]]

[[highlight:
Instructor Emphasis |
Smoothness before precision. Outside visual reference before instrument fixation. Give the learner enough time to observe what each input actually does to the airplane.
]]
